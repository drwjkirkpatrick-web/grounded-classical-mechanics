# Lesson 001: What Is Physics? Motion, Forces and Energy Around Us

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define physics as the study of matter, motion, forces and energy.
2. Identify at least five examples of physics at work in the school compound.
3. Name the quantity being demonstrated in each example (force, motion or energy).
4. State one reason physics skills matter for Kenyan work and daily life.

## Materials
- Exercise book and pen per learner (the only equipment today — this lesson is a thinking walk)
- Chalkboard or chart for the class list
- The school compound itself: gate, water tank, flagpole, playground, nearby road (observe from a safe distance)

## Vocabulary
- **physics** (fizikia) — the study of matter, motion, forces and energy
- **matter** (materia) — anything that has mass and takes up space
- **force** (nguvu) — a push or a pull
- **energy** (nishati) — the ability to do work or cause change
- **motion** (mwendo) — change of position with time

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. In your own words, what do you think physics studies?
2. Name one thing in the school compound that involves a force.
3. A boda boda speeds up along the road. Is energy involved? Say yes or no and give a reason.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept any honest attempt, e.g. "the study of how things move / why things happen."
2. Accept any: gate hinge (push), water tank (weight/pressure), swing (pull of gravity), ball games.
3. Yes — speeding up means its motion energy (kinetic energy) is increasing; the engine supplies it.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test questions; learners write individually; collect or mark later against the post-test.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a stone and let it drop. Ask: *what just happened, and why?* Take three answers without judging. Then announce: this year we learn to answer such questions properly — that study is called physics.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — the physics walk. Move the class around the compound in an orderly line. At each station, learners note the object and the physics: the gate hinge (force and turning), the raised water tank (stored energy, pressure in pipes), the flagpole rope (pulling force), a seesaw or swing (forces balancing), boda bodas passing (motion and energy). Each learner must record at least five examples in the exercise book with one word of physics beside each.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board, build the definition from their examples: physics studies matter (what things are made of), motion (how they move), forces (pushes and pulls) and energy (the ability to cause change). Sort the class list into the four headings. Confront the misconception head-on: physics is not only for laboratories — the jua kali workshop, the matatu stage and the shamba are physics laboratories.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *physics is everywhere; this course uses the objects around us.* Homework: write down two examples of physics at home and bring them tomorrow.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Define physics in one sentence. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Name one thing in the school compound that involves a force, and say what the force does. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Sort these into *matter*, *motion*, *force* or *energy*: (a) a flying football, (b) a jerrican of water, (c) a jembe pushing soil, (d) a charged solar lamp lighting a room.
4. Why is a raised water tank an example of stored energy? Explain in one or two sentences.
5. Challenge: a trader says "physics is only for engineers." Give one argument from market life to show she already uses physics.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Physics is the study of matter, motion, forces and energy.
2. Accept any with a correct role, e.g. gate hinge — a push makes the gate turn; tank — gravity pulls the water down the pipe.
3. (a) motion; (b) matter (accept energy if argued as weight — direct them to matter); (c) force; (d) energy.
4. The water is held high; gravity can pull it down through pipes, so its position stores energy that is released when the tap opens.
5. Accept: weighing produce, levering loads onto a mkokoteni, judging how high to stack crates, knowing a full debe is heavier than an empty one.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** List three physics careers in Kenya and the physics each uses daily.
   **Worked Answer:** (1) Jua kali fabricator — forces, moments and material strength when cutting, bending and welding metal; must judge load paths so a gate or trailer does not sag. (2) Civil engineer — forces in equilibrium, pressure and materials for bridges, roads and water systems; every structure must balance loads safely. (3) Radiographer — energy of X-rays, their absorption by tissue, and shielding; uses physics of radiation to image bones safely. Accept also: solar technician, electrician, aircraft mechanic.
2. **Q:** A 1000-litre tank sits 4 m above a tap. The water in it has mass 1000 kg (1 L ≈ 1 kg). Taking g = 10 N/kg, what is the weight of the stored water, and why does raising the tank help water flow?
   **Worked Answer:** Weight = mg = 1000 kg × 10 N/kg = 10 000 N. Raised water has gravitational potential energy (mgh = 1000 × 10 × 4 = 40 000 J); as it falls, that energy becomes the pressure that pushes water out of the tap — no pump needed.
3. **Q:** During the walk, a learner says the stationary flagpole "has no physics because nothing is moving." Correct this with physics language.
   **Worked Answer:** The pole is matter with mass; gravity pulls it down (its weight) while the ground pushes up an equal force — the forces are balanced, which is why it does not move. Balanced forces and equilibrium are physics just as much as motion is.

## Safety Notes
- The walk passes near roads: set a boundary line, post a responsible learner or teacher as lookout, and stop the group before discussing traffic examples.
- Do not climb on the water tank stand, gate or flagpole to "demonstrate" — observe from the ground.
- Hot season: carry water, keep the walk in shade where possible, and watch for signs of overheating.
- Stone-drop engage demo: drop at arm's length over bare ground, away from feet and learners' toes.

## Teacher Notes
- No prior knowledge assumed — this is lesson 1 of 365; establish the routine (pre-test, activity, post-test) that repeats daily.
- Ages 10–13: focus on spotting and naming examples. Ages 14–17: push for the four-heading classification and the advanced questions.
- This lesson seeds Unit 1: measurement only makes sense once learners agree physics describes real things — revisit today's list when introducing each measuring tool.
- Local tips: if the school has no tank, use a neighbour's rooftop tank or a water kiosk visible from the gate; boda examples work everywhere.
- CBE learner record: note each learner's pre/post growth and one oral contribution to the compound walk as evidence for scientific inquiry competencies.
