# Lesson 003: The Scientific Investigation: Ask, Predict, Test, Record

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. List the steps of a scientific investigation: ask, predict, test, record, conclude.
2. Write a testable hypothesis about a swinging pendulum.
3. Record results honestly, even when they contradict the prediction.
4. Time swings using counting and a simple watch or phone.

## Materials
- One fist-sized stone per group (smooth, no sharp edges)
- Sisal string, about 1 m per group
- A tree branch, doorway lintel or desk edge to hang the pendulum from
- Phone stopwatch or clock with a seconds hand (one per class is enough)
- Exercise books for recording tables

## Vocabulary
- **investigation** (uchunguzi) — a planned test that answers a question
- **hypothesis** (nadharia) — a testable statement you believe is true before testing
- **prediction** (tabiri) — what you expect to observe in a specific test
- **evidence** (ushahidi) — the recorded results, whether or not they agree with you
- **pendulum** — a mass swinging on a string (no common Kiswahili word; use as is)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. A heavy stone and a light stone swing on equal strings. Predict: which completes one swing faster?
2. What should you do if your test result disagrees with what you predicted?
3. Put these in order: record, ask a question, test, predict.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept any prediction with a reason — this is exactly what the lesson tests (the honest answer: mass does not change the time).
2. Record it honestly and rethink the explanation — never change the result to fit the guess.
3. Ask a question → predict → test → record.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — administer; keep the papers — question 1 returns at the end.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hang the stone pendulum from the lintel and start it swinging. Ask: *what could make this swing faster or slower?* Board every idea: mass, string length, push strength, swing width.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — the investigation. Question on the board: *does a heavier pendulum swing faster?* Each group writes a hypothesis ("Heavier stones swing faster" or the reverse), then tests it: same string length, same release point, time 10 swings with a heavy stone, then a lighter one. Record both times in a table. Teacher circulates asking: *are you changing only the mass? What else must stay the same?*
4. **Explain (8 min)** — collect group results on the board: within timing error, the times match. Mass does not set the swing time (length does — that is next). Walk the steps: ask → hypothesis → predict → test → record → conclude. Confront the misconception head-on: a wrong hypothesis is not a failed investigation — honest evidence that disproves a guess is exactly how science moves.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark where time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *honest records beat clever guesses.* Homework: write a hypothesis about whether a longer string swings slower, to be tested in a later lesson.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. A heavy stone and a light stone swing on equal-length strings released the same way. Which completes 10 swings faster? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Your result disagrees with your prediction. What do you do? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Write the five steps of an investigation in order.
4. Write one hypothesis about pendulums that we did *not* test today.
5. Challenge: why do we time 10 swings and divide by 10 instead of timing one swing?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Neither — they take about the same time; mass does not set the swing time at equal length.
2. Record the result honestly and revise the hypothesis/explanation; do not hide or alter the data.
3. Ask a question → write a hypothesis → make a prediction → test (experiment) → record and conclude.
4. Accept e.g. "Longer strings swing slower", "A bigger push makes it swing faster", "A wider swing takes longer."
5. Reaction time error (about 0.2 s at start and stop) is spread over 10 swings, so the error per swing is ~10 times smaller.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Distinguish a hypothesis from a prediction, and write both for the swing experiment.
   **Worked Answer:** A hypothesis is a general, testable claim about how the world works; a prediction is the specific observation you expect in one test if the hypothesis is true. Hypothesis: "The mass of a pendulum does not affect its swing time." Prediction: "A 200 g stone and a 500 g stone on 1 m strings will each take about the same time for 10 swings." The test confirms or rejects the prediction, which supports or weakens the hypothesis.
2. **Q:** A group times 10 swings at 14.2 s with the heavy stone and 13.8 s with the light one, then reports "the heavy stone is slower." Criticise this conclusion using timing-error reasoning.
   **Worked Answer:** Time per swing: 1.42 s vs 1.38 s — a difference of 0.04 s, smaller than human reaction time (~0.2 s) on the stopwatch. The difference is inside measurement error, so the honest conclusion is "no difference detected." To settle it they would need more swings per trial and several repeated trials, not a stronger opinion.
3. **Q:** Why is "the experiment failed" a scientifically wrong thing to say when the result contradicts the hypothesis?
   **Worked Answer:** The experiment's job is to produce evidence, not to please the hypothesis. A clean test that disproves a claim has succeeded — it has taught us something true (mass does not matter here). It fails only if the method was sloppy (changed two variables, miscounted swings), because then the evidence cannot be trusted either way.

## Safety Notes
- Stones swing at face height: hang pendulums away from walkways, release gently (no throwing), and keep bystanders an arm's length clear.
- Check knots before every trial; a flying stone is the main injury risk — retie any frayed sisal.
- No standing on desks to reach lintels; use a doorway at shoulder height or a low branch.
- Keep stones off desk edges when not swinging — a rolling stone lands on toes.
