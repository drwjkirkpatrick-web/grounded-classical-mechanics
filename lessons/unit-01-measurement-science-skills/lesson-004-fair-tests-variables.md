# Lesson 004: Fair Tests and Variables

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define independent, dependent and controlled variables.
2. Design a fair test by changing one thing and keeping the rest the same.
3. Run a drop test (stone, rubber ball, paper ball) and identify which variable decides the fall time.
4. Spot an unfair test and say how to fix it.

## Materials
- A stone (palm-sized), a rubber ball, and a sheet of paper (to crumple into a ball) per group
- Tape measure or marked wall strip, 2–3 m
- A chair or desk to drop from (teacher supervises)
- Exercise books for results tables

## Vocabulary
- **variable** (kigeuzi) — anything that can change in a test
- **independent variable** — the one thing *you* change on purpose
- **dependent variable** — the thing you *measure* as the result
- **controlled variables** — everything you keep the same so the test is fair
- **fair test** (jaribio la haki) — a test where only the independent variable differs

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. You drop a stone and a crumpled paper ball from the same height. Predict which lands first.
2. In that test, what must stay the same for it to be fair? Name one thing.
3. A learner changes the drop height AND the ball in the same test. Can she conclude anything? Why?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept any prediction with a reason (the stone lands first — air resistance slows the paper ball).
2. Accept any one: drop height, release method (no throwing), same timing method, same spot (no wind).
3. No — with two changes she cannot tell which one caused the difference.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — administer; learners commit to their prediction in writing.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a stone and a flat sheet of paper and drop them side by side. The stone wins easily. Crumple the paper into a tight ball and repeat — now it is nearly a tie. Ask: *what did I actually change?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — group drop tests. Test 1 (fair): stone vs rubber ball from the same 2 m mark — time or judge which lands first. Test 2 (fair): flat paper vs the same paper crumpled — same height. Test 3 (deliberately unfair): drop the stone from 2 m and the paper ball from 1 m; ask groups to explain why no conclusion is possible. Groups list for each test: independent variable, dependent variable, and at least two controlled variables.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the three variable types with the day's examples: changing the object (independent), the fall time (dependent), height/release/wind (controlled). Physics: same-height drops fall together unless *air resistance* differs — the paper's shape, not its mass, slowed it. Confront the misconception: "heavy things always fall faster" — the rubber ball and stone landed together; only the flat sheet lost, because of air.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *change one thing, keep the rest, measure the result.* Homework: describe a fair test for "does hotter water dissolve sugar faster?" naming all three variable types.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Stone vs crumpled paper ball, same height, same release: which lands first and why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Name two things that must stay the same in that drop test. (mirrors pre-test)
3. In "does string length change a pendulum's swing time?", name the independent, dependent and one controlled variable.
4. A student tests "do bigger balls bounce higher?" using a big rubber ball and a small stone. Why is this unfair, and how do you fix it?
5. Challenge: flat paper falls slowly but the same paper crumpled falls fast. Which variable explains it?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Nearly together (stone slightly first); both fall at the same rate, but the paper ball still meets a little more air resistance.
2. Accept any two: same height, same release (no push), same timing/judging method, still air.
3. Independent: string length; dependent: swing time (time per swing); controlled: mass of the bob, release angle/point.
4. Two things differ — size AND material/mass — so any bounce difference has two possible causes. Fix: use two balls of the same material that differ only in size (or same size, different material in a separate test).
5. Shape (surface area facing the air) — the mass and material are identical in both trials, so only air resistance changed.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Two balls differ in BOTH mass and size, and the big heavy one lands first. Identify the confounding variable and redesign the test.
   **Worked Answer:** Size is the confounder: the big ball may win because it is heavier, or because its larger size... actually size increases air resistance, so the clean suspect is mass — but with both changed, either could explain the result; the variables are confounded. Redesign: (a) two balls of the same size, different mass (fill one hollow ball with sand), testing mass alone; (b) two balls of the same mass, different size (crumple vs flat paper), testing shape alone. One variable per test.
2. **Q:** A drop from 2.0 m takes about 0.63 s (using t = √(2h/g), g = 10 N/kg). Your group's measured times are 0.9 s, 0.8 s and 1.0 s. What does the spread tell you, and which variable was poorly controlled?
   **Worked Answer:** The predicted 0.63 s is far below the measurements, and the spread (0.8–1.0 s) is large relative to the value — human reaction time on the stopwatch (~0.2 s each way) dominated the measurement. The poorly controlled "variable" is the timing method. Better: drop three objects together and judge landing order by sound, or drop from a greater height so the fall time dwarfs reaction error.
3. **Q:** Design one fair test, runnable in a Kenyan homestead, that isolates air resistance as the independent variable.
   **Worked Answer:** Example: drop two identical sheets of paper, one flat and one crumpled tight, from the roof edge on a still day; repeat 5 times each and record landing order. Independent: shape (air resistance); dependent: fall time/landing order; controlled: same paper (same mass), same height, same release, no wind. Any design with only shape changing and repeats earns full marks.

## Safety Notes
- Dropping from chair height: only one learner drops at a time; others stand back from the landing zone; never drop near feet.
- Stones break tiles and toes: drop onto bare ground or a sand tray, not onto desks or concrete edges where chips can fly.
- Do not lean out of windows or off verandas to gain height — 2 m from a supervised chair is enough.
- Pick up paper scraps after; wind-blown litter is the fastest way to lose outdoor privileges.

## Teacher Notes
- Builds on Lesson 003 (investigation steps); variables are the engine inside the "test" step and return in every practical lesson.
- Ages 10–13: master the chant "change one, keep the rest, measure one." Ages 14–17: add the confounding-variable reasoning from the advanced questions.
- The stone-vs-rubber-ball tie is the key physics seed for free fall (Unit 2) — flag it: at equal size-ish shape, mass does not change fall time.
- Local tips: a beaten old tennis/rubber ball from the market is perfect; the "paper" can be an old exercise-book page. Chalk the height marks on the wall beforehand.
- CBE learner record: assess each group's written variable lists; the unfair-test critique is strong evidence of investigation-design competency.
