# Lesson 005: SI Units and Why Standards Matter

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Name the SI base units for length (metre), mass (kilogram), time (second) and the common unit for volume (litre).
2. Explain why body-based measures (cubit, stride, handful) cause disputes.
3. Describe how standards protect buyers and sellers at the market.
4. Convert between related units (m ↔ cm ↔ mm; L ↔ mL).

## Materials
- Metre stick (or a 1 m stick marked against a tape)
- A debe tin (the classic ~18 kg maize tin measure)
- A 20-litre jerrican (or any marked container)
- Exercise books
- Chalkboard for the dispute role-play "receipts"

## Vocabulary
- **unit** (kizio) — an agreed amount used to measure with
- **standard** (kiwango) — a unit that everyone agrees on and can check
- **cubit** (dhira) — old length from elbow to fingertip — different for every arm
- **SI units** — the international system: metre, kilogram, second and friends
- **litre** (lita) — common volume unit; 1 L of water has mass ≈ 1 kg

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Name any unit you used this week, at home or at the market.
2. Your grandmother measures cloth with her arm. What could go wrong if the shopkeeper measures with his arm?
3. Guess: how many centimetres make one metre?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept any: kilogram of sugar, litre of milk, metres of cloth, minutes.
2. Arms differ in length — buyer and seller will disagree on the amount, and someone is cheated.
3. 100 cm (accept guesses; it is answered again in the post-test).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — administer; collect.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — role-play: two learners "sell" a length of sisal rope using their own strides along the classroom — a tall learner's 10 strides vs a short learner's 10 strides. Measure both with the metre stick. The class sees the disagreement with their own eyes.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — stations tour. Station 1 (length): compare cubits — elbow-to-fingertip of three learners against the metre stick; record the spread. Station 2 (volume): pour water into the debe and the jerrican; read the jerrican's 20 L mark; discuss why the market debe was standardised by law. Station 3 (mass and time): lift 1 kg of sugar (or 1 L of water = 1 kg) and feel it; clap steady seconds against a clock. At each station learners write the SI unit and why it beats the body version.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the four units of the course: metre (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, litre (L) for volume. Quick conversions: 1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm; 1 L = 1000 mL. Confront the misconception: "standards are just government rules" — they are *agreements that make trade fair and science repeatable*; the metre started as a physical bar, now defined by light itself, so anyone anywhere can check it.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *no shared unit, no fair trade, no repeatable science.* Homework: ask at home which units the family uses daily and where each standard comes from (shop scale, debe, clock).

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Name the SI unit for length and for mass. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why do body measures like the cubit cause market disputes? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Convert: (a) 2.5 m to cm; (b) 750 mL to L.
4. A trader's debe is dented inward. Who loses, and why does the law care?
5. Challenge: 1 L of water has mass 1 kg. What is the mass of water in a full 20 L jerrican, and why is that fact useful?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Metre (m) and kilogram (kg).
2. Bodies differ — each person's cubit/stride/handful gives a different amount, so buyer and seller can never agree without a shared standard.
3. (a) 2.5 × 100 = 250 cm; (b) 750 ÷ 1000 = 0.75 L.
4. The buyer loses — a dented tin holds less than a full debe while charging full price; standards law makes measures checkable so trade stays fair.
5. 20 kg. Useful: you can check any water container's volume just by weighing it (mass in kg = volume in L), and you can estimate loads a person or donkey can safely carry.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A trader sells a "debe" of maize whose tin is dented inward, removing 8% of its volume. A true debe holds about 18 L. If maize sells at 60 KSh per litre-equivalent, quantify the buyer's loss per debe.
   **Worked Answer:** Lost volume = 8% × 18 L = 0.08 × 18 = 1.44 L. Money lost = 1.44 × 60 = 86.4 KSh per debe. Over 10 debes that is 864 KSh — the price of honest standards enforcement in one market visit.
2. **Q:** The metre was once defined by a metal bar in Paris; today it is defined using the speed of light (299 792 458 m/s) and the second. Why did scientists abandon the bar?
   **Worked Answer:** A physical bar changes: it expands with temperature, wears at the ends, can be damaged, and copies drift. The speed of light is a constant of nature — any well-equipped laboratory anywhere can realise the metre from it, forever, with no pilgrimage to Paris. Standards improve from artefacts to constants.
3. **Q:** Kenya once standardised the debe, yet sacks and "gorogoro" tins persist. Using the language of fair tests from Lesson 004, explain the measurement problem with unstandardised containers.
   **Worked Answer:** The container is an uncontrolled variable: two "gorogoros" differ in volume, so price-per-container comparisons between traders are meaningless — like comparing fall times while changing two variables. A standard litre/kilogram measure makes the container a controlled variable so only the true amount differs.

## Safety Notes
- Full 20 L jerricans weigh 20 kg: two learners lift together with bent knees, or the teacher demonstrates; never lift overhead.
- Water spills make floors slippery — pour over a basin and mop drips immediately (see the class safety charter).
- The debe's cut rim can be sharp — check edges and fold or tape them before class handling.
- Role-play strides: clear the walkway first so learners do not collide while pacing.

## Teacher Notes
- Builds on Lessons 001–004; this is the foundation lesson for all of Unit 1's measuring tools (rulers, callipers, balances, clocks, containers).
- Ages 10–13: memorise m, kg, s, L and the ×100/×1000 conversions. Ages 14–17: add the economics and history-of-standards reasoning in the advanced questions.
- Local tips: a real debe from the market is worth the purchase — it is the most famous Kenyan measure; the standard debe is about 18 L (often quoted by its ~18 kg maize capacity). Weights and Measures officers used to stamp market tins — mention it.
- 1 L water = 1 kg is the course's most-used conversion; drill it today.
- CBE learner record: conversions in the post-test plus station notes give evidence for the measurement competency; keep one learner's station sheet as a portfolio sample.
