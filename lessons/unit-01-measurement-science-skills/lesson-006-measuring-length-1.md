# Lesson 006: Measuring Length I: Rulers and Tape Measures

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Use a ruler correctly: start at the zero mark, eye level with the mark, read to the nearest millimetre.
2. Choose the right tool and unit for the object: mm/cm for small, m for rooms.
3. Convert between mm, cm and m.
4. Measure desk, book, classroom length and their own footstride, recording with units.

## Materials
- 30 cm rulers (one per learner or pair)
- A tailor's tape measure (or 3–5 m builder's tape) per group
- Exercise books (to measure and to record in)
- Masking tape or chalk for classroom-length marks

## Vocabulary
- **millimetre** (mm) — smallest mark on the ruler; 10 mm = 1 cm
- **centimetre** (cm) — 100 cm = 1 m
- **metre** (m) — SI unit of length
- **parallax error** — wrong reading from viewing the scale at an angle
- **stride** (hatua) — one full step; a useful personal measure once calibrated

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. How many millimetres are in 1 cm? How many centimetres in 1 m?
2. A ruler's end is worn away. Where should you start measuring instead, and what must you remember to do?
3. Guess the length of your exercise book in cm — then hold the book and check your guess by eye.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. 10 mm = 1 cm; 100 cm = 1 m.
2. Start at a clear mark (e.g. 1 cm) and subtract that mark from the reading at the other end.
3. Accept any guess around 20 cm (typical exercise book ≈ 21 cm); the point is guessing before measuring.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — administer; rulers stay face-down until the Explore block.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show two rulers: one starting at the very edge, one with a worn edge. Ask: *does this book measure 20 cm or 21 cm?* Demonstrate parallax: read the same mark from above and from the side — the class sees the reading "move."
3. **Explore (15 min)** — measurement circuit, recording every result with units: (1) exercise book length and width with a ruler, zero-end and eye-level rules enforced; (2) desk length and height with a ruler or short tape; (3) classroom length with the tailor's tape, two learners holding ends, third reading; (4) each learner's footstride: walk five normal strides along a taped line, measure the total, divide by five. Compare strides around the class — back to Lesson 005's standard.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the ruler rules: zero (or subtract), scale touching the object, eye directly above the mark, read to the nearest mm. Unit choice: a book in mm/cm, a room in m; never mix units in one table. Confront the misconception: "the number on the ruler is the truth" — the *last digit is always an estimate*, and we say to which mark we read ("to the nearest 1 mm").
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *right tool, right unit, eye level, write the unit.* Homework: measure your bed and your footstride at home; bring both numbers with units.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Fill in: 1 cm = ___ mm; 1 m = ___ cm; 2.4 m = ___ cm. (mirrors pre-test)
2. State two rules for reading a ruler correctly. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A learner measures a pencil from the 2 cm mark to the 17 cm mark. How long is the pencil?
4. Why is a tailor's tape better than a 30 cm ruler for measuring the classroom? Give two reasons.
5. Challenge: learner A's stride is 52 cm, learner B's is 68 cm. They both pace "20 strides" of a field. How far apart are their estimates, in metres?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 10; 100; 240.
2. Accept any two: start at zero (or subtract the start mark); scale touching the object; eye directly above the mark; read to the nearest mm.
3. 17 − 2 = 15 cm.
4. It is long enough for one continuous reading (no adding errors from moving a short ruler many times) and it bends along any sag or obstacle; also quicker and one pair of hands can hold it.
5. A: 20 × 0.52 = 10.4 m; B: 20 × 0.68 = 13.6 m; difference = 3.2 m — uncalibrated strides make poor standards.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** The classroom is 7.4 m long. Convert this to mm and explain when mm precision is pointless.
   **Worked Answer:** 7.4 m × 1000 = 7400 mm. Millimetre precision is pointless when the object or the tool cannot support it: classroom walls are uneven, the tape sags by centimetres, and the metre marks dominate the error — quoting 7403 mm would pretend an accuracy we did not measure. Rule: your answer's precision should match the tool's (a tape: nearest cm; a ruler: nearest mm).
2. **Q:** Measuring a 30 cm desk edge with a 30 cm ruler, you misalign the zero by 2 mm and read the far end at an angle, adding 3 mm. What is the total error, and what is the percentage error?
   **Worked Answer:** Total error = 2 + 3 = 5 mm = 0.5 cm, so the reading could be 30.5 cm instead of 30.0 cm. Percentage error = (0.5 / 30.0) × 100 ≈ 1.7%. Small absolute errors matter more on short lengths — the same 5 mm on a 7.4 m classroom is only 0.07%.
3. **Q:** Design a way to measure the classroom length if the only tool is your calibrated stride of 60 cm, and estimate the uncertainty.
   **Worked Answer:** Pace the room heel-to-toe (not normal stride, which varies), counting strides; multiply by the calibrated stride length, e.g. 12 strides × 0.60 m ≈ 7.2 m. Uncertainty: stride varies by perhaps ±3 cm step to step; over 12 strides expect roughly ±0.1–0.2 m, so report "about 7.2 m, give or take 0.2 m" — honest measurement includes an uncertainty estimate.

## Safety Notes
- Rulers are not swords — no fencing; a snapped plastic ruler throws sharp fragments.
- Walking-stride activity: clear bags and benches from the pacing line to prevent trips.
- Tape measures retract fast and can pinch or whip fingers — rewind slowly, one hand guiding.
- Standing on desks to measure wall height is forbidden; measure reachable heights only.

## Teacher Notes
- Builds on Lesson 005 (units); leads directly to Lesson 007 (callipers) and Lesson 008 (estimation uses today's calibrated stride).
- Ages 10–13: drill the four ruler rules and unit writing. Ages 14–17: push error and precision language from the advanced questions.
- Common class discovery: exercise books are ~21 × 15 cm; strides vary hugely — both feed the standards story.
- Local tips: tailor's tapes are cheap at any market sewing stall and more forgiving than builder's tapes for young hands; a chalk line on the floor marks the stride course.
- CBE learner record: the completed measurement table (object, tool, value with unit) is direct evidence for the measurement competency; check units on every line.
