# Lesson 008: Estimation Skills: Guess, Then Measure

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Estimate lengths and masses before measuring, then score the error.
2. Calibrate their own "internal ruler" using known references (stride, hand span, 1 kg bag).
3. Explain why fundis and market traders estimate first — and when estimation is not enough.
4. Use shadow ratios to estimate a tall object's height (preview of similar triangles).

## Materials
- Five sticks of various lengths (10 cm to 2 m), labelled A–E
- Five stones of various masses (100 g to 2 kg), labelled A–E
- Metre stick and a balance or known 1 kg bag of sugar (reference mass)
- Chalk and sunny ground for the shadow demo
- Exercise books for the estimate-vs-measured tables

## Vocabulary
- **estimate** (makadirio) — a reasoned guess made before measuring
- **calibrate** (rekebisha) — to check and adjust your estimator against a known standard
- **reference** (rejeo) — a known size you carry in memory (your stride, a 1 kg bag)
- **error** (kosa) — estimate minus measured value; the sign shows over- or under-guessing
- **ratio** (uwiano) — a comparison of two quantities by division

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Without tools, estimate the height of your classroom door in metres.
2. Estimate the mass of your exercise book in grams.
3. Why might a trader who estimates well still choose to weigh a customer's order?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept 1.8–2.5 m (typical doors ≈ 2.0 m); score it in the lesson.
2. Accept 50–150 g (typical A5 96-page book ≈ 80–100 g).
3. Estimates guide, standards settle payment — money needs a measurement both sides trust (Lesson 005).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — administer; learners keep their estimates to compare later.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a stick and a stone: *how long? how heavy?* Take rapid-fire guesses, then measure both live. Notice together: some learners were close — how did they do it?
3. **Explore (15 min)** — the estimation gauntlet. Round 1 (cold): each learner estimates all five sticks (cm) and all five stones (g), writing estimates only. Round 2 (calibrated): now handle the references — a metre stick, your known hand span, the 1 kg bag — and re-estimate. Then measure everything and fill the table: estimate, measured, error. Compute who improved most between rounds. Finish with the shadow preview: measure a learner's height and shadow, and the flagpole's shadow, on sunny ground; set up the ratio height/shadow.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the method: estimate → calibrate against a reference → measure → learn from your error. Estimation is a real fundi skill (cutting mabati, judging loads), but it is checked by measurement whenever money or safety is involved. Confront the misconception: "estimating is just guessing" — a calibrated estimate is a skill with measurable error, and practice shrinks the error (their Round 2 numbers prove it).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *guess first, measure second, learn from the gap.* Homework: estimate then measure three objects at home (one length, one mass, one volume); record both values with units.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Estimate the length of this chalkboard in metres, then state what reference you used. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Estimate the mass of a full 500 ml water bottle in grams, and justify using a known fact. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A learner estimated a stick at 45 cm; it measured 52 cm. Find the error and say whether it is an over- or under-estimate.
4. Give one situation where an estimate alone is fine, and one where you must measure.
5. Challenge: your 1.6 m friend casts a 1.2 m shadow at the same time a tree casts 9.0 m. Estimate the tree's height.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Accept 3–5 m with a stated reference ("about four of my strides", "two door-heights").
2. 500 ml of water has mass 500 g (1 L = 1 kg), plus the bottle ≈ 520–550 g total; accept ~0.5 kg with the water fact as justification.
3. Error = 45 − 52 = −7 cm; an under-estimate by 7 cm.
4. Estimate fine: judging how much rope to cut for a clothesline (slack is harmless). Must measure: selling maize by the kilogram, cutting glass/mabati to fit, medicine doses.
5. Shadow ratio = 1.2/1.6 = 0.75, so tree height = 9.0 ÷ 0.75 = 12 m (or equivalently 9.0 × 1.6/1.2 = 12 m).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A flagpole casts a 6.0 m shadow at the same moment your 1.6 m height casts a 1.5 m shadow. Compute the pole height using similar triangles.
   **Worked Answer:** The sun's angle is the same for both, so height/shadow is equal: pole/6.0 = 1.6/1.5. Pole = 6.0 × 1.6 / 1.5 = 9.6/1.5 = 6.4 m. This is similar triangles — the height-to-shadow ratio is shared by every object at that moment.
2. **Q:** Your stride estimates a field as 84 m, but the tape says 92 m. Your stride count was 120 paces. What is your true stride length, and what stride did you assume?
   **Worked Answer:** True stride = 92/120 = 0.767 m ≈ 0.77 m. Assumed stride = 84/120 = 0.70 m. You under-calibrated by 0.07 m per pace — about 9% low, which became a 8.7% under-estimate of the field. Calibration error scales with distance; check your stride over a known 20 m before trusting it over 100 m.
3. **Q:** A trader claims she can estimate 1 kg of beans by hand within 20 g. Design a fair test of her claim (refer to Lesson 004).
   **Worked Answer:** Independent variable: trial number (e.g. 10 trials). Dependent: error = her scooped mass − 1000 g, read on a checked scale. Controlled: same beans, same bowl, blind to the scale, no adjusting after scooping. Accept the claim if most trials land within ±20 g. Report the mean error and the spread — a fair test measures skill rather than debating it.

## Safety Notes
- Sun exposure during the shadow activity: hats on, work in short bursts, and never look directly at the sun — read shadows only.
- Stones stay below waist height during handling; no tossing between learners.
- Walking the flagpole shadow: one group at a time near the pole base; watch for guy-ropes and pegs as trip hazards.
- The metre stick is a measuring tool, not a walking stick or pointer at eye level.

## Teacher Notes
- Builds on Lessons 005–007 (units and tools); the shadow ratio previews similar triangles (used again in Unit 1's measurement work and later optics).
- Ages 10–13: run both estimation rounds and celebrate most-improved estimators. Ages 14–17: add the error calculations and the similar-triangles advanced questions.
- Keep each class's calibrated stride list — it feeds fieldwork later in the course.
- Local tips: markets are estimation academies — a grain trader's scoop and a mama mboga's bunch sizing are calibrated skills; invite learners to interview one trader about how they learned their hands.
- CBE learner record: the estimate-vs-measured table with signed errors is direct evidence of measurement and data-interpretation competencies.
