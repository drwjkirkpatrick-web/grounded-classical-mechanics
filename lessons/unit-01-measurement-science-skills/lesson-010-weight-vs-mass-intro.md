# Lesson 010: Weight vs Mass: A First Look

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define mass as the amount of matter (kg) and weight as gravity's pull on that mass (N).
2. Use W = m × g with g = 10 N/kg to convert between mass and weight.
3. Interpret a rubber-band spring balance as a weight measurer, not a mass measurer.
4. Explain why your mass stays the same on the Moon but your weight changes.

## Materials
- Rubber band strip (from an old inner tube is best), one per pair
- Paperclip bent into a hook
- Jerrican with water (or a 1 kg bag of sugar) as a known load
- Marker pen and a card strip for a rough scale
- Exercise books

## Vocabulary
- **mass** (uzani) — amount of matter in a body; measured in kilograms
- **weight** (uzito) — the force with which gravity pulls a body; measured in newtons (N)
- **newton** — the unit of force; 1 kg is pulled with about 10 N on Earth
- **gravity** (graviti / uvutano) — Earth's pull on everything near it
- **spring balance** (mizani ya springi) — a stretch-based force meter

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. In the market, "nataka kilo mbili za ngano" — is kilo a measure of matter or of pull? Your best guess.
2. Would you be heavier, lighter or the same on the Moon? Guess and say why.
3. What happens to a rubber band when you hang a jerrican of water on it?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept either with reasoning; scientifically it is the amount of matter (mass), measured by comparison with standard kilograms.
2. Lighter — the Moon pulls less strongly. (Accept any reasoned guess.)
3. It stretches; the heavier the load, the longer it gets.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — distribute slips; collect before the demo.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hang the jerrican from the rubber band in front of the class. It stretches dramatically. Ask: *what is pulling it down — and would that pull be the same everywhere in the universe?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build a simple spring balance: knot the rubber strip to the paperclip hook, tape a card scale beside it, mark zero with no load, then mark the stretch for a half jerrican and a full jerrican. They use it to "weigh" a shoe and a book, recording stretch in centimetres. Teacher circulates asking: *are you measuring matter, or pull?*
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the two ideas side by side: **mass** = matter in the body (kg, never changes wherever you go); **weight** = gravity's pull on that matter (newtons, changes with the strength of gravity). Introduce W = m × g with g = 10 N/kg on Earth. Confront the big misconception head-on: kilograms and newtons are not the same thing wearing different clothes — one is matter, the other is force.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *mass is you; weight is the Earth's pull on you*. Homework: ask at home what the bathroom scale reads (kg) and calculate your own weight in newtons.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Is the kilogram a measure of matter or of gravitational pull? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Would your mass and your weight change if you went to the Moon? Answer for each. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A bag of maize has mass 12 kg. Calculate its weight on Earth (g = 10 N/kg).
4. Why does a rubber-band balance actually measure weight and not mass?
5. Challenge: a trader's spring balance reads correctly in Nairobi. Carried to the Moon, would it read the same load as higher, lower or equal? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Matter (mass).
2. Mass stays the same (same matter); weight decreases because the Moon's gravity is weaker.
3. W = m × g = 12 kg × 10 N/kg = 120 N.
4. It responds to the downward pull on the load — the force stretching it. The same mass in weaker gravity would stretch it less, so it measures pull (weight).
5. Lower — the same mass is pulled with less force on the Moon, so the spring stretches less and under-reads.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Your mass is 45 kg. State your weight on Earth (g = 10 N/kg) and on the Moon (g = 1.6 N/kg).
   **Worked Answer:** Earth: W = 45 × 10 = 450 N. Moon: W = 45 × 1.6 = 72 N. The mass is 45 kg in both places — only the pull changes.
2. **Q:** A spring balance reads 38 N when holding a fish at a Mombasa market. What is the fish's mass? What would the same fish's mass and weight be on the Moon?
   **Worked Answer:** m = W/g = 38/10 = 3.8 kg. On the Moon the mass is still 3.8 kg; weight = 3.8 × 1.6 ≈ 6.1 N.
3. **Q:** A student says, "My weight is 52 kilograms." Correct the statement in two different ways and explain which one a physicist prefers.
   **Worked Answer:** Either "my mass is 52 kg" or "my weight is 520 N" (52 × 10). The physicist prefers the second when talking about weight, because weight is a force and the newton is the force unit; kilograms measure mass only.

## Safety Notes
- A stretched rubber band can snap and flick the hook toward eyes — never stretch toward the face; check bands for cracks before use and replace perished rubber.
- Lift the jerrican with two hands and a straight back; a 20 L jerrican is 20 kg.
- Water spills make floors slippery — mop immediately and keep the demo away from walkways.
- Do not hang body parts (fingers) from the hook to "calibrate" — use objects only.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 005 and 009 (units, mass). This is the first force measured in newtons; Unit 2 builds the whole forces strand on it.
- Ages 10–13: keep to the two-word summary — mass = matter, weight = pull. Ages 14–17: push the W = mg calculations and the Moon questions.
- Inner-tube rubber from a boda boda fundi stretches far better than shop rubber bands; ask a day ahead. Calibrate against a 1 kg sugar bag (10 N) for a trustworthy scale mark.
- CBE assessment: the pre/post mirror pair (mass vs weight) is the growth evidence; note learners still writing "weight in kg" for follow-up.
