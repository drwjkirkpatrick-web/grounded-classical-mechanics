# Lesson 011: Measuring Time: Heartbeats, Stopwatches and Sundials

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** sundial

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Name the second as the SI base unit of time and count seconds reliably.
2. Calibrate their resting pulse against a clock and state why body clocks drift.
3. Build a stick-and-shadow sundial and mark hourly shadow positions.
4. Explain that any good clock needs a regular, repeating event.

## Materials
- Stopwatch or phone clock (one per group; a wall clock with a seconds hand works)
- Straight stick, 30–50 cm, one per group
- Chalk or a sharp stone for marking
- Sunny flat ground (school field) — or a torch and a bottle for indoor simulation
- Exercise books and pencils

## Vocabulary
- **second** (sekunde) — the SI base unit of time
- **pulse** (mapigo ya moyo) — heartbeat felt at the wrist or neck
- **calibrate** (kurekebisha kipimo) — to check a measurer against a trusted one
- **sundial** (saa ya jua) — a clock that reads time from a shadow
- **interval** (kipindi) — the gap between two events

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. How long is one lesson at this school? Give the number in minutes.
2. Without a clock, how could you tell roughly when midday comes? One idea.
3. Do you think your heart beats at exactly the same rate all day? Yes or no, with a reason.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept the school's real figure (usually 35–45 minutes).
2. Accept: shadows are shortest at midday / sun is highest / church bell or radio.
3. No — it speeds up with exercise, fear, heat; slows at rest. (Accept any sensible reason.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected; note today's sunny window.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — ask the class to stand and estimate 60 seconds of silence, sitting when they think a minute has passed while you time them. The huge scatter of sit-down times is the hook: *we need better clocks than our feelings*.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — three quick stations in groups. (a) Pulse calibration: count wrist beats for exactly 60 s against the stopwatch; record beats per minute. (b) Repeat after 20 star jumps — compare. (c) Sundial start (see Hands-On Build): plant the stick, mark the shadow tip now, and agree to mark it again at the next hour.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board: the second is the base unit; 60 s = 1 min, 60 min = 1 h. Every clock, from a heartbeat to a phone, counts a repeating event. Confront the misconception that a heartbeat is a reliable clock — the star-jump data proves it drifts; a good clock needs a *regular* repeater.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a clock is a counter of regular repeats*. Homework: mark the sundial shadow once more near sunset with an adult and note the clock time.

## Hands-On Build: Stick-and-Shadow Sundial
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; choose the ground the day before.*

### Parts
- Straight stick — 1 per group — 30–50 cm, as straight as possible
- Chalk — 2–3 sticks per group — or a sharp stone for scratching marks
- Sunny flat ground — school compound — must stay undisturbed all day
- Small stones — 4–6 per group — to pin markings against wind

### Workflow
1. Push the stick vertically into flat sunny ground. **Checkpoint:** sight it from two directions — it must look upright from both, not leaning.
2. At a known clock time, mark where the shadow tip falls with chalk; write the time beside the mark.
3. Predict where the tip will be in one hour; place a small stone at the guess.
4. One hour later, mark the true tip position and the new time. **Checkpoint:** the two marks should differ — if they are identical, the stick or the marks were disturbed.
5. Connect marks to the stick base with scratched arcs to make the first hour lines.
6. Plan to add one mark each daylight hour; by tomorrow the class reads time to the nearest hour.

### Common Mistakes
- **Leaning stick** — hours come out uneven; re-set it vertically before marking.
- **Ground that floods or is swept** — marks vanish; pick a quiet corner, not a path.
- **Marking the shadow edge, not the tip** — edges blur; always mark the sharp tip.
- **Giving up on a cloudy hour** — skip it and continue; a sundial needs sun.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. What is the SI base unit of time? (mirrors pre-test item on time units)
2. Without any clock, name one natural repeating event you could use to estimate time. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Juma counts 72 heartbeats in a minute at rest, then 110 after running. Is his heart a good clock? Explain using the data.
4. Describe how to read the time from a stick sundial.
5. Challenge: why does every good clock — sundial, watch, phone — contain or follow something that repeats regularly?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The second.
2. Accept: moving shadow, sunrise/sunset, regular bell, heartbeat (with its weakness noted).
3. No — a good clock must tick at a constant rate; his pulse changed from 72 to 110 beats per minute with activity, so it drifts.
4. The stick's shadow tip falls on or near a marked hour line; the nearest marked line (with its recorded time) tells the hour.
5. Because time is measured by counting equal intervals; only a regular repeater gives equal intervals to count.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Why does a Kenyan sundial disagree with clock time across the year, even though both are "correct"? Introduce the equation of time qualitatively.
   **Worked Answer:** Clocks keep mean solar time — every day counted as exactly 24 equal hours. The true sun runs fast or slow through the year because Earth's orbit is slightly elliptical (orbital speed varies) and its axis is tilted (the sun's eastward motion projects unevenly onto our sky). The difference, called the equation of time, reaches roughly ±15 minutes. Near the equator the tilt effect dominates; a Nairobi sundial can read up to a quarter hour off clock time even with perfect construction.
2. **Q:** A learner's pulse is 66 beats per minute at rest. She times a jiko boiling water as "90 heartbeats". How long is that in seconds, and what assumption did she make?
   **Worked Answer:** 90 beats ÷ 66 beats per minute = 1.36 min ≈ 82 s. Assumption: her pulse stayed at the resting rate the whole time — false if she was rushing around the kitchen, which is exactly why this is a poor clock.
3. **Q:** Kenya sits almost on the equator. Why are our sundial hour lines nearly evenly spaced through the year compared with a sundial in Norway?
   **Worked Answer:** Near the equator the sun's daily path is always steep — day length barely changes (about 12 h all year), so the shadow sweeps through nearly the same angle each hour in every season. In Norway, summer days are long and winter days short, so the usable hour-line pattern changes strongly with season.

## Safety Notes
- Never look directly at the sun while checking shadows — judge the shadow on the ground only.
- Star jumps: clear desks and stones from the area; learners with asthma sit this station out and time-keep instead.
- Sunny ground in Kenya means heat — hats and water for the outdoor block; keep it to 15 minutes.
- Sticks pointed upward are eye-level hazards for shorter learners — push them fully into the ground, cap with tape if tall.
