# Lesson 012: Measuring Volume I: Regular Solids and Containers

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define volume as the space an object or liquid occupies.
2. Calculate the volume of a box shape using V = l × w × h.
3. Convert between cm³, litres and m³ (1 L = 1000 cm³; 1 m³ = 1000 L).
4. Read volume markings on a jerrican and sufuria with healthy suspicion.

## Materials
- Jerrican (20 L) and a smaller bottle with markings (e.g. 500 ml soda bottle)
- Sufuria of known size
- Ruler or tape measure
- Water in a basin
- Exercise books

## Vocabulary
- **volume** (kiasi) — the amount of space something occupies
- **litre** (lita) — everyday liquid unit; 1 L = 1000 cm³
- **cubic centimetre** (sentimita za ujazo) — a cube 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm
- **cubic metre** — SI volume unit; 1 m³ = 1000 L
- **marking** (alama) — the printed line on a container

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Which holds more: a 20 L jerrican or a 2 L soda bottle? By how many times?
2. A box is 10 cm long, 5 cm wide and 2 cm high. How many 1 cm cubes fit inside? Guess.
3. What unit do we buy milk in at the shop?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The jerrican; 10 times more.
2. 100 cubes (10 × 5 × 2) — accept any guess at this stage; revisit in the lesson.
3. Litres (or millilitres / "half litre").

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the "20-litre" jerrican. Ask: *the maker printed 20 L. Should we believe him? How could we check without pouring a single drop?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups measure the inside length, width and water-depth of the jerrican with a ruler (in cm), then compute V = l × w × h in cm³ and convert to litres (÷ 1000). Repeat for the sufuria approximated as a box or cylinder by estimation. Compare each measured volume with the printed marking and discuss the difference.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the conversion ladder: 1 m³ = 1000 L; 1 L = 1000 cm³; 1 cm³ = 1 ml. Volume of a regular box = l × w × h. Confront the misconception that a tall container must hold more than a wide one — pour equal water between a tall bottle and a sufuria to kill that idea.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *three lengths make one volume*. Homework: find one labelled container at home, measure it, and check the label.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Arrange from largest to smallest: 1 m³, 1 L, 100 cm³. (mirrors pre-test size comparison)
2. A box is 10 cm × 5 cm × 2 cm. What is its volume in cm³? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Convert: (a) 3.5 L to cm³; (b) 2500 cm³ to litres; (c) 2 m³ to litres.
4. A water trough is 1 m long, 0.5 m wide and 0.4 m deep. How many litres of water fill it?
5. Challenge: your measured jerrican volume comes out bigger than the "20 L" label. Give two possible reasons.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 1 m³ > 1 L > 100 cm³ (1 m³ = 1000 L; 1 L = 1000 cm³; so 100 cm³ = 0.1 L).
2. 10 × 5 × 2 = 100 cm³.
3. (a) 3500 cm³; (b) 2.5 L; (c) 2000 L.
4. V = 1 × 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 m³ = 0.2 × 1000 = 200 L.
5. The label marks usable/fill volume, not brim volume; the walls are not a perfect box (rounded corners widen it); or the maker under-labels to guarantee at least 20 L.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A "20-litre" jerrican measures 24 × 18 × 47 cm internally. Compute its true volume in litres and the percentage error of the label.
   **Worked Answer:** V = 24 × 18 × 47 = 20 304 cm³ = 20.304 L ≈ 20.3 L. Label error = (20.304 − 20)/20.304 × 100% ≈ 1.5% under-statement. The jerrican truly holds about 20.3 L — the label is honest within about 1.5%.
2. **Q:** A school water tank is a cuboid 2 m × 1.5 m × 1 m. A family uses 150 L per day. How many full days does a full tank last, and what assumption are you making?
   **Worked Answer:** V = 2 × 1.5 × 1 = 3 m³ = 3000 L. Days = 3000/150 = 20 days. Assumes the tank starts completely full and no refilling, leaks or evaporation.
3. **Q:** Why do scientists prefer m³ and cm³ over litres when writing formulas, even though shops sell in litres?
   **Worked Answer:** The litre is defined from the cubic decimetre (1 L = 1000 cm³ exactly) but it is not an SI base-derived unit with a power-of-ten link to the metre in one step — converting needs the factor 1000 both ways. Cubic metres come straight from the base unit (length³), so formulas like density = mass/volume stay in pure SI with no hidden conversion constants.

## Safety Notes
- Water on a cement floor is a slip hazard — measure over a basin and wipe spills at once.
- Jerrican edges and cut tins can be sharp; use an intact jerrican and tape any rough rim.
- Do not drink the measuring water; it has sat in storage containers.
- A full 20 L jerrican is 20 kg — lift with two hands, knees bent, or work with it half full.
