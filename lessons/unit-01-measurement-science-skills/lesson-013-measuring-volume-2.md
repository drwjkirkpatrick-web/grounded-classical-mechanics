# Lesson 013: Measuring Volume II: Displacement for Irregular Objects

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** eureka-can

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain why a ruler cannot measure the volume of a stone, but water can.
2. Build an overflow (eureka) can from a tin with a spout hole.
3. Measure the volume of an irregular object by collecting displaced water.
4. State that the volume of water pushed out equals the volume of the object.

## Materials
- Empty tin (1–2 L) with a small spout hole punched near the top — or a plastic bottle with a straw spout glued in
- Basin to catch overflow
- Stones of two or three sizes, tied with string
- Measuring container (graduated jug, or a bottle calibrated with the jerrican from Lesson 012)
- Water supply and a rag per group

## Vocabulary
- **displacement** (usukumaji wa maji) — water pushed aside by an object entering it
- **overflow can** (eureka can) — a full can with a spout that catches displaced water
- **irregular** (isiyo ya kawaida) — having no simple shape for a formula
- **submerge** (kuzamisha) — to put fully under water
- **meniscus** — the curved surface of water; read it at eye level

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Why is it hard to find the volume of a stone using a ruler?
2. You step into a full basin of water. What happens to the water, and why?
3. Guess: a small stone is dropped into a full glass. The water that spills out — is its volume more, less, or equal to the stone's volume?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Its sides are not straight; l × w × h needs a box shape.
2. It overflows — the body pushes water out of the way.
3. Equal (accept any guess now; the lesson proves it).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; groups collect their tins and stones.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — retell Archimedes in the bath, shouting "Eureka!" in Syracuse. Ask: *a king's crown is not a box — how did the bath betray its volume?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups build the eureka can (see Hands-On Build), then measure the volumes of two stones by displacement, recording each in cm³. Fast groups check whether two different-shaped stones of equal volume displace equal water.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the key law: **volume of displaced water = volume of the submerged object**. The water has nowhere else to go, so it faithfully reports the object's size. Confront the misconception that the object's *weight* sets the spill — a light big mango spills more than a heavy small nail; size, not heaviness, displaces.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *water keeps the accounts of volume*. Homework: measure the volume of one irregular object at home (a potato works) and bring the number.

## Hands-On Build: Eureka Can from a Tin
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; punch spout holes the day before with a nail.*

### Parts
- Empty tin — 1 per group — 1–2 L, clean, label removed; spout hole pre-punched 2 cm below the rim (adult job — sharp metal)
- Basin — 1 per group — catches the overflow
- Measuring container — 1 per group — graduated jug or calibrated bottle
- Stones on string — 2–3 per group — string lets you lower without splashing
- Water and rag — per group — for filling and wiping

### Workflow
1. Set the tin in the basin and fill it with water until it stops dripping from the spout hole. **Checkpoint:** wait until the drip stops completely — the water level is now exactly at spout height.
2. Empty and dry the measuring container; place it under the spout.
3. Lower one stone slowly by its string until fully under water. Catch every drop that comes out.
4. **Checkpoint:** if water splashed over the rim instead of the spout, start over — that water escaped the accounts.
5. Read the collected water volume in cm³ (or ml — same thing). Record it as the stone's volume.
6. Repeat for the second stone; compare with a ruler estimate to see how wrong the ruler was.

### Common Mistakes
- **Reading while dripping continues** — volume too small; wait for the last drop.
- **Stone touching the spout or rim** — blocks the flow; hold it centred.
- **Tin not full to spout level first** — the first water swallowed is never measured; always start dripping-full.
- **Splashing the stone in** — spillage over the rim inflates the reading; lower slowly on the string.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Why can we not measure a stone's volume accurately with a ruler? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A stone lowered into a full eureka can pushes out 45 cm³ of water. What is the stone's volume? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Put these in order of the water they displace when submerged: a 1 kg nail, a 1 kg mango, a 0.5 kg mango.
4. Describe the steps to measure a potato's volume with a eureka can.
5. Challenge: a learner says the heavy nail displaces more water than the light plastic toy of the same size. Agree or disagree, with reasoning.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. It is irregular — no straight length, width and height to multiply.
2. 45 cm³ — displaced water volume equals object volume.
3. 1 kg mango = 0.5 kg mango if they are the same size — displacement follows size (volume), not mass; a bigger mango displaces most. Expected order: the two mangoes by size first, nail last (iron is dense, small volume per kg).
4. Fill can to the spout, wait for drips to stop, place dry measuring jug under spout, lower potato on string until submerged, collect all overflow, read the volume.
5. Disagree — equal size means equal displaced volume; weight is irrelevant to displacement (it matters for floating/sinking, later).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A stone displaces 85 cm³ of water and weighs 2.1 N in air. Compute its density (take g = 10 N/kg).
   **Worked Answer:** Mass m = W/g = 2.1/10 = 0.21 kg = 210 g. Density = m/V = 210 g / 85 cm³ ≈ 2.47 g/cm³ ≈ 2.5 g/cm³ = 2500 kg/m³ — typical rock.
2. **Q:** The displaced water from a stone is collected and found to have a mass of 85 g. Without a measuring jug, how big is the stone?
   **Worked Answer:** Water density is 1 g/cm³ (1000 kg/m³), so V = m/ρ = 85/1 = 85 cm³. Weighing displaced water is another way to read volume — this is why 1 L of water is exactly 1 kg.
3. **Q:** Archimedes' crown problem: a gold crown (density 19 300 kg/m³) and a same-mass fake made of gold-painted lead (11 300 kg/m³). Which displaces more water, and how does this expose the fake?
   **Worked Answer:** For equal mass, the less dense metal has more volume (V = m/ρ). The lead fake displaces more water than pure gold of the same mass. Archimedes compared the crown's overflow with that of an equal mass of pure gold — extra overflow proved cheaper metal inside.

## Safety Notes
- Punched tin edges are razor sharp — an adult punches and smooths spout holes; learners never handle the open cut edge without tape.
- Water plus cement floor equals falls — each group keeps a rag and wipes at once.
- Stones on strings can still drop onto toes — lower over the basin, never carry swinging.
- Do not drink from the experiment tins; they are workshop containers, not kitchenware.
