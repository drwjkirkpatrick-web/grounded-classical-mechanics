# Lesson 014: Density I: Heavy for Its Size

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define density as mass per unit volume and write ρ = m/V.
2. Give the units kg/m³ and g/cm³ and convert between them (1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³).
3. Predict which of two equal-size objects is denser from their masses.
4. Recall the density of water as 1000 kg/m³ and use it as the float/sink reference.

## Materials
- Wood block, a stone, and a steel bolt — chosen as close to the same size as possible
- Beam balance from Lesson 009 (or a known 1 kg sugar bag for hand comparison)
- Ruler for rough volume estimates
- Basin of water for the float/sink demo
- Exercise books

## Vocabulary
- **density** (msongamano) — how much mass is packed into each unit of volume
- **mass** (uzani) — amount of matter, in kg
- **volume** (kiasi) — space occupied, in m³ or cm³
- **float** (kuelea) — to stay on top of a liquid
- **sink** (kuzama) — to go to the bottom

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Which is heavier to carry: a sack of charcoal or the same-size sack of maize? Your experience.
2. A wood block and a stone are exactly the same size. The stone is heavier. What does that tell you about the stone's material?
3. Write what you think the word "density" means, in your own words.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The maize sack — charcoal is very light for its size.
2. Its matter is more tightly packed — more mass in the same space.
3. Accept any attempt; formal definition comes in the lesson.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the classic joke done seriously: *which is heavier, a kilogram of iron or a kilogram of feathers?* Let the class argue, then reframe: the real question is which is heavier **for the same size**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pass the three matched-size objects (wood, stone, steel) around in groups. Learners rank them by hand, then confirm on the balance, recording mass for each. Since the sizes match, the ranking by mass is a ranking by "packed-ness". Drop each in the water basin and record float or sink.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the definition: density = mass ÷ volume, ρ = m/V, units kg/m³ (SI) or g/cm³ (lab). Water is the reference: 1000 kg/m³ = 1 g/cm³. Things denser than water sink; less dense things float. Confront the misconception that "heavy things sink" — a heavy ship floats and a light nail sinks; it is density, not total heaviness, that decides.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *density is mass packed per space*. Homework: name one thing at home that floats and one that sinks, with a density reason.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. A kilogram of iron or a kilogram of feathers — which is heavier? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Two blocks are the same size; block A has mass 2 kg, block B has mass 5 kg. Which is denser, and by how many times? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A stone has mass 300 g and volume 120 cm³. Calculate its density in g/cm³.
4. The density of water is 1000 kg/m³. A block of wood is 600 kg/m³. Does it float or sink? Explain.
5. Challenge: a huge ship of steel floats, yet a small steel nail sinks. Resolve the paradox in two sentences.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Neither — both are one kilogram; the iron is just denser (smaller volume).
2. Block B; it has 5/2 = 2.5 times the mass in the same volume, so 2.5 times the density.
3. ρ = m/V = 300/120 = 2.5 g/cm³.
4. It floats — 600 < 1000 kg/m³, so it is less dense than water.
5. The ship's steel shell encloses air, so its average density (total mass over total volume) is less than water's; the nail is solid steel, denser than water, and sinks.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Convert 2.7 g/cm³ (aluminium) to kg/m³. Why do engineers prefer kg/m³?
   **Worked Answer:** 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³ (a gram is 1/1000 kg but a cm³ is 1/1 000 000 m³, so the ratio grows by 1000). Thus 2.7 g/cm³ = 2700 kg/m³. Engineers work in SI throughout — forces in newtons, masses in kg, lengths in metres — so density in kg/m³ plugs into formulas like mass = ρV and weight = mg without extra conversion factors.
2. **Q:** A mabati sheet of aluminium measures 2.0 m × 1.0 m × 0.001 m and its density is 2700 kg/m³. Find its mass.
   **Worked Answer:** V = 2.0 × 1.0 × 0.001 = 0.002 m³. m = ρV = 2700 × 0.002 = 5.4 kg.
3. **Q:** Mercury's density is 13 600 kg/m³. Will a steel bolt (about 7800 kg/m³) float or sink in mercury? What about in water? Explain both.
   **Worked Answer:** In mercury it floats (7800 < 13 600); in water it sinks (7800 > 1000). Floating depends only on whether the object's density is below the liquid's — "heavy" metals can float on a denser liquid.

## Safety Notes
- The steel bolt and stone are dense — a drop on a bare foot injures; pass objects hand-to-hand, never toss.
- Water basin demo: wipe splashes immediately to prevent slips on cement.
- Edges of cut wood blocks may have splinters — sand or tape rough edges before class.
- No tasting or mouthing of any workshop material (oils, rust, paint).

- Assumes Lessons 009, 012, 013 (mass, volume both ways). Lesson 015 immediately applies this by measuring real densities with the eureka can.
