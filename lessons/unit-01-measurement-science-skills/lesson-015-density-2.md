# Lesson 015: Density II: Measuring Densities of Local Materials

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Measure the density of an irregular solid using mass ÷ displaced volume.
2. Apply the full measurement chain: balance for mass, eureka can for volume, formula for density.
3. Compare measured densities with water (1000 kg/m³) and predict float or sink before testing.
4. Present results in a clean quantity-and-unit table.

## Materials
- Beam balance from Lesson 009 with known masses (or a spring balance)
- Eureka can set from Lesson 013, with basins and measuring jugs
- Test objects per group: a nail, a stone, a dry maize cob, a lump of charcoal
- Water and rags
- Exercise books for the results table

## Vocabulary
- **density** (msongamano) — mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V
- **displacement** (usukumaji wa maji) — water pushed out by a submerged object
- **prediction** (utabiri) — a statement made before testing
- **average density** — total mass over total volume, air holes included
- **porous** (yenye mapengo) — full of tiny air spaces

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Write the formula for density and the meaning of each symbol.
2. To find the density of a stone, what two measurements must you make?
3. Predict: will a lump of charcoal float or sink in water? Give a reason.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. ρ = m/V; ρ density, m mass, V volume.
2. Its mass (balance) and its volume (displacement).
3. Accept either with reasoning — most say sink because it is "like wood that got burnt"; the lesson surprises them.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; groups collect apparatus.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the charcoal: *wood floats. Charcoal is burnt wood. So — float or sink?* Take a class vote and write the tally on the board to revisit later.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups run the full measurement chain on all four objects: weigh each on the balance (mass in g), displace each in the eureka can (volume in cm³), then compute ρ = m/V. Before dropping each in water, they write a float/sink prediction from their density. The maize cob and charcoal need a push to submerge fully — note that.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — collect class data on the board: nail ≈ 7–8 g/cm³, stone ≈ 2.5–3 g/cm³, maize cob ≈ 0.3–0.6 g/cm³, charcoal often ≈ 0.4–0.9 g/cm³. Every value below 1 g/cm³ floats. Confront the misconception that burning makes wood "heavier" — charcoal floats because its pores trap air, so average density stays below water.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *measure mass, measure volume, divide*. Homework: predict, then test, whether a bar of soap floats at home; bring the verdict.

## Hands-On Build: Density Station (Balance + Eureka Can)
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; both instruments come from earlier lessons.*

### Parts
- Beam balance — 1 per group — from Lesson 009; check it is zeroed
- Eureka can — 1 per group — from Lesson 013; check the spout is clear
- Nail, stone, maize cob, charcoal — 1 set per group — dry objects only
- Measuring jug — 1 per group — calibrated bottle acceptable
- String — short pieces — for lowering objects gently

### Workflow
1. Zero the balance with empty pans, then find each object's mass in grams; record in the table.
2. Fill the eureka can to the spout and wait for drips to stop.
3. Lower each object on string until fully submerged; collect the overflow. **Checkpoint:** porous objects (cob, charcoal) must be held under until bubbles stop, or the volume reads low.
4. Read each displaced volume in cm³; record beside its mass.
5. **Checkpoint:** before calculating, check each pair makes sense — a nail should show a small volume for a solid mass.
6. Compute ρ = m/V for all four objects in g/cm³; predict float or sink; then test in the basin.

### Common Mistakes
- **Wet objects on the balance** — water adds mass; dry everything first.
- **Charcoal not held under** — trapped air keeps it up and the displaced volume reads small; hold it submerged.
- **Mixing units in the table** — g with m³ gives nonsense; stick to g and cm³, convert only at the end.
- **Skipping the prediction** — the predict-then-test habit is the science; insist on it.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Write the density formula and name each symbol and its unit. (mirrors pre-test)
2. To measure the density of an irregular object, name the two instruments needed and what each measures. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A stone has mass 260 g and displaces 100 cm³ of water. Find its density in g/cm³ and kg/m³.
4. An object's density is 850 kg/m³. Will it float or sink in water? Explain.
5. Challenge: charcoal comes from wood, and wood floats — yet people say "burnt things are heavier." Use your data to correct them.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. ρ = m/V: ρ density (kg/m³ or g/cm³), m mass (kg or g), V volume (m³ or cm³).
2. Balance (mass) and eureka/displacement can (volume).
3. ρ = 260/100 = 2.6 g/cm³ = 2600 kg/m³.
4. Float — 850 < 1000 kg/m³, less dense than water.
5. Measured charcoal density is below 1 g/cm³ (it floats); burning drives off matter and opens air pores, so average density falls, not rises.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Charcoal floats, yet it is wood-derived. Explain using trapped air pores and average density.
   **Worked Answer:** Solid wood substance has density near 600–800 kg/m³, already below water. Charring drives off water and volatile matter, leaving a skeleton full of air pores. Average density = total mass ÷ total volume, and the volume now includes air spaces of nearly zero mass, so the average falls further — often 400–900 kg/m³. While the pores hold air, the lump floats; soak it long enough and waterlogged charcoal can sink.
2. **Q:** A nail has mass 7.8 g and displaces 1.0 cm³ of water. Find its density in kg/m³ and identify the likely metal.
   **Worked Answer:** ρ = 7.8/1.0 = 7.8 g/cm³ = 7800 kg/m³ — that is steel (iron), matching common nail material.
3. **Q:** Your maize-cob density came out as 1.3 g/cm³, yet the cob floated in the basin test. Give the experimental error and the fix.
   **Worked Answer:** A density above 1 g/cm³ must sink, so the measurement is wrong — the cob was not held fully submerged (or bubbles kept rising), so displaced volume read low. Fix: hold the cob under with a thin stick until bubbling stops and measure again; the true volume is larger, dropping ρ below 1 g/cm³.

## Safety Notes
- Nails are sharp and often rusty — handle by the head, keep points away from palms, and ensure tetanus awareness; no throwing.
- Charcoal dust irritates eyes and lungs — handle lumps gently; no crushing in class; wash hands after.
- Water spillage rules from Lesson 013 apply: rags ready, wipe at once.
- Balance pans carry small loads only; the nail is fine, but no standing on or overloading the beam.
