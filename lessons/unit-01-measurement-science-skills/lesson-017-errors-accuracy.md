# Lesson 017: Errors, Accuracy and Parallax

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Distinguish random error (scatter) from systematic error (shift) with examples.
2. Demonstrate parallax error reading a ruler and state the eye-level rule.
3. Identify and correct zero error on a balance.
4. Improve a measurement by repeating readings and averaging.

## Materials
- Rulers, one per pair
- Spring balance (or the rubber-band balance from Lesson 010)
- A few objects to measure: a book, a desk, a stone
- A coin for the parallax demo
- Exercise books

## Vocabulary
- **error** (kosa) — the gap between your reading and the true value
- **accuracy** (usahihi) — closeness to the true value
- **parallax** — apparent shift of a mark when viewed from an angle
- **zero error** — an instrument that does not read zero when empty
- **average** (wastani) — the mean of repeated readings

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Two learners measure the same desk and get 119.8 cm and 120.1 cm. Is one of them lying? Explain.
2. A scale reads 0.2 kg with nothing on it. You weigh a fish and read 2.2 kg. What is the fish's true mass?
3. When you read a ruler from the side, does the reading change? Try it and describe.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. No — no measurement is exact; small differences are normal scatter (random error).
2. 2.2 − 0.2 = 2.0 kg (subtract the zero error).
3. Yes — the mark seems to move against the scale (parallax); accept any observation.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the coin demo: lay a coin under a transparent ruler (or beside one). View straight down, then from the left, then the right — the coin's edge "moves" across the markings. *Which reading is true? Only the eye-level one.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — three quick tasks in pairs. (a) Parallax: measure a book's length viewing from an angle, then from directly above; record both. (b) Zero error: check the spring balance with no load; record its reading; correct it in a second weighing. (c) Repeat and average: each partner measures the same desk width three times; pool all six readings, compute the mean, and note the scatter.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the two families: **random error** scatters readings both ways (unsteady hands, timing, estimation between marks) — reduced by repeating and averaging. **Systematic error** pushes every reading the same way (zero error, stretched ruler, parallax habit) — reduced only by fixing the instrument or the method. Confront the misconception that averaging cures everything: averaging cannot remove a systematic shift.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *random error averages away; systematic error must be fixed*. Homework: check one scale at home for zero error and report its reading when empty.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Two careful learners measure the same desk: 119.8 cm and 120.1 cm. Explain how both can be right. (mirrors pre-test)
2. An empty scale reads 0.2 kg; with a fish it reads 2.2 kg. True mass? (mirrors pre-test)
3. State the rule for where your eye must be when reading a ruler or scale, and why.
4. Classify as random or systematic: (a) your hand shakes when starting a stopwatch; (b) your ruler's first 2 mm are worn away; (c) you guess between millimetre marks.
5. Challenge: a class averages 20 readings of a desk length but the ruler used was stretched by 1 cm. Is the average accurate? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Both readings carry small random errors (estimation between marks, ruler placement); neither is exact, and their mean 119.95 ≈ 120.0 cm is the best estimate.
2. 2.2 − 0.2 = 2.0 kg.
3. Eye directly above/level with the mark, line of sight at 90° to the scale — otherwise parallax shifts the apparent position and corrupts the reading.
4. (a) random; (b) systematic — every reading is 2 mm short; (c) random.
5. No — averaging kills random scatter, but the 1 cm stretch shifts every reading the same way (systematic), so the average is still 1 cm off; the fix is a good ruler.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Three students measure a desk: 119.8, 120.1, 119.9 cm. Give the mean and the spread; decide whether a 4th reading of 122.0 cm is an outlier.
   **Worked Answer:** Mean = (119.8 + 120.1 + 119.9)/3 = 359.8/3 = 119.93 ≈ 119.9 cm. Spread = 120.1 − 119.8 = 0.3 cm. The 122.0 reading is 2.1 cm above the mean — about 7 times the whole spread of the careful readings — so it is an outlier (likely a misread or wrong zero point); exclude it and re-measure.
2. **Q:** A spring balance reads 0.4 N empty. It measures a stone as 3.4 N. A student "fixes" the zero error by averaging five readings of 3.4 N. Explain the mistake and give the correct weight.
   **Worked Answer:** Zero error is systematic — it shifts every reading by +0.4 N, and averaging identical shifted readings changes nothing. Correct by subtracting: W = 3.4 − 0.4 = 3.0 N.
3. **Q:** Why does repeating a measurement 10 times and averaging improve accuracy for random error, in terms of how the errors add?
   **Worked Answer:** Random errors are as likely positive as negative, so in a sum they partly cancel; the average's error grows like the spread divided by √N. Ten readings shrink the random error by about √10 ≈ 3.2 times. A systematic error, identical in every reading, divides to itself — which is why it survives averaging.

## Safety Notes
- Spring balances under load can slip and snap back — keep faces away from the line of pull.
- Rulers are measuring tools, not swords; no fencing in pairs work.
- Desk-moving for measurement: lift with legs, watch fingers between desks.
- Coin demo uses small objects — keep them off the floor to avoid slips and choking hazards for younger siblings at home.
