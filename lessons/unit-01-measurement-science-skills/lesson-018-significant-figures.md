# Lesson 018: Honest Numbers: Significant Figures and Rounding

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that a measurement's digits must match the instrument's precision.
2. Count significant figures in a stated value.
3. Round a calculated result to the correct number of significant figures.
4. Spot and correct false precision in everyday claims.

## Materials
- Rulers (millimetre-marked), one per pair
- Balances from earlier lessons
- Exercise books
- A calculator or phone for the false-precision demo (teacher's)
- Board and chalk

## Vocabulary
- **significant figures** (tarakimu muhimu) — the digits in a number that carry real measured meaning
- **precision** (usahihi wa kipimo) — how finely an instrument can read
- **rounding** (kukamilisha namba) — writing a number to fewer digits by rule
- **false precision** — more digits claimed than the measurement justifies
- **estimate** (makadirio) — the last, uncertain digit between the marks

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Your ruler has millimetre marks. You measure a pencil. Which reading is sensible: 14.5 cm, or 14.52731 cm? Why?
2. A trader announces his cow weighs "423.771 kg". What is suspicious?
3. Round 3.847 to two decimal places.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. 14.5 cm — the ruler cannot show hundredths of a millimetre; extra digits are invented.
2. No market scale reads to the nearest gram on a cow; the digits past the scale's ability are fiction.
3. 3.85.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — tell the museum joke: the guide says the fossil is "90 000 006 years old" because six years ago they told him it was 90 million. The class laughs — then the point lands: digits you did not earn are jokes, not data.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs measure a book's length with the mm ruler (record honestly, e.g. 29.7 cm), then compute the book's area: length × width, both measured. They write the calculator's full answer, then round it to the significant figures of the least precise measurement. Repeat for a desk. Class compares rounded answers on the board.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the rules: (1) count significant figures from the first non-zero digit; (2) a result is only as good as its worst input — round the answer to the fewest significant figures among the measurements; (3) zeros at the end of whole numbers are ambiguous — use units to be honest (say 2.0 m, not "2 m maybe"). Confront the misconception that more digits means more science — more digits without a better instrument means less honesty.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *digits are earned by the instrument*. Homework: find one false-precision claim at home or in the market and rewrite it honestly.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. A mm ruler measures a stick. Which report is honest: 31.2 cm or 31.2468 cm? Explain. (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is wrong with claiming a cow weighs 423.771 kg on a market scale? (mirrors pre-test)
3. How many significant figures: (a) 45.2; (b) 0.036; (c) 1000 (ambiguous — explain).
4. A rectangle measures 12.5 cm by 3.2 cm. Compute the area and report it with correct significant figures.
5. Challenge: a phone says your walking speed is 1.4837291 m/s, but your distance came from pacing and your time from a friend's count. Rewrite the speed honestly and defend the rounding.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 31.2 cm — the ruler resolves millimetres (one decimal in cm); later digits are invented.
2. The scale cannot weigh a cow to the gram; digits beyond its precision are false precision — honest report: about 424 kg.
3. (a) 3; (b) 2 (leading zeros only place the point); (c) ambiguous — could be 1, 2, 3 or 4; written 1.0 × 10³ it would be 2.
4. 12.5 × 3.2 = 40.0 cm² on the calculator; the least precise input (3.2) has 2 s.f., so report 40 cm² (i.e. 4.0 × 10¹ cm²).
5. Both inputs are rough (pacing ±10%, counted time ±1 s or worse), so at most 2 s.f. are defensible: 1.5 m/s.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Multiply 12.5 cm × 3.2 cm and state the area with correct significant figures. Justify the rule.
   **Worked Answer:** Raw product: 40.0 cm². Inputs have 3 and 2 significant figures. The rule: a result cannot be more precise than its least precise input — 3.2 cm means the true width lies between 3.15 and 3.25 cm, so the area lies between 39.4 and 40.6 cm². Only two digits survive: **40 cm²** (2 s.f.). Writing 40.0 cm² would claim knowledge of tenths we never measured.
2. **Q:** A class measures a jerrican's volume as 20 304 cm³ from internal dimensions 24 × 18 × 47 cm, each to the nearest cm. Report the volume honestly.
   **Worked Answer:** Each dimension has 2 s.f., so the volume deserves 2 s.f.: 20 304 → **2.0 × 10⁴ cm³** (≈ 20 L). The "304" detail is smaller than the uncertainty from rounding each side by up to 0.5 cm (which alone moves the volume by several hundred cm³).
3. **Q:** Rule-check: masses 2.50 kg and 2.5 kg are added. Which answer is honest: 5.00 kg, 5.0 kg, or 5 kg?
   **Worked Answer:** For addition, line up decimal places: 2.50 + 2.5 = 5.0 — the result keeps the least number of decimal places among inputs (one), giving **5.0 kg**. The hundredths digit of the 2.50 kg mass is swamped by the unknown hundredths of the 2.5 kg mass.

## Safety Notes
- This is a desk-based lesson; the only hazards are the usual ones — rulers stay on desks, not in the air.
- Calculator phones stay flat on desks and are used for physics only; collect them if attention drifts.
- If balances are demonstrated, load pans gently as before — no overloading beyond 1 kg.
