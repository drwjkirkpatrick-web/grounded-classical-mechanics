# Lesson 019: Designing Data Tables

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State the rules of a good data table: headers with quantity and unit, one reading per cell, raw data before processed columns.
2. Build a table for a stretch-vs-load experiment before doing it.
3. Criticise and repair a badly designed table.
4. Explain why the unit belongs in the header, never inside the cells.

## Materials
- Exercise books and rulers (for drawing ruled tables)
- Rubber strip (inner-tube) and a set of water-bottle "weights" (500 ml = 0.5 kg) for the demo
- A prepared "bad table" example on the board or a handout
- Pencils — never ink for raw data drafts

## Vocabulary
- **data** (data / takwimu) — recorded measurements
- **table** (jedwali) — rows and columns organising data
- **header** (kichwa cha jedwali) — the top cell naming quantity and unit
- **raw data** — readings exactly as measured, before any calculation
- **processed data** — values computed from raw data (means, differences)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. A learner records: "mass 2, stretch 5, mass 4, stretch 9" on a scrap of paper. List two problems with this record.
2. Where should the unit (cm, kg, s) be written in a table — in every cell or once in the header?
3. Design in words a table for recording how far a rubber strip stretches as you add water bottles. Name the columns.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Any two: no units, no headers, no clear pairing of which stretch goes with which mass, no repeated readings.
2. Once, in the header.
3. Accept: Load (bottles or kg) | Stretch (cm) — with optional mean column.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the "bad table": mixed units inside cells ("2 kg", "350 g" in one column), no headers, an average in the middle of raw rows. Ask: *would you trust this person's results? What exactly is wrong?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — demo first: hang the rubber strip, add one 500 ml bottle (0.5 kg = 5 N), measure stretch. Then pairs design the full table for the experiment *before* touching data: Load (N), Stretch trial 1 (cm), trial 2, trial 3, Mean stretch (cm). They rule it, fill two real rows from the demo, and finally repair the bad table from the Engage step into standard form.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the four rules: (1) every column has a header with quantity and unit, e.g. Load (N); (2) units never repeat inside cells; (3) raw readings first, processed columns (means) after; (4) rows ordered by the quantity you changed. Confront the misconception that tables are decoration — the table IS the experiment's memory; a bad table destroys good data.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *headers carry the units; raw before cooked*. Homework: rule a blank table for Lesson 020 (a timed walk: time and distance) and bring it.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. List two things wrong with this record: "mass 2, stretch 5, mass 4, stretch 9". (mirrors pre-test)
2. Where do units belong in a data table, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Rewrite honestly as a table: Load 5 N gave stretches 2.1, 2.3, 2.2 cm; load 10 N gave 4.0, 4.2, 4.1 cm. Include a mean column.
4. Why should raw trial readings appear before the mean column, not the mean alone?
5. Challenge: a table's column reads "Stretch" with cells "2 cm", "35 mm", "0.4 cm". State the fault and the fix.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Any two: no units; no headers; no repeat readings; unclear pairing/order.
2. In the header — once. Repeating units in cells invites mixed units and hides them; the header makes the unit a contract for the whole column.
3. Table: Load (N) | Trial 1 (cm) | Trial 2 (cm) | Trial 3 (cm) | Mean (cm); rows: 5 | 2.1 | 2.3 | 2.2 | 2.2; 10 | 4.0 | 4.2 | 4.1 | 4.1. (Means: 6.6/3 = 2.2; 12.3/3 = 4.1.)
4. Raw trials let anyone check the mean and spot outliers; a mean alone hides scatter and mistakes.
5. Units are mixed inside cells; fix: put "Stretch (cm)" in the header and convert all cells to cm (2.0, 3.5, 0.4).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Redesign this bad table into standard form: Column 1 "mass" with cells "2 kg, 350 g, 0.5 kg"; Column 2 "stretch" with cells "5, 9.5, 2" and no units; a mean of "5.5" written at the bottom of column 2.
   **Worked Answer:** Standard form — Header: Mass (kg) | Stretch (cm): rows 2.00 | 5.0; 0.35 | 9.5; 0.50 | 2.0. The false mean "5.5" is deleted or recomputed only within one load's trials; averaging stretches from different loads is meaningless. Better still, sort rows by mass: 0.35, 0.50, 2.00 kg.
2. **Q:** In the stretch experiment, why do we make load the leftmost column and order rows by increasing load?
   **Worked Answer:** Load is the independent variable — the quantity we choose. Convention puts it first, and ordering rows by it reveals the pattern (stretch growing with load) at a glance; a scrambled order hides trends and invites plotting errors later.
3. **Q:** A table has columns: Load (N), Extension (cm), Spring constant (N/cm). Which columns are raw and which processed? A learner computes k = load ÷ extension as 10 N ÷ 2.0 cm = 5 N/cm. Critique one step further.
   **Worked Answer:** Load and extension are raw (measured); spring constant is processed (calculated). Critique: k from a single point ignores the zero-load length and scatter — the honest method uses several load-extension pairs (or the gradient of the graph in Lesson 020+), not one division.

## Safety Notes
- Demo only: the loaded rubber strip can snap if over-stretched — two or three bottles maximum, faces away from the line of stretch.
- Water bottles as weights: caps tight, bottles intact; a burst bottle floods exercise books.
- Rulers stay on desks while ruling tables; no ruler fencing during peer-marking.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 009–018; this lesson formalises the recording habit they have been using informally. It feeds Lesson 020 (graphs) directly — tonight's homework table is tomorrow's plot.
- Ages 10–13: the four rules and a clean two-row table are the target. Ages 14–17: independent/dependent variable language and the processed-column critique.
- The "bad table" handout: invent one with real local flavour (mixed debes and kilograms of maize) — learners repair what they recognise.
- CBE assessment: the repaired table and the designed stretch table are the G10-PHY-1.1 data-presentation evidence.
