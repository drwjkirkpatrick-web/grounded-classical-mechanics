# Lesson 020: Graphs I: Plotting Your Walk

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Name the parts of a graph: axes, labels with units, scale, points, title.
2. Choose scales so a plot fills the page.
3. Plot distance–time data of a real 20 m walk as small sharp dots.
4. Draw a first line of best fit and say what it means.

## Materials
- Graph paper (or exercise books ruled into squares), one sheet per learner
- Tape measure or a measured 20 m course on the field
- Stopwatch or phone timer
- Pencils and rulers for straight lines
- The blank table designed as Lesson 019 homework

## Vocabulary
- **axis** (mhimili) — the numbered line along an edge of the graph
- **scale** (kizio) — how many units each square represents
- **origin** (asili) — the (0, 0) corner where axes meet
- **line of best fit** (mstari wa wastani) — the straight line passing nearest all points
- **plot** (kupiga grafu) — to place data points on the axes

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. In a distance–time graph, which quantity goes on the bottom (x) axis: time or distance?
2. You walked 20 m in 25 s. What two numbers describe the final point of your graph?
3. Why do scientists draw graphs instead of leaving numbers in tables? One reason.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Time — the quantity you control goes on the x-axis.
2. (25 s, 20 m) — time first, then distance.
3. Accept: shows patterns at a glance / reveals trends and odd points / easy comparisons.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; check the homework tables from Lesson 019 are ready.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — draw two graphs of the same walk: one with the data squeezed into a tiny corner, one filling the page. *Same numbers — which picture speaks?* The class chooses instantly. That choice is today's skill.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — field work first: one volunteer walks a steady 20 m while timekeepers call the time at 5 m, 10 m, 15 m, 20 m (markers pre-measured). Class records the four (time, distance) pairs in their tables. Back inside, each learner sets axes (time x, distance y), picks a scale so the data fills the page, plots the four points as small sharp dots, and rules a line of best fit through them from the origin.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the rules: label both axes with quantity and unit; choose scales that spread the data over most of the page; dots small and exact (never blobs); the line of best fit follows the trend, not every dot. Confront the misconception of "joining the dots" — real data has scatter; the honest line averages it.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect the graphs themselves.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a good graph fills the page and tells the truth*. Homework: plot today's walk again at home with a different scale and note which is clearer.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. In a distance–time graph, which axis carries time, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A walker reaches 20 m at 25 s. Write the final point's coordinates. (mirrors pre-test)
3. List three things every honest graph must have.
4. Points plotted: (5 s, 4 m), (10 s, 8 m), (15 s, 13 m), (20 s, 16 m). One point sits off the line. Which, and what do you do about it?
5. Challenge: your friend's graph uses 1 square = 1 s, so 25 squares of time cover only a strip of the page while 20 m of distance uses the full height. Criticise and fix.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The x-axis — time is the independent quantity we chose at each marker; distance depends on it.
2. (25 s, 20 m).
3. Any three: labelled axes with units; a sensible scale filling the page; a title; small accurate points; a line of best fit.
4. (15 s, 13 m) is off — expected near 12 m; keep the dot (never hide data) but draw the line of best fit through the main trend; it may be a timing slip worth a re-walk.
5. The scales are unbalanced — the plot is a thin strip that hides the pattern. Fix: choose scales so both data ranges spread over most of the page, e.g. time axis 1 square = 2 s and distance axis 1 square = 2 m.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Choose scales so the plot fills the page: a 20 m walk in 25 s on a sheet with 25 × 18 usable squares. Propose axis ranges and divisions.
   **Worked Answer:** Time axis (25 squares): range 0–25 s at 1 square = 1 s, or better 0–30 s at 1 square = 1.2 s ≈ use 0–25 s for a full-width plot. Distance axis (18 squares): range 0–20 m → 20/18 ≈ 1.1 m per square; choose 1 square = 1 m with the axis running to 18 m plus a 2 m margin, or 0–22.5 m at 1.25 m per square. Simplest honest choice: x-axis 0–25 s (1 square = 1 s), y-axis 0–20 m (1 square = 1 m) — data spans the whole sheet both ways.
2. **Q:** From the class line of best fit (20 m at 25 s), read the walker's speed. What does the line's steepness mean?
   **Worked Answer:** Speed = gradient = rise/run = 20 m / 25 s = 0.8 m/s. Steepness IS speed — a steeper distance–time line means a faster walker; a flat line means standing still.
3. **Q:** A learner plots distance on the x-axis and time on the y-axis, then computes the gradient as 1.25 s/m and calls it "speed". Untangle the error.
   **Worked Answer:** With axes swapped, the gradient is time ÷ distance = 25 s / 20 m = 1.25 s/m — that is *pace* (seconds per metre), the reciprocal of speed. True speed = 1/1.25 = 0.8 m/s. This is why axis convention matters: the y-over-x gradient must mean what you claim it means.

## Safety Notes
- Field segment: mark the 20 m course away from roads and the playing field's traffic; a teacher watches boundaries.
- Walkers walk — no running for this data; a sprint ruins both the steady-speed story and ankles.
- Sun and heat: hats and water if the field block is long; keep it brisk.
- Tape measures snap back when let go — reel in by hand, never release a stretched tape.
