# Lesson 021: Graphs II: Slope Tells Speed

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Read the gradient (slope) of a distance–time graph as a speed.
2. Explain that a steeper line means a faster journey.
3. State that a straight line through the origin means distance is proportional to time (constant speed).
4. Interpolate a missing point from a straight-line graph.

## Materials
- Graph paper (one sheet per learner; exercise-book squares work)
- Rulers (one per pair; a straight mabati offcut or book edge can substitute)
- Board drawn with a large distance–time grid
- Chalk and marker pens
- Prepared data tables from a "walk across the playground" (from Lesson 020 work)

## Vocabulary
- **gradient** (mwinamo) — how steep a line is; rise over run
- **origin** (asili) — the point (0, 0) where the axes meet
- **proportional** (sawia) — doubling one quantity doubles the other
- **interpolate** (kukadiria katikati) — read a value between measured points

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. On a distance–time graph, what do you think a steeper line tells us?
2. A walker covers 2 m every second. How far has she gone after 5 s?
3. Draw a rough line for a journey where the walker starts fast and then stops to rest.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept any sensible idea: the journey is faster / covers more distance in the same time.
2. 2 m/s × 5 s = 10 m.
3. Line rises steeply at first, then becomes flat (horizontal) during the rest.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before the board work begins.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — tell the story of two boda bodas leaving the same stage: one reaches 100 m in 10 s, the other in 20 s. Draw both journeys as two straight lines on the same axes. Ask: *which line belongs to which rider, and how can you tell at a glance?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs plot the class walk data on graph paper: time (s) on the x-axis, distance (m) on the y-axis. They join the points, then compute the gradient: pick two points far apart, rise ÷ run. They repeat for a second, slower data set and compare steepness. Teacher circulates asking: *what are the units of your gradient? What does a flat part of the line mean?*
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board: gradient = rise/run = distance/time = speed, in m/s. Steeper line = larger gradient = faster. A straight line through the origin means distance is proportional to time — the speed never changes. Show interpolation: read the distance at 7 s even though nobody measured it. Confront the misconception head-on: a horizontal line is not "going up a hill" — it means *stopped*.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark in class if time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the slope of a distance–time graph is the speed*. Homework: sketch a distance–time line for your walk to school tomorrow, marking one stop.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. On a distance–time graph, what does a steeper line tell us? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A walker covers 2 m every second. How far after 5 s, and what is the gradient of her graph? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A line passes through (0 s, 0 m) and (10 s, 40 m). Calculate its gradient with units and say what it means.
4. On the same graph, read off the distance at 5 s. What is this reading-between-points called?
5. Challenge: learner A's line is steeper than learner B's, but B walked further in total. Explain how both can be true.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The journey is faster — more distance covered per unit time.
2. 10 m; gradient = 10 m ÷ 5 s = 2 m/s.
3. Gradient = 40 m ÷ 10 s = 4 m/s; the walker moves at a constant speed of 4 m/s.
4. 20 m; interpolation.
5. A was faster (larger gradient) but walked for less time; B was slower but walked longer, so covered more total distance.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** On a distance–time graph a straight line passes through (2 s, 6 m) and (8 s, 30 m). Compute the gradient with units and state its physical meaning.
   **Worked Answer:** Gradient = rise/run = (30 − 6) m / (8 − 2) s = 24 m / 6 s = 4 m/s. Physically, it is the constant speed of the object: it advances 4 metres every second.
2. **Q:** A matatu's journey graph is a straight line from the origin to (300 s, 6000 m), then horizontal for 60 s, then rises again. Find the speed in the first part and explain the horizontal part.
   **Worked Answer:** Speed = 6000 m / 300 s = 20 m/s (= 72 km/h). The horizontal part means distance is not changing — the matatu is stopped (stage, traffic jam or police check) for 60 s.
3. **Q:** Two lines on the same axes: line P through (0,0) and (4 s, 12 m); line Q through (0,0) and (4 s, 20 m). Without plotting, which is faster and by how much?
   **Worked Answer:** P: 12/4 = 3 m/s. Q: 20/4 = 5 m/s. Q is faster by 5 − 3 = 2 m/s; on the graph Q's line is steeper.

## Safety Notes
- Rulers and metre sticks are for measuring, not fencing — supervise pairs during plotting.
- If any playground walking is repeated to check data, keep learners clear of the road and boda parking area.
- Pencils down during board explanations to avoid pokes when turning around in crowded desks.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 020 (plotting points and axes) has run; this lesson gives the plotted line its meaning.
- Younger learners (10–13): keep to "steeper = faster" and reading single points. Older learners: insist on units in every gradient and push the advanced questions with their own data.
- Graph paper is sold in stationery shops in every market town; if short, draw grids in exercise books with a ruler the day before.
- A good local data set: time a learner walking, then jogging, 20 m outside before the lesson.
- CBE assessment note: pre/post growth shows data-handling progress; file one plotted graph per learner as evidence for the investigation competency.
