# Lesson 022: Area and Volume Formulas in the Real World

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Measure a real rectangular floor and compute its area in m².
2. Calculate the volume of a cylindrical water tank using V = πr²h.
3. Convert between cubic metres and litres (1 m³ = 1000 L).
4. Explain why roof area matters for rainwater harvesting.

## Materials
- Tape measure (a fundi's 5 m tape; or a knotted string calibrated against a metre stick)
- String for measuring tank circumference
- Chalk for marking lengths on the floor
- A real cylindrical container to inspect: plastic water tank, bucket, or debe tin
- Board and marker pens
- Learners' exercise books for calculations

## Vocabulary
- **area** (eneo) — the flat surface a shape covers, in square units
- **volume** (ujazo) — the space inside a container, in cubic units
- **cylinder** (silinda) — a solid with circular ends, like a tank or debe
- **radius** (nusu kipenyo) — half the distance across a circle
- **litre** (lita) — 1000 litres fill exactly 1 m³

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Our classroom is about 6 m long and 5 m wide. Guess its floor area.
2. Which holds more water: a tall thin tank or a short wide one? Give a reason.
3. How many litres do you think make 1 cubic metre? Write your guess.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. 6 m × 5 m = 30 m² (accept close estimates with the multiplication idea).
2. Accept reasoned answers either way; volume depends on both width and height, not one alone.
3. Accept any guess now; the answer 1000 L is revealed in the lesson.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before measuring starts.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a debe tin and a jerrican: *the fundi who made the tank outside — how did he know how many jerricans it holds?* Board the question and take two quick guesses.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — three rotating activities in pairs. (a) Floor area: chalk-mark the classroom length and width, measure with the tape, compute A = l × w in m². (b) Tank volume: wrap string around a bucket or tank to get circumference C, find r = C ÷ 2π (π ≈ 3.14), measure height h, compute V = πr²h. (c) Litres link: the teacher shows a 1 L bottle and a chalk-drawn 1 m × 1 m × 1 m cube frame on the floor; class reasons out that the cube holds 1000 bottles. Groups record all values on the board table.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate formulas: rectangle A = l × w; circle A = πr²; cylinder V = πr²h. Key conversion: 1 m³ = 1000 L, so multiply cubic metres by 1000 to get litres. Teaser for roof catchment: rain falls on the *area* of the roof, so a bigger roof fills a tank faster. Confront the misconception that "taller always means more water" — a wide short tank can beat a tall thin one.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark in class if time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *measure once, compute, and you know what a container holds before you fill it*. Homework: estimate the volume of one container at home in litres.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. A floor is 6 m by 5 m. Calculate its area. (mirrors pre-test)
2. How many litres are in 1 m³? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A cylindrical bucket has r = 0.15 m and h = 0.30 m. Find its volume in m³, then in litres. (π = 3.14)
4. A roof is 8 m by 6 m. If 10 mm of rain falls, the water depth is 0.01 m. How many litres land on the roof?
5. Challenge: tank A is r = 1 m, h = 1 m; tank B is r = 0.5 m, h = 4 m. Which holds more? Show working.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A = 6 × 5 = 30 m².
2. 1000 L.
3. V = 3.14 × 0.15² × 0.30 = 3.14 × 0.0225 × 0.30 ≈ 0.0212 m³ ≈ 21 L (a normal bucket!).
4. V = 8 × 6 × 0.01 = 0.48 m³ = 480 L.
5. A: 3.14 × 1² × 1 = 3.14 m³. B: 3.14 × 0.25 × 4 = 3.14 m³. They hold the same — height and width trade off.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A cylindrical water tank has r = 1.2 m and h = 1.8 m. Compute its volume in litres (π = 3.14), then find how many days it supplies a family using 200 L/day.
   **Worked Answer:** V = πr²h = 3.14 × 1.2² × 1.8 = 3.14 × 1.44 × 1.8 ≈ 8.14 m³. In litres: 8.14 × 1000 ≈ 8140 L. Days = 8140 ÷ 200 ≈ 40.7, so about 40 days of supply.
2. **Q:** The family wants the tank to last 60 days at 200 L/day with the same r = 1.2 m. What height must the tank be?
   **Worked Answer:** Needed volume = 60 × 200 = 12 000 L = 12 m³. h = V ÷ (πr²) = 12 ÷ (3.14 × 1.44) = 12 ÷ 4.52 ≈ 2.65 m.
3. **Q:** A school roof is 20 m by 10 m. In one storm, 25 mm of rain falls. The gutters send all of it into a 10 000 L tank. Does the tank fill?
   **Worked Answer:** Volume = 20 × 10 × 0.025 = 5 m³ = 5000 L. Only half the tank fills — 5000 L < 10 000 L. (Bonus discussion: in a real system some water is lost to splash and first-flush, so even less arrives.)

## Safety Notes
- Tape measures snap back fast — rewind slowly and keep the blade away from faces and fingers.
- Do not climb to measure real roofs or stand on tanks; all roof numbers are given data, ground-level only.
- Wipe up any spilled water from the litre demonstration immediately to prevent slips.
- Lift heavy containers with bent knees, two learners per large jerrican.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 017–020 (length, mass, time, graphing) have run; formulas here use those measuring skills.
- Younger learners (10–13): measure and multiply only; teacher handles the π steps on the board. Older learners: full πr²h calculations and the advanced tank-sizing questions.
- A real black plastic tank on the school compound is the best teaching aid — ask the school caretaker for its labelled litres and test it against your calculation.
- String around a cylinder gives circumference directly; r = C ÷ 6.28 is less error-prone than estimating diameter across an open tank mouth.
- CBE assessment note: keep each learner's floor-area and tank-volume workings; this is direct evidence for the measurement and estimation competency.
