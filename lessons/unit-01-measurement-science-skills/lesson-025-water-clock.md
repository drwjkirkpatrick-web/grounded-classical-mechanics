# Lesson 025: Build a Water Clock (Clepsydra)

**Unit:** 1 — Measurement & Scientific Skills  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** water-clock

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a dripping-bottle water clock from local materials.
2. Calibrate the clock by marking the receiver jar against a real clock.
3. Measure the drip rate and observe that it falls as the reservoir empties.
4. Connect the slowing drip to water pressure P = ρgh (preview of Unit 3).

## Materials
- Two clear plastic bottles per group (1.5 L soda bottles; one as reservoir, one as receiver)
- Nail (for piercing the hole) — teacher handles, or a heated nail held with pliers by the teacher only
- Water and a jug
- Marker pens (permanent) for calibration marks
- Clock or phone stopwatch visible to the class
- Sticky tape or a strip of exercise-book paper for the scale
- Basin to catch spills

## Vocabulary
- **clepsydra** (saa ya maji) — "water thief", the ancient water clock
- **calibration** (urekebishaji) — marking a scale using a trusted instrument
- **drip rate** (kimo cha matone) — volume of water passing per minute
- **pressure** (shinikizo) — the push of the water; grows with depth
- **reservoir** (hifadhi ya maji) — the upper bottle holding the supply

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Before clocks reached the village, how do you think people measured short times?
2. A bottle with a small hole full of water drips into a jar. Will it drip at the same speed from full to empty? Guess and give a reason.
3. What would you use to mark "5 minutes" on the jar?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept: shadow of the sun, burning candles, singing known songs, heartbeat, sand in an hourglass, water dripping.
2. Accept any reasoned guess; the truth (it slows as the level falls) is discovered today.
3. Compare against a real clock or phone: when the clock shows 5 minutes have passed, mark the water level.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before the demo.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the picture on the board of an Egyptian clepsydra and the word itself. *Three thousand years ago a court in Egypt timed speeches with dripping water. Today we build the same machine from soda bottles — and we will catch it telling lies.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups build the clock (see Hands-On Build). As soon as a clock drips, groups begin calibration: start the stopwatch and fill the reservoir to the top mark; every 2 minutes mark the level in the receiver. By the end each group has a 10-minute scale and a discovery to report: the later marks are *closer together* — the clock runs slow as it empties.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: the push that drives the drip is water pressure, and pressure depends on depth: P = ρgh with ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 10 N/kg. A full reservoir has a deep column above the hole, so a fast drip; as the level falls, h shrinks, pressure falls, the drip slows. That is why the equal-time marks bunch together — an uncalibrated clepsydra *lies* unless its scale is made by experiment. Confront the misconception that the hole "gets tired" or "blocks up" — the water above the hole is simply shorter.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark in class if time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *every clock needs calibration, and water pressure grows with depth*. Homework: draw your group's receiver with its marks and write one sentence on why the marks are uneven.

## Hands-On Build: Bottle Water Clock
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; collect bottles and pierce holes the day before if possible.*

### Parts
- Clear plastic bottle, 1.5 L — 1 per group — cut the bottom off to make an open-topped reservoir (or leave the cap on and loosen it for air)
- Second clear bottle or straight-sided jar — 1 per group — the receiver
- Nail — 1 — for the drip hole; teacher pierces caps or bottle shoulders for younger classes (safety-critical)
- Water and jug — shared
- Permanent marker — 1 per group
- Paper strip or tape — for the scale on the receiver
- Basin — catches overflow and spills

### Workflow
1. Teacher pierces one small round hole (about 1–2 mm) in the reservoir bottle near its base, or through the cap. Small hole = slow, long-lasting drip.
2. Stand the reservoir above the receiver (reservoir resting on two stones or held by a partner over the jar rim).
3. Fill the reservoir to a marked top line and start the stopwatch at the first drip. **Checkpoint:** drips fall one by one, not a stream — if it streams, the hole is too big; plug with tape and re-pierce.
4. At each 2-minute call from the teacher, mark the water level in the receiver and write the time beside it.
5. **Checkpoint:** after 4 minutes the spacing between the first two marks should be visibly larger than between later marks — if all marks are perfectly even, check the reservoir is not being secretly refilled.
6. Continue to 10 minutes. Final test: cover the marks, drip the clock again, and use only the scale to call out "5 minutes" — check against the stopwatch.

### Common Mistakes
- **Hole too big** — reservoir empties in a minute; start again with a smaller hole or a tape flap over it.
- **No air entry** — a sealed reservoir glugs and stops; leave the top open or the cap loose.
- **Marking the reservoir instead of the receiver** — the scale belongs on the jar that fills.
- **Reservoir held at different heights** — keep the setup still all lesson; moving it changes the head of water above the hole.
- **Reading the meniscus from above** — always read with eyes level with the water surface.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Does a dripping bottle clock drip at the same speed from full to empty? Explain. (mirrors pre-test)
2. How did we mark "5 minutes" on the receiver jar? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Write the pressure formula and say what each symbol means.
4. The water column above our hole fell from 20 cm to 10 cm. By what factor did the pressure at the hole change?
5. Challenge: design one change that would make the clock drip at a constant rate all day.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. No. It drips fastest when full and slows as the level falls, because pressure at the hole depends on the depth of water above it.
2. We ran the clock against a stopwatch and marked the receiver level each time the stopwatch reached a chosen time (calibration).
3. P = ρgh: P pressure (N/m²), ρ density of water (1000 kg/m³), g = 10 N/kg, h depth of water above the hole (m).
4. Pressure is proportional to h, so halving h halves the pressure: factor of 2 smaller (from 2000 N/m² to 1000 N/m²).
5. Keep the water level above the hole constant — e.g., an overflow feeder (a second bottle that tops the reservoir up to an overflow spout), so h never changes.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Using P = ρgh, explain why the drips slow as the reservoir empties, and compute the pressure at the hole when the water is 25 cm deep (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg).
   **Worked Answer:** Pressure at the hole is set by the depth of water above it. As the reservoir empties, h falls, so P falls, so the flow speed and drip rate fall. At h = 0.25 m: P = 1000 × 10 × 0.25 = 2500 N/m² (Pa) above atmospheric pressure.
2. **Q:** Propose a constant-head fix (overflow feeder) and explain the physics of why it keeps time better.
   **Worked Answer:** Feed the reservoir from a supply bottle and cut an overflow spout at a fixed level: any excess water runs out of the spout, so the free surface stays exactly at spout height. Then h above the drip hole is constant, P = ρgh is constant, and the drip rate is constant — equal minutes produce equal rises and the scale is perfectly even. (Mariotte's bottle is the classic version.)
3. **Q:** Our 10-minute clock's marks bunch together near the bottom. If the first 2 minutes filled 60 mL and the last 2 minutes filled 30 mL, estimate how the average drip rate changed, and link it to h.
   **Worked Answer:** Average rate fell from 60/2 = 30 mL/min to 30/2 = 15 mL/min — it halved. Rate depends on pressure, and pressure on h, so the head of water above the hole roughly halved over the run (true flow also depends on hole shape, but the trend is pure P = ρgh).

## Safety Notes
- Nail piercing is done by the teacher, away from learners, with the bottle held in a clamp or against a board — never in the palm of the hand. A nail heated in a jiko flame melts a cleaner hole; handle it with pliers and let it cool before learners touch the bottle.
- Cut bottle edges are sharp — teacher trims with scissors and tapes the rim; check each group before water goes in.
- Water spills make floors slippery: work over basins, mop drips immediately.
- Do not drink from the apparatus; bottles are recycled and water sits in open jars.
- Small groups only around each clock — no crowding over glass jars.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 017–019 (time, mass, density) have run; this is the Unit 1 project build and the first taste of pressure (G10-PHY-1.2), developed fully in Unit 3.
- Younger learners (10–13): build, calibrate and spot the uneven marks — the words "more water above pushes harder" are enough. Older learners: full P = ρgh numbers and the constant-head design question.
- Ask the local soda kiosk for used 1.5 L bottles a week ahead; straight-sided juice bottles make the best receivers because equal volumes rise equally.
- Keep one calibrated clock for Unit 3 — it returns when pressure is studied formally (project slug: water-clock).
- If time runs short, pierce holes before class and run calibration to 6 minutes only; the bunching of marks still shows clearly.
- CBE assessment note: photograph each group's calibrated scale; the build plus the calibration record is evidence for the scientific-investigation competency.
