# Lesson 027: Force: Pushes and Pulls Everywhere

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define a force as a push or a pull.
2. Identify at least ten forces acting in the school compound or homestead.
3. Describe each force by what it does to an object.
4. Sort forces into contact and non-contact groups.

## Materials
- Sufuria with a lid (any cooking pot with a cover works)
- Notebook and pencil per learner
- Chalk for the board list
- Access to the school compound for the force hunt

## Vocabulary
- **force** (nguvu) — a push or a pull on an object
- **push** (kusukuma) — a force that shoves an object away
- **pull** (kuvuta) — a force that drags an object towards you
- **contact force** — a force where things must touch
- **non-contact force** — a force that acts at a distance, like gravity

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. In your own words, what is a force?
2. Name one force you used before coming to school today.
3. Can a force act on something without touching it? Yes or no — guess if unsure.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A push or a pull (accept any wording close to this).
2. Accept any real example: pulling a blanket, pushing a door, lifting a jerrican, kicking a ball.
3. Yes. (Gravity pulls a mango down without touching it; accept the guess either way at pre-test.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before the demo.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold the sufuria in one hand and lift its lid with the other. Ask: *what did my hand just do?* Then drop the lid onto the pot: *what pulled it down?* Board the words "push" and "pull".
3. **Explore (15 min)** — the force hunt. Take the class outside in pairs with notebooks. Each pair lists ten forces they can see or feel in the compound: someone pulling a mkokoteni, wind pushing the mabati roof, a foot kicking a stone, hands opening a door, a hen scratching soil, clothes pulling on a hanging line. Back inside, pairs read their lists; the class builds one master board list of at least fifteen forces, each stated as "X pushes/pulls Y".
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: a force is simply a push or a pull, and we know forces by what they do. Split the board list into two columns: forces where things touch (contact) and forces that act at a distance (non-contact). Gravity is the one non-contact force they already know — the mango falls with nobody touching it. Confront the misconception that only living things or engines can push: the wind pushes, water pushes, a stretched rope pulls.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a force is a push or a pull, and forces are everywhere.* Homework: find three more forces at home and write each as one sentence — "the jembe pulls the soil".

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. In your own words, what is a force? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Name two forces acting in our compound right now. (mirrors pre-test)
3. The wind rattles the mabati sheets at night. Is that a push or a pull, and is it contact or non-contact?
4. A mango falls from the tree. Name the force and say whether anything touched the mango.
5. Challenge: a magnet picks up a pin. Push, pull, or neither — and contact or non-contact?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A push or a pull.
2. Accept any two real forces: wind on the flag, feet pushing the ground, a door being pulled shut.
3. A push (or a push-and-pull rattling); it is a contact force — moving air touches the sheets.
4. Gravity; nothing touched it, so it is a non-contact force.
5. A pull, and non-contact — the magnet attracts the pin across a gap.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Classify each of these as contact or non-contact and justify: (a) a boda boda braking, (b) the Moon held near Earth, (c) a fish pulled by a line, (d) static electricity picking up paper bits.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Contact — the brake blocks squeeze the wheel; surfaces touch. (b) Non-contact — gravity acts across 384,000 km of empty space with nothing between. (c) Contact — the line touches the fish hook; the pull travels along the taut line. (d) Non-contact — the electric force acts across a small air gap without touching.
2. **Q:** A learner says, "Gravity cannot be a force because nobody is pushing or pulling." Defend gravity as a force using the definition from this lesson.
   **Worked Answer:** A force is defined by what it does, not by who does it. Gravity pulls every mass towards Earth — a released stone accelerates downwards, a jerrican of water presses on your head with about 200 N. Since gravity produces a measurable pull (weight = mass × 10 N/kg), it fits the definition of a force even though no hand or rope is involved.

## Safety Notes
- During the compound force hunt, set clear boundaries — no entering the kitchen area with hot jikos, no climbing trees or water tanks.
- Learners must not test forces on each other (no pushing or pulling classmates as a "demo").
- The sufuria lid is metal and can have sharp edges; the teacher handles it, not learners.
- Watch for wasps around eaves and avoid muddy patches where learners may slip.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes no prior physics beyond everyday language; this is the unit opener for formal force ideas.
- Younger learners (10–13): keep the hunt playful and oral; the two-column sort is enough. Older learners: push them to state each force precisely as "A pushes/pulls B" — vague answers like "wind force" should become "wind pushes the flag".
- Kiswahili helps here: many learners already say *nguvu* for effort; connect it to the formal word *force*.
- If the compound is small, send pairs to observe a single busy spot (gate, kitchen, field) rather than roaming.
- CBE assessment note: the pre/post pair measures growth in the "identifying forces" competency; keep both slips for the learner record.
