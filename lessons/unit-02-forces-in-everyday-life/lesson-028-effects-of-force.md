# Lesson 028: What Forces Do: Move, Stop, Turn, Shape

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State the four effects of a force: start motion, stop motion, change direction, change shape.
2. Demonstrate each effect with a ball, clay, a stick and a rubber strip.
3. Match everyday events to the correct effect of force.
4. Explain that one force can produce more than one effect at the same time.

## Materials
- A ball (football or homemade plastic-bag ball)
- Clay or soft mud, a lump per group
- A green (flexible) stick, about one arm long, per group
- Rubber strips cut from an old inner tube
- Chalk and board space for the four-column chart

## Vocabulary
- **effect** (athari) — what a force does to an object
- **motion** (mwendo) — movement from place to place
- **direction** (mwelekeo) — the way something is heading
- **deform** — to change shape, temporarily or permanently
- **elastic** — springing back to the old shape when the force stops

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. List two things a force can do to an object.
2. A goalkeeper catches a fast ball. What did the goalkeeper's hands do to the ball's motion?
3. Can a force change an object's shape? Give one example or say "not sure".

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept any two: move it, stop it, turn it, change its shape, squeeze it, stretch it, bend it.
2. Stopped it (slowed it to rest).
3. Yes — e.g., squeezing mud, bending a stick, stretching rubber. (Accept a guess at pre-test.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before the demos.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — kick the ball gently across the room. Ask: *what did my foot do?* Then trap it under your foot: *and now?* Two different effects from one kind of force. Board the question: *what can forces do?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — four stations, groups rotate every 3–4 minutes. Station 1 (ball): kick, stop, and pass to a partner who redirects it — motion started, stopped, turned. Station 2 (clay): squeeze, flatten, roll — shape changed permanently. Station 3 (green stick): bend it gently and release — shape changes and returns. Station 4 (rubber strip): stretch and release — it snaps back. At each station learners say aloud the sentence frame: "The force of ___ made the ___ ___."
4. **Explain (8 min)** — build the four-column chart on the board: MOVE, STOP, TURN, CHANGE SHAPE. Sort class examples into columns. Highlight that one kick does two effects (starts motion AND turns), and that clay keeps its new shape while the stick and rubber return — plant the word *elastic*. Confront the misconception that "a force always moves something": squeezing clay moves nothing across the room, yet the force is real.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark if time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *forces move, stop, turn and shape.* Homework: watch cooking at home and write which of the four effects happens when ugali is stirred and turned.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Name the four effects a force can have on an object. (mirrors pre-test)
2. A goalkeeper punches a ball away to the side. Which two effects happened? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Kneading dough for chapati: which effect of force is at work, and is the shape change permanent or elastic?
4. A matatu driver turns the steering wheel. Which effect is the driver's force having on the vehicle?
5. Challenge: when you sit on a cushion, it does not move across the room. Is a force still acting? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Start motion, stop motion, change direction, change shape.
2. Changed the ball's direction and changed its speed (slowed/stopped its original motion).
3. Change of shape; permanent — the dough keeps its new form.
4. Change of direction (turning).
5. Yes — your weight pushes on the cushion and its shape changes (it deforms), so a force is acting even though nothing travels.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** When you bend a green stick, it changes shape but its centre does not move anywhere. Where does the work your muscles do end up?
   **Worked Answer:** It is stored as elastic potential energy in the bent fibres of the wood (plus a little heat as fibres rub internally). Release the stick and that stored energy converts to kinetic energy — the stick springs back. This previews the energy unit: work done on an elastic object is not lost; it is stored and recoverable.
2. **Q:** A footballer kicks a 0.4 kg ball and it accelerates from rest to 15 m/s. If the foot is in contact for 0.1 s, estimate the average force, and name which effect(s) of force this is.
   **Worked Answer:** a = Δv/Δt = 15/0.1 = 150 m/s². F = ma = 0.4 × 150 = 60 N. The force both started motion (from rest) and set the direction of travel — two effects at once.
3. **Q:** Explain why a blacksmith hammering hot metal is using the same "change shape" effect as a child squeezing clay, and why heating the metal matters.
   **Worked Answer:** Both apply a force that deforms the material permanently (plastic deformation). Hot metal is softer — its particles slide past each other more easily — so the same hammer force produces a larger shape change per blow than on cold metal.

## Safety Notes
- Kicking balls indoors: clear the space of desks and learners' bags; soft kicks only, or take station 1 outside.
- Green sticks can snap and whip — bend gently, never near faces, and inspect for cracks first.
- Mud and clay work must end with handwashing; keep clay away from the sufuria/kitchen area.
- Rubber strips snap back hard — stretch away from the face and never release one end while a partner holds the other.

## Teacher Notes
- Builds directly on Lesson 027 (forces as pushes/pulls); now learners classify what those forces do.
- Younger learners (10–13): the sentence frame at each station is the core skill — insist on full sentences. Older learners: introduce "deformation" vocabulary and the elastic vs plastic distinction formally.
- The ball can be a tightly stuffed plastic-bag ball — no money needed. Inner-tube rubber comes free from any boda or bicycle fundi.
- Watch for groups turning stations into free play; the rotation timer keeps it sharp.
- CBE assessment note: the station sentences are good evidence for the "communicating science" competency; note learners who use the four terms correctly by post-test.
