# Lesson 029: Measuring Force: The Newton and the Rubber-Strip Meter

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** force-meter

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State that force is measured in newtons (N), and that 1 kg of mass weighs about 10 N on Earth.
2. Build a working force meter from an inner-tube rubber strip, a paperclip pointer and a card scale.
3. Calibrate the meter using water: 1 litre of water ≈ 1 kg ≈ 10 N.
4. Explain how a shop spring balance works on the same principle.

## Materials
- Rubber strips cut from an old inner tube, about 20 cm each (one per pair)
- Large paperclips (two per pair — one as hook, one as pointer)
- Stiff card strips for the scale (from a carton box)
- Marker pens
- A jerrican or plastic bottle plus a measuring jug or 1-litre bottle
- Water
- String to hang the meter

## Vocabulary
- **newton** (N) — the unit of force; 1 N is roughly the weight of a small orange
- **calibrate** — to mark a scale using known standards
- **spring balance** (mizani ya springi) — a force meter using a stretchy element
- **extension** — how much the strip stretches under load

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. What unit do you think force is measured in? (Guess if you do not know.)
2. A full 1-litre bottle of water has a mass of 1 kg. What pulls it down, and how strong do you think that pull is?
3. How does the shopkeeper's hanging scale know how heavy your sukuma is?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Newtons (N) — accept any guess at pre-test; many will say kilograms.
2. Gravity pulls it; the pull is about 10 N (accept any number at pre-test).
3. Something inside stretches or compresses and moves a pointer along a scale (accept sensible descriptions).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before the build.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show a real spring balance if available (or draw one). Hook on a 1-litre bottle of water: it reads about 10 N. Announce: *today we build our own from boda inner tube.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the rubber-strip meter (see Hands-On Build), then calibrate: hang the strip, mark zero with the empty bottle, then mark 10 N with 1 litre of water, 20 N with 2 litres if the strip allows. Teams then measure the weight of a mystery object (a stone in a small bag) and write their reading on the board for comparison.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: force is measured in newtons; gravity pulls 1 kg with about 10 N, so 1 litre of water is a perfect calibration standard. The rubber strip is our "spring": more force → more extension → pointer moves further. Board the calibration idea: known force in, mark out. Confront the misconception that kilograms measure force — kg measures mass; the balance only works because gravity turns mass into weight at 10 N per kg.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *1 kg weighs about 10 N, and a stretchy thing plus a scale measures force.* Homework: find one scale at home or at the market and note its units (kg or N).

## Hands-On Build: Rubber-Strip Force Meter
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; cut strips and cards the day before.*

### Parts
- Inner-tube rubber strip — 1 per pair, ~20 cm — free from a bicycle/boda fundi; avoid cracked, perished rubber (it snaps)
- Paperclips — 2 per pair — one unbent into a hook, one as a pointer
- Stiff card strip — 1 per pair — carton box from any shop
- String — short piece — to hang the strip from a nail or desk edge
- 1-litre bottle and water — the calibration standard
- Marker pen — for scale marks

### Workflow
1. Unbend one paperclip into an S-hook and attach it to the bottom end of the rubber strip.
2. Fix the second paperclip at the same end as a pointer, pointing sideways.
3. Pin or tie the top of the strip so it hangs beside the vertical card.
4. **Checkpoint:** tug the hook gently — the strip should stretch and recover smoothly, and the pointer should sweep along the card.
5. With nothing hanging, mark the pointer position "0 N".
6. Hang 1 litre of water. Mark the new pointer position "10 N".
7. Add a second litre if possible; mark "20 N". Divide the gaps into even 2 N steps.
8. **Final test:** weigh the mystery stone; compare readings across teams — they should agree within a newton or two.

### Common Mistakes
- **Strip tied to the card** — the strip must hang free beside the card, not touch it, or friction spoils readings.
- **Marking 0 with the bottle already on** — zero is with no load (or subtract the empty bottle's weight).
- **Overstretching past recovery** — rubber that stays stretched gives drifting readings; stay within gentle stretch.
- **Reading the pointer at an angle** — eyes level with the pointer every time.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Force is measured in what unit? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is the weight of 1 litre of water, in newtons? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why did we use water to calibrate the meter instead of guessing the marks?
4. Two teams measure the same stone and get 12 N and 19 N. Give one likely reason for the difference.
5. Challenge: your rubber strip gets less stretchy on a cold morning. What happens to your readings?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Newtons (N).
2. About 10 N (mass 1 kg × g = 10 N/kg).
3. Water gives known forces (10 N per litre), so the marks are true, not guessed — that is what calibration means.
4. Accept one: overstretched strip, zero marked differently, reading at an angle, strip touching the card, different strip stiffness.
5. A stiffer strip stretches less for the same force, so the pointer moves less — you would under-read the true force until you recalibrate.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Suppose your strip stretches non-linearly: marks at 0, 10 and 20 N are evenly spaced, but at 30 N the real extension is larger than your even-step scale predicts. You read a stone as 24 N. Describe the error and a fix.
   **Worked Answer:** The even-step assumption makes every reading above 20 N wrong — the true force for that pointer position is higher than 24 N (the strip stretched extra, so the load is more than the scale says). Fix: calibrate with more known points (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 N using half-litre steps) and draw the actual calibration curve, then read forces from the curve instead of assuming even spacing.
2. **Q:** A stone weighs 15 N on your meter. Find its mass in kg, then in grams.
   **Worked Answer:** m = W/g = 15 N ÷ 10 N/kg = 1.5 kg = 1500 g.
3. **Q:** The market spring balance is marked in kilograms, yet it measures a force. Explain how it can be "correct" for trade but wrong physics.
   **Worked Answer:** The balance measures weight (a force) but its scale divides by g ≈ 10 N/kg to display mass. That conversion is fine as long as g stays the same everywhere locally — so for market trade in one place it reads true mass. Physically, though, the instrument responds to force; on the Moon the same sukuma would "weigh" far fewer kilograms on that scale even though its mass is unchanged.

## Safety Notes
- Overstretched rubber snaps and can flick an eye — stretch gently, keep faces away from the line of the strip, and retire cracked strips.
- A 2-litre load dropped on a foot hurts — hang loads low over a soft surface and keep toes clear.
- Spilled water on a mud or cement floor is slippery — mop at once.
- Paperclip hooks have sharp ends once unbent; bend them fully closed so no point faces skin.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 027–028 (what forces are and do). This is the first quantitative lesson of the unit — the 1 kg ↔ 10 N bridge is the key number to land.
- Younger learners (10–13): celebrate a meter that reads "about right"; skip non-linearity. Older learners: do the half-litre multi-point calibration and discuss the curve.
- Inner tubes: any boda/bicycle fundi has punctured tubes free; pick supple black rubber, not the red brittle kind.
- If no spring balance exists to show in Engage, borrow one from a market trader for the day — learners must see a "real" one to connect the build to the world.
- Keep the meters: later lessons (friction pull tests, Lesson 037) reuse them.
- CBE assessment note: the calibrated meter plus the mystery-stone reading is direct evidence for the "measurement and instrumentation" competency.
