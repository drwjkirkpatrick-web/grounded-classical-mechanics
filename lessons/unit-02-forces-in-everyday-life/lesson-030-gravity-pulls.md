# Lesson 030: Gravity: Earth's Pull on Everything

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe gravity as Earth's pull on every object, directed downwards towards Earth's centre.
2. Explain that dropped and thrown objects are both pulled down by gravity.
3. Define weight as the force of gravity on a mass, and use g ≈ 10 N/kg.
4. Give evidence that gravity acts on everything, not just heavy things.

## Materials
- A ripe mango or a stone (for dropping)
- A ball for throwing
- A small stone and a feather or dry leaf (drop comparison)
- Chalk and board
- A safe open drop zone outdoors or a cleared indoor space

## Vocabulary
- **gravity** (graviti / nguvu ya mvutano wa dunia) — Earth's pull on all masses
- **weight** (uzani) — the force of gravity on an object, measured in newtons
- **mass** — the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms
- **g** — gravitational field strength; about 10 N/kg at Earth's surface
- **fall** (kuanguka) — to move downwards under gravity

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Why does a ripe mango fall down from the tree instead of floating up?
2. If you throw a stone straight up, what happens to it, and why?
3. Does gravity pull on light things like a feather, or only heavy things? Guess if unsure.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Earth pulls it down (gravity) — accept any version of "something pulls it down".
2. It slows, stops briefly, and falls back — because gravity keeps pulling it down.
3. Gravity pulls on everything (the feather just falls slowly because of air). Accept guesses at pre-test.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before the demos.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold the mango at arm's length. *What happens if I let go — and how do you know before I do it?* Drop it. Then ask the deeper question: *who touched it to make it move?* Nobody — yet it moved. That invisible pull is today's subject.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — three quick demos the class predicts before each. (a) Drop the mango/stone: straight down, speeding up. (b) Throw the ball up: it rises, slows, stops, returns — gravity never stopped acting, even while it rose. (c) Drop stone and leaf side by side: the stone lands first, but both were pulled down; the leaf is held back by air (preview of Lesson 038). Learners then feel weight: hold a 1-litre bottle at arm's length for 30 seconds — the pull they fight is gravity on 1 kg, about 10 N.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board the key ideas: gravity is a non-contact force pulling every mass towards Earth's centre; "down" means "towards Earth's centre" everywhere on the planet. Introduce weight: the force of gravity on a mass, W = mg with g ≈ 10 N/kg, so 1 kg weighs 10 N, 2 kg weighs 20 N. Confront the misconception that gravity needs air or only acts on heavy things — the stone and leaf were both pulled; the Moon is pulled too, with no air at all.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark if time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *everything is pulled down at 10 N per kilogram.* Homework: find your own mass in kg (clinic card, home scale) and calculate your weight in newtons.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. What force pulls a mango straight down when it lets go of the branch? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A stone thrown straight up comes back down. Explain why. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A jerrican holds 5 kg of water. What is the weight of that water? Show your working.
4. Which is a force: mass or weight? Which is measured in kilograms and which in newtons?
5. Challenge: astronauts say things are "weightless" far from Earth. Can mass ever be zero? Explain the difference.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Gravity — Earth's pull.
2. Gravity pulls down on it the whole time: it slows the rise to a stop, then pulls it back down, faster and faster.
3. W = mg = 5 kg × 10 N/kg = 50 N.
4. Weight is the force (newtons); mass is the amount of matter (kilograms).
5. No — mass is the matter in the object and never vanishes. Weight can become nearly zero where gravity is very weak or when falling freely, but the mass stays the same.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** The Moon is pulled by Earth's gravity, yet it does not fall onto Earth. How can that be?
   **Worked Answer:** The Moon IS falling — but it also has a huge sideways speed (about 1 km/s). Each second gravity pulls it towards Earth while its sideways motion carries it forward; the result is that it keeps missing Earth and circles it. An orbit is continuous falling with enough sideways speed. Seed only: full treatment comes with circular motion later.
2. **Q:** A 45 kg learner stands on Earth. (a) Find her weight. (b) On the Moon g ≈ 1.6 N/kg — find her weight and mass there.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) W = mg = 45 × 10 = 450 N. (b) Moon weight = 45 × 1.6 = 72 N. Her mass is still 45 kg — mass does not change with location; only weight does.
3. **Q:** A learner argues: "A stone falls faster than a leaf, so gravity pulls harder on heavy things — that proves heavy objects are pulled more." Untangle the true and false parts.
   **Worked Answer:** True: gravity DOES pull harder on bigger mass — W = mg, so a 2 kg stone feels twice the pull of a 1 kg stone. False: that is not why the stone lands first. Without air, heavy and light objects accelerate the same (g is the same for all), because the bigger pull on the bigger mass is exactly balanced by its greater inertia. The leaf lags only because air resistance acts on its large light surface.

## Safety Notes
- Drop zone: clear learners away from below and around the drop; only the teacher drops from height, from ground level or a low step — never from roofs or trees.
- Do not drop stones where they can bounce into ankles; soft ground or a sand patch is best.
- Thrown balls: one demonstrator at a time, throw straight up in an open area away from faces and windows.
- Mango juice on floors is slippery — clean splashes immediately.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 027–029; learners already know force, its effects, and the newton.
- Younger learners (10–13): the story version is enough — Earth pulls everything down, and weight is how hard. Older learners: W = mg calculations and the Moon question.
- "Down" towards Earth's centre is worth a globe or ball demonstration: ask where "down" is for someone in Australia — a powerful moment.
- If no feather or dry leaf is available, a flat sheet of paper vs the same sheet crumpled makes the same point (and previews Lesson 038).
- CBE assessment note: post-test Q3 is the CBE computation check — record who can apply W = mg; that skill recurs all year.
