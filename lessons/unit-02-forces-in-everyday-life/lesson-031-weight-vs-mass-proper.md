# Lesson 031: Weight = Mass × g: Getting It Right

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Distinguish mass (kg) from weight (N) in speech and in calculations.
2. Measure mass with a beam balance and weight with a spring balance.
3. Verify experimentally that weight ÷ mass is always about 10 N/kg.
4. Use W = mg to convert between mass and weight.

## Materials
- Spring balance (the rubber-strip meter from Lesson 029 works, or a market spring balance)
- Beam balance or simple lever balance (a balanced stick with two string pans works)
- Stones of known mass (1 kg, 2 kg — or marked water bottles: 1 L = 1 kg)
- Water bottles of various sizes
- Notebook and a results table drawn on the board

## Vocabulary
- **mass** (uzani wa chembe / massi) — amount of matter, in kilograms; never changes with place
- **weight** — force of gravity on that mass, in newtons; changes if g changes
- **beam balance** — compares masses directly against known masses
- **spring balance** — measures the pull (weight) by stretch
- **g** — the constant linking them: about 10 N/kg on Earth

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. What is the difference between mass and weight? (Say as much as you know.)
2. A stone has mass 3 kg. What is its weight on Earth?
3. Which instrument measures mass: a beam balance or a spring balance? Guess if unsure.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Mass is the amount of matter (kg); weight is gravity's pull on it (N). Accept partial answers.
2. W = mg = 3 × 10 = 30 N. (Accept any attempt at pre-test.)
3. Beam balance measures mass; spring balance measures weight (force). Accept guesses.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before the measuring.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the two balances. *One of these would give the same answer on the Moon; the other would not. Which is which?* Take quick votes and park the question for the Explain block.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups measure 4–5 objects (stones, bottles) twice each: mass on the beam balance, then weight on the spring balance. Record both in the class table on the board. As numbers accumulate, ask: *divide weight by mass — what do you notice?* Every row gives about 10. Learners have just measured g for themselves.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: the beam balance compares mass against known masses, so it needs gravity only to press both sides equally — it reads the same anywhere. The spring balance measures the pull directly, so it depends on g. That answers the Engage vote. Board the formula W = mg, g ≈ 10 N/kg, and run two conversions aloud. Confront the language trap: market traders say "weight" in kilograms; in physics class kg is mass and N is weight.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *weight is mass times 10 — two different ideas, two different units.* Homework: convert the masses of three household items (sufuria, jerrican of water, bag of maize) into newtons.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Fill in the gaps: mass is measured in ___ with a ___ balance; weight is measured in ___ with a ___ balance. (mirrors pre-test)
2. A stone has mass 3 kg. What is its weight? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Our class table: object A — mass 2 kg, weight 20 N; object B — mass 5 kg, weight 50 N. What is weight ÷ mass in each row, and what is that number called?
4. A bag of maize weighs 500 N. Find its mass.
5. Challenge: why does a beam balance give the correct mass even on a mountain where g is slightly less?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. kilograms, beam; newtons, spring.
2. W = mg = 3 × 10 = 30 N.
3. 20 ÷ 2 = 10 and 50 ÷ 5 = 10; the number is g, about 10 N/kg.
4. m = W/g = 500 ÷ 10 = 50 kg.
5. Both pans are pulled by the same weaker g, so the object still balances exactly against the same known masses — the comparison cancels g out.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A bathroom scale calibrated in Nairobi reads "60 kg" for a learner. Is that reading mass or force, and what happens if the same scale is carried to a high-altitude station where g is 0.2% lower?
   **Worked Answer:** The scale is a spring device, so it truly measures force (weight = 600 N) and then divides by g to display kilograms. At altitude, the real weight falls by 0.2% (to about 598.8 N), but the scale still divides by the Nairobi g — so it reads about 59.9 kg. The mass never changed; the instrument's assumption about g is what produces the small error. Beam balances have no such error.
2. **Q:** Your class measures a stone: beam balance says 1.8 kg, spring balance says 19 N. Is the spring balance reading consistent with g = 10 N/kg? What might explain it?
   **Worked Answer:** Expected weight = 1.8 × 10 = 18 N. The 19 N reading is 1 N high (about 5%). Likely causes: the spring/rubber has been overstretched, zero error, or reading the scale at an angle. A 5% instrument error is plausible; the physics is fine.
3. **Q:** A trader wants to sell maize flour by weight "fairly anywhere in Kenya". Should the trader use a beam balance or a spring balance? Justify.
   **Worked Answer:** A beam balance. g varies slightly with altitude and latitude, so a spring balance reads slightly different newtons for the same flour in different towns — and its printed kg scale assumes one value of g. A beam balance compares the flour against standard masses, cancelling g entirely, so the same kilograms are sold everywhere.

## Safety Notes
- Spring balances and rubber meters snap if overloaded — check the maximum before hanging heavy stones; hang loads low over soft ground.
- Stones drop on toes: set the rule "hands under, not feet under" when passing stones.
- Homemade beam balances tip over; anchor the pivot and keep fingers out from under falling pans.
- Water bottles used as standards must be sealed — spills make floors slippery.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 029–030 (newtons, g introduced). This lesson makes W = mg routine — drill it kindly.
- Younger learners (10–13): the table and the "always 10!" discovery is the lesson; keep formula work to whole numbers. Older learners: percentage errors and the altitude question.
- Known masses: 1-litre and 2-litre water bottles are perfect (1 L = 1 kg); mark them permanently for the store cupboard.
- No beam balance? Balance a metre stick on a pencil pivot, hang known and unknown masses each side, and slide to balance — good enough for 10% accuracy.
- CBE assessment note: post-test Q1 and Q2 are the growth evidence; Q3 checks the experimental-verification competency.
