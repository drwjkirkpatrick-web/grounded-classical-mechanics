# Lesson 032: Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State that a force is a push or a pull measured in newtons.
2. Decide whether forces on an object are balanced or unbalanced.
3. Predict the motion change caused by a net force, including its direction.
4. Explain why a stationary object can still have large forces acting on it.

## Materials
- Sisal rope, 4–6 m (market; any strong rope works)
- Chalk for a centre line on the ground
- Spring balance or the rubber-strip force meter from Lesson 029 (optional, for reading pulls)
- An empty debe or jerrican for the "unmovable" demo

## Vocabulary
- **balanced forces** (nguvu zinazolingana) — equal and opposite forces; no change in motion
- **unbalanced forces** — forces that do not cancel; motion must change
- **net force** (nguvu jumla) — the single force left after all pushes and pulls are combined
- **stationary** (imetulia) — not moving
- **tug-of-war** (kuvuta kamba) — the pulling game used to test forces

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Two teams pull a rope in opposite directions and the rope does not move. What can you say about the two pulls?
2. A 10 N push to the right meets a 6 N push to the left on a box. Which way does the box start to move?
3. A book rests on a desk without moving. Are any forces acting on it? Guess if unsure.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The pulls are equal (balanced) — accept "they are the same strength".
2. To the right — the bigger push wins. (Accept any directional answer with a reason.)
3. Yes: weight pulls it down and the desk pushes it up. Accept any sensible guess.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before going outside.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — mark a chalk line on the ground. Ask: *when two boda riders push one stalled motorbike from opposite sides equally hard, what happens?* Learners argue; park the answers for later.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — run three tug-of-war trials with the sisal rope across the chalk line. (a) Matched teams: the rope barely moves — freeze the game and ask what each side is doing. (b) Add two learners to one side: the rope accelerates toward the stronger team. (c) Teacher holds the middle with the spring balance while two learners pull equally: the rope is still, yet the meter reads a large force — a stationary thing can carry big forces. Finish by pushing the empty debe: one learner pushes, it accelerates; two push opposite equally, it stays.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board work: draw arrows for each trial. Balanced forces → net force = 0 → motion does not change (a still thing stays still, a moving thing keeps its speed). Unbalanced forces → net force ≠ 0 → motion changes in the direction of the bigger force. Confront the misconception head-on: "no motion means no forces" is wrong — the spring balance proved a still rope can be stretched by huge pulls.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark against the key.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *watch the net force, not the single forces.* Homework: find one balanced-forces situation at home (clothesline, hanging pot) and describe the two forces in one sentence.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Two teams pull a rope in opposite directions and the rope does not move. What can you say about the two pulls? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A 10 N push to the right meets a 6 N push to the left on a box. Which way does the box move, and how big is the net force? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A hanging jerrican is perfectly still. Name the two forces on it and say whether they are balanced.
4. During the spring-balance tug, the meter read 80 N but the rope did not move. Explain how that is possible.
5. Challenge: a mkokoteni cart rolls at steady speed on level ground. Is the net force on it zero or not? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. They are equal in size and opposite in direction — balanced.
2. Net force = 10 − 6 = 4 N to the right, so the box accelerates to the right.
3. Weight (gravity) pulls down; the rope's tension pulls up. They are balanced because the jerrican does not move.
4. Both learners pull hard — 80 N each way — but the forces cancel, so the rope's motion does not change even though it is stretched.
5. Zero. Steady speed in a straight line means no motion change, so push and friction must be balanced.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** In a market dispute, one trader pulls a crate left with 400 N while another pulls right with 350 N. Find the net force, its direction, and describe the motion change if the crate starts from rest.
   **Worked Answer:** Net force = 400 − 350 = 50 N to the left. An unbalanced force on a resting crate accelerates it leftwards; its speed toward the left increases every second while the 50 N net force lasts (a = F/m once the crate's mass is known).
2. **Q:** Two teams each pull with 700 N in a tug-of-war and the rope is stationary. What is the tension in the rope, and what is the net force on the rope?
   **Worked Answer:** Net force = 700 − 700 = 0 N (balanced). But the tension is 700 N, not zero and not 1400 N — each end pulls with 700 N and the rope transmits that pull. This is why a still rope can still snap.
3. **Q:** A 50 kg mkokoteni accelerates at 0.4 m/s² on level ground. The pusher applies 60 N. What frictional force acts against him?
   **Worked Answer:** Net force needed: F = ma = 50 × 0.4 = 20 N forward. Push − friction = net → friction = 60 − 20 = 40 N opposing the motion.

## Safety Notes
- Tug-of-war falls: clear stones and holes from the pulling area; teams stand astride, not wrapped in the rope — no winding rope around hands or wrists (crush injury if it whips).
- A snapping sisal rope lashes back; inspect for frayed sections before pulling hard and keep faces away from the line.
- Stop the game the instant the chalk line is crossed to avoid pile-ups.
- When pushing the debe, push low on the body so it slides rather than tips onto toes.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 027–029 (forces, effects, measuring force) have run; this lesson turns "forces change motion" into the balanced/unbalanced rule that the whole unit reuses.
- Younger learners (10–13): keep it qualitative — same size vs different sizes. Older learners: do all arithmetic in newtons and introduce F = ma in the wrap.
- If the class is too big for one rope, run two parallel tugs with a referee each; rotate the spring-balance demo so everyone sees the needle.
- A jua kali fundi or hardware shop can supply a cheap spring scale; until then, the rubber-strip meter from Lesson 029 is fine.
- CBE assessment note: record pre/post growth per learner; the tug observations count toward the "scientific investigation" competency evidence.
