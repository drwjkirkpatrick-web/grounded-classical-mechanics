# Lesson 033: Drawing Force Diagrams

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.5  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Represent a force with an arrow whose direction and length show the force's direction and size.
2. Draw labelled force diagrams for a book on a desk, a hanging jerrican, and a leaning ladder.
3. Use a diagram to decide whether forces balance and predict motion.
4. Read a simple diagram and name each force in words.

## Materials
- Chalk and a board (or large paper)
- A stack of exercise books
- A jerrican with string for hanging
- A wall or post for the leaning-ladder picture
- Rulers or straight sticks for neat arrows

## Vocabulary
- **force diagram** (mchoro wa nguvu) — a sketch showing forces as arrows on an object
- **arrow** (mshale) — shows direction; its length shows size
- **label** — the name of each force: weight, push, tension, friction
- **component** — the part of a slanted force acting in one chosen direction (for older learners)
- **free body** — the single object we draw, ignoring everything else

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Draw anything you think could represent "a 5 N push to the left".
2. A book sits on a desk. List the forces you think act on it.
3. Why might a picture with arrows be more useful than a sentence about forces? Guess if unsure.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Any arrow pointing left, ideally with "5 N" written. Accept all attempts.
2. Weight down; desk pushing up. Accept partial answers.
3. It shows direction and size at a glance and makes balancing easy to check. Accept any sensible idea.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected; books stacked ready.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hang the jerrican from a string so everyone sees it. Ask: *if I draw this situation, what must my picture show so someone in another school understands it without seeing it?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — three stations, teacher-led. (a) Book on desk: learners push it gently, then draw it — teacher models two arrows: weight down (long), desk push up (equally long), plus a small push arrow while sliding. (b) Hanging jerrican: weight arrow down, tension arrow up the string; try two strings sharing the load and draw three arrows. (c) Chalk sketch of a ladder leaning on a wall: weight down at the middle, wall push horizontal at the top, ground push (up and along) at the foot. Learners copy each diagram and annotate it.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate the rules on the board: draw only the object; every force gets one arrow; arrow starts where the force acts; length ≈ size; label everything. Confront the misconception: "a moving book keeps a 'motion force' arrow" — once the hand leaves, no push arrow remains; friction slows it.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *an arrow is a force you can see.* Homework: draw the force diagram of a pot hanging from a kitchen hook.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Draw a 5 N push to the left and a 10 N push to the right on the same box. (mirrors pre-test)
2. A book sits on a desk. Draw its force diagram with labels. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A jerrican hangs from one string. Draw and label the forces, and say if they balance.
4. A learner draws a "speed force" arrow in the direction a thrown stone is flying. Explain the mistake.
5. Challenge: two strings share a hanging jerrican equally. If the jerrican weighs 40 N, how big is each tension?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Left arrow half the length of the right arrow, labelled 5 N and 10 N.
2. Weight arrow down from the book's middle; desk-support arrow up, same length; both labelled.
3. Weight 40 N (or W) down, tension up the string; they balance because the jerrican is still.
4. No hand touches the stone in flight, so there is no push force; only weight (and a little air drag) act. Motion is not a force.
5. The two tensions add to hold 40 N, so each is 20 N.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A hanging jerrican is pulled sideways so its rope makes 20° with the vertical. Draw the diagram and explain which part (component) of the tension carries the weight.
   **Worked Answer:** Arrows: weight straight down, tension along the rope (20° off vertical). Only the vertical part of the tension — T cos 20° — fights gravity, so at balance T cos 20° = W. The horizontal part, T sin 20°, is what pulled the jerrican sideways. Because cos 20° ≈ 0.94 < 1, the tension T must be slightly bigger than the weight itself.
2. **Q:** A box is pushed along a floor with 30 N and friction is 10 N. Draw the full diagram (four arrows) and state the net force.
   **Worked Answer:** Weight down and floor-support up (equal, cancel); 30 N push forward; 10 N friction backward. Net force = 30 − 10 = 20 N forward, so the box accelerates forward.
3. **Q:** Using the ladder diagram, explain why the foot of a leaning ladder is more likely to slip on a polished floor than on rough ground.
   **Worked Answer:** The wall pushes the top of the ladder horizontally; the ground must push back horizontally at the foot to balance it, and that horizontal push is friction. On polished concrete, friction is small, so the needed sideways push is missing and the foot slides outward.

## Safety Notes
- Hanging jerrican: use little water (2–3 L is enough) and check knots — a falling jerrican injures toes.
- Do not actually climb or stress a real ladder for the demo; a chalk picture or a short plank leaning safely is enough.
- Keep the swinging jerrican clear of faces when demonstrating the sideways pull.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 032 (balanced forces); diagrams are the tool the rest of the unit (moments, friction lab, normal force) will lean on.
- Younger learners (10–13): insist on two rules only — arrows show direction, longer arrow = bigger force. Older learners: introduce vertical/horizontal components informally with the slanted rope.
- Chalk on the veranda floor works if the board is crowded; learners enjoy critiquing each other's diagrams — build in a gallery walk if time allows.
- Any fundi workshop wall shows real leaning poles; photograph or sketch one for next lesson's compression work.
- CBE assessment note: collect the post-test diagrams — they are direct evidence of the "communication in science" competency.
