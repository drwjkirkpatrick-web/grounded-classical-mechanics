# Lesson 034: Friction I: The Force That Opposes Sliding

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe friction as a force that opposes sliding between two touching surfaces.
2. State the direction of friction: always against the sliding (or attempted sliding).
3. Compare friction on different surfaces (smooth floor vs grass) through a fair test.
4. Give everyday examples where friction slows things down.

## Materials
- Every learner's own shoes (one on, one off if the floor is clean)
- A smooth floor patch (veranda or classroom cement)
- A grass patch outside
- An old sandal to drag on a string
- Chalk to mark start lines

## Vocabulary
- **friction** (msuguano) — force that resists sliding between surfaces
- **surface** (uso) — the touching face of a material
- **rough** (kukwaruza) — having bumps and teeth that catch
- **smooth** (laini) — even, with few bumps
- **oppose** (kupinga) — act against the direction of motion

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Rub your hands together fast for ten seconds. What do you feel, and what do you think caused it?
2. A sliding stone stops on its own on the floor. What makes it stop?
3. Is it easier to drag a sandal across cement or across grass? Predict and say why.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Warmth/heat, from rubbing. Accept "friction" if offered but do not require the word yet.
2. Friction (accept "the floor rubs it" or "something slows it").
3. Cement — grass grips more. Accept any prediction with a reason.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; shoes kept on until the demo.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — whole class rubs palms hard for ten seconds. Hands are hot: where did that heat come from? Board the word *msuguano* — friction.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — three quick investigations. (a) Hand slide: press a palm lightly then firmly on the desk and drag — firm pressing gives more grip. (b) Shoe drag: one learner drags a foot (shoe on) across smooth cement, then across grass, same effort — grass wins the grip contest. (c) Sandal on a string: drag the old sandal along a chalk line on cement, then on grass; learners compare the pull needed by feel and describe which way friction points (against the drag, every time). Ask repeatedly: *which way does friction act?*
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: friction acts between touching surfaces, against sliding or attempted sliding; rougher surfaces and harder pressing give more friction. Introduce the micro-picture: even "smooth" cement has hills and valleys that catch like tiny jembe teeth. Confront the misconception: friction is not "stickiness" or glue — it acts even between dry dusty surfaces.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *friction always fights the slide.* Homework: find two places at home where friction slows something (door hinge, drawer, brake of a boda) and name the two surfaces.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. What makes a sliding stone stop on a bare floor? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Is it easier to drag a sandal across cement or across grass? Explain using the word friction. (mirrors pre-test)
3. You drag a sack of maize to the right. Which way does friction act on the sack?
4. Give two changes that increase friction between a shoe and the ground.
5. Challenge: a crate is at rest and you push it gently, but it does not move. Is friction acting? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Friction between the stone and the floor opposes its sliding until it stops.
2. Cement. Grass is rougher and grips the sole more, so friction on grass is greater; cement gives less friction and an easier drag.
3. To the left — friction always opposes the sliding direction.
4. Press down harder (more weight) and use a rougher surface or deeper tread.
5. Yes. Friction balances the small push (that is why the crate stays put) — it acts against the *attempted* slide.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Friction is said to come from microscopic welds where surface bumps touch and cold-weld together. Use this idea to explain why polishing a surface reduces friction only up to a point.
   **Worked Answer:** Polishing removes big bumps, so fewer weld points form and friction falls. But the flatter the surfaces get, the more real contact area there is between them, so more microscopic weld sites form over the larger intimate area. Beyond a certain polish the two effects balance, and with extremely smooth clean surfaces (like two glass plates) friction can even rise again.
2. **Q:** A 12 kg box needs a 36 N horizontal pull to keep sliding at steady speed. What is the friction force, and what does "steady speed" tell you?
   **Worked Answer:** Steady speed means balanced forces, so friction = pull = 36 N opposing motion. (The box's weight is 12 × 10 = 120 N, so friction here is 36/120 = 0.3 of the weight — a preview of the coefficient of friction.)
3. **Q:** Rubbing hands produces heat. Where did that thermal energy come from, in force-and-motion terms?
   **Worked Answer:** Muscles do work to slide the hands against friction; friction converts that ordered kinetic energy into disordered thermal energy at the skin. No energy is created — the effort of the arms becomes heat (and a tiny amount of sound).

## Safety Notes
- Palm-rubbing is brief — stop anyone rubbing to soreness; check no learner has skin conditions aggravated by rubbing.
- Shoe-drag area must be free of stones, glass and thorns, especially on the grass patch — walk it first.
- Drag sandals and sacks along the ground, never toward anyone's feet; keep the chalk lane one-way to avoid collisions.
- Wash hands after handling dusty ground before eating.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 032 (balanced forces) — the idea that a still pushed crate has friction balancing the push is the key stretch moment.
- Younger learners (10–13): keep everything by feel and direction words. Older learners: quantify with the spring balance if available and connect to Lesson 037's lab.
- Grass patch: school field edge works; in dry season, dusty bare soil is a good "medium" surface between cement and grass.
- This is the first of three friction lessons (035 helpful, 036 reducing) — do not steal their examples today; keep the focus on "friction opposes sliding".
- CBE assessment note: the surface-comparison drag is a fair-test conversation — note learners who control the "same sandal, same load" variables.
