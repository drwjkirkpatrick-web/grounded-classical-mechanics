# Lesson 038: Air Resistance: The Invisible Brake

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** parachute-mk0

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe air resistance (drag) as a force opposing motion through air.
2. Show that a larger surface catches more drag (flat vs crumpled paper).
3. Build a small plastic-bag parachute that slows a falling stone.
4. State that drag grows with speed, previewing terminal velocity.

## Materials
- Paper sheets (two per pair — scrap paper is fine)
- Thin plastic bags (market carrier bags, hole-free)
- String or tough thread, 4 pieces of ~30 cm per parachute
- Small stones (50–100 g) as payload, one per pair
- Cellotape
- A raised drop point (veranda edge, desk on the ground) and a tape measure

## Vocabulary
- **air resistance / drag** (upinzani wa hewa) — force opposing motion through air
- **parachute** (mwavuli wa kuruka) — a canopy that catches air to slow a fall
- **streamlined** — shaped to slip through air with little drag
- **terminal velocity** — the steady fall speed when drag balances weight
- **canopy** — the catching surface of the parachute

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Two identical paper sheets drop from the same height — one flat, one crumpled into a ball. Predict which lands first and why.
2. What is a parachute for?
3. Does air push on things that are not moving? How do you know?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Crumpled lands first — flat catches more air. Accept any prediction with a reason.
2. To slow a fall (by catching air). Accept simple answers.
3. Yes — wind pushes us; still air presses all around but evenly. Accept honest reasoning.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; paper pairs distributed.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the classic drop: flat sheet vs crumpled ball from shoulder height. The ball thumps down while the sheet floats and sails. *Same paper, same weight — what changed?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — (a) Pairs repeat the drop three times and describe the flat sheet's wobble — drag flipping it. (b) Build the parachute (see Hands-On Build). (c) Drop the stone alone vs stone-with-parachute from the same mark; partners time both with a shared count ("one-thousand, two-thousand…") and compare.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: moving through air means shoving air aside; the air pushes back — that push is drag. Bigger catching area → more drag; faster motion → more drag. Falling objects speed up until drag grows to match weight, then fall steady (terminal velocity — name it, do not derive it). Confront the misconception: "heavier always falls faster" — same-shape stones of different weight land together; it is area vs weight that decides.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *air is thin, but fast motion feels it.* Homework: watch a falling leaf or seed at home and write one sentence on how its shape uses drag.

## Hands-On Build: Plastic-Bag Parachute Mk0
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; pre-cut strings the day before.*

### Parts
- Plastic bag — 1 per pair — thin market carrier, no holes; cut to a square canopy ~40 × 40 cm
- String — 4 × 30 cm per pair — thread shreds; use thin twine or fishing line
- Stone payload — 50–100 g — tie-ready, no sharp points that cut string
- Cellotape — small squares — to anchor strings to canopy corners
- Scissors — shared pairs — supervise

### Workflow
1. Cut the bag into one flat square canopy.
2. Tape one string to each corner, all four equal length. **Checkpoint:** lift the canopy by the four string ends — it should hang as a level square, not a tilted kite; re-tape offenders.
3. Tie the four free ends together, then tie the bundle to the stone.
4. Hold the canopy high, spread it open, and release — do not throw. **Checkpoint:** a good chute blossoms open in its own diameter of fall; if it streams like a rope, the strings are tangled or unequal.
5. Timed trial: drop the bare stone and the chuted stone side by side from the same mark; record both times.

### Common Mistakes
- **Unequal strings** — canopy tilts, spills air, and dives; re-measure all four.
- **Throwing upward** — ruins the comparison; release from rest.
- **Holes in the canopy** — air escapes through instead of building pressure beneath; patch or replace.
- **Too heavy a stone** — beyond ~100 g the chute just delays the thump; keep payloads modest.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Flat paper vs crumpled paper, same sheet, same height — which lands first and why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is a parachute for, and how does it work? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name the force that slows a falling parachute and state its direction.
4. Our chuted stone took 3 counts, the bare stone 1 count, from the same height. What does that show?
5. Challenge: a falling drop of rain keeps speeding up, then stops speeding up and falls steadily. What two forces have balanced?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Crumpled. The flat sheet presents a large area to the air, so drag slows it; the ball presents a small area.
2. To slow a fall; its big canopy catches air so drag grows large enough to nearly balance the payload's weight.
3. Air resistance (drag), acting upward — opposite the fall.
4. The parachute's drag greatly reduced the falling speed — same height, three times the duration.
5. Weight (down) and drag (up). When equal, net force is zero, so speed stays constant — terminal velocity.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Why does a falling raindrop not accelerate forever? Explain terminal velocity using forces.
   **Worked Answer:** Weight pulls the drop down with a constant force, while drag grows with speed. Early in the fall weight > drag, so the drop accelerates. As speed rises, drag rises until drag = weight; the net force becomes zero and the drop stops accelerating, continuing at one steady speed — its terminal velocity. Larger or less-streamlined drops balance sooner, at lower speeds.
2. **Q:** Our 80 g stone reached a steady fall under the parachute. Estimate the drag force during that steady phase. (g = 10 N/kg)
   **Worked Answer:** Steady fall ⇒ drag balances weight. W = mg = 0.080 kg × 10 N/kg = 0.8 N. So drag ≈ 0.8 N upward — small, but that is the weight of the stone itself, which is exactly the point.
3. **Q:** A skydiver "spreads eagle" to slow down and tucks to speed up, all at the same weight. Explain with the two drag factors from today's lesson.
   **Worked Answer:** Drag depends on catching area and speed. Spread-eagle maximises area, so drag matches weight at a lower speed (lower terminal velocity); tucking minimises area, so the diver must fall faster before drag grows to balance weight (higher terminal velocity). Weight never changed — only shape.

## Safety Notes
- Scissors: pass handle-first; cut on desks, not palms; count pairs out and back in.
- Drop zone: mark a clear landing area and a "drop!" call before each release — falling stones and heads below do not mix; no drops from high places beyond the veranda.
- Plastic bags are suffocation hazards for small children — collect every scrap after class.
- Stones: small and smooth; anyone flinging a stone sits out the rest of the build.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 032 (balanced forces) and gravity lessons 030–031; drag as "the third everyday resistance force" before water resistance (039).
- Project note: this parachute is **Mk0** — keep the canopies; a later project lesson refines design and measures descent rate properly.
- Younger learners (10–13): the drop comparisons carry everything; insist on "drag pushes up". Older learners: terminal-velocity reasoning and the 0.8 N estimate.
- Thin "butchery" bags blossom best; heavy duty bags fly like stones. Collect bags from the market a day ahead and check for holes against the light.
- CBE assessment note: the timed side-by-side trial is a fair-test conversation — record which learners kept height and release style identical.
