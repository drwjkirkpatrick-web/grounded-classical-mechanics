# Lesson 039: Water Resistance and Streamlining

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Feel and describe water resistance as drag in a liquid, much stronger than air drag.
2. Show that shape matters: a streamlined shape meets less water drag than a blunt one.
3. Mould clay hulls and compare their drag with a spring-balance pull test.
4. Connect streamlining to fish, canoes and boat hulls on Lake Victoria.

## Materials
- Basins or wide buckets of water (one per group; a trough works)
- Clay or stiff mud (kisamvu/folding clay from a riverbank or craft shop)
- String and the spring balance from Lesson 029
- A stone or small weight to sink each clay hull level
- Towels/rags and a defined splash zone
- Pictures or drawings: tilapia, dugout canoe, box, wedge

## Vocabulary
- **water resistance** (upinzani wa maji) — drag force opposing motion through water
- **streamlined** (umbo laini) — tapered shape that parts water and closes behind cleanly
- **hull** — the body of a boat that displaces water
- **blunt** — flat-fronted shape that shoves water and drags
- **eddy** — swirling water left behind a blunt shape; wasted energy

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Sweep your hand flat through water, then edge-on. Predict which is harder and guess why.
2. Why are fish shaped the way they are?
3. Is water drag bigger or smaller than air drag at the same speed? Guess.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Flat is harder — bigger face pushes more water. Accept any prediction.
2. Their shape slips through water easily. Accept simple answers.
3. Bigger — water is much thicker/denser than air. Accept guesses.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; basins filled before class.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — teacher sweeps a hand through a basin flat-palm, then edge-on, learners watch the splash and hear the effort. *Same hand, same speed — why did one way fight me?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — (a) Every learner tries the flat vs edge sweep (hand just under the surface) and describes it. (b) Build and drag-test hulls (see Hands-On Build): each group moulds one blunt block and one fish-shaped hull of the same clay mass, then pulls each across the basin on a string with the spring balance, reading the steady force. Board the results.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: water must be pushed aside ahead and must fill in behind; blunt shapes shove a wall of water and leave dragging eddies, streamlined shapes part it gently and let it close cleanly. Water is about 800 times denser than air (1000 vs about 1.2 kg/m³), so drag at the same speed is far bigger — that is why wading at the beach exhausts you. Confront the misconception: "drag depends only on speed" — shape decided the result at equal speed today.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *in water, shape is everything.* Homework: look at a canoe, boat or fish and sketch its outline with one sentence on how it reduces drag.

## Hands-On Build: Clay Hull Drag Test
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; soften clay and fill basins the day before.*

### Parts
- Clay — two equal lumps per group (~200 g each) — riverbank clay works; knead out stones and grass
- Basin of water — 1 per group — fill to two-thirds so hulls float clear of the bottom
- String — ~50 cm per hull — thin and light
- Spring balance — shared — zero it before pulls
- Small stone — to ballast hulls so they sit level, same stone in both

### Workflow
1. Weigh or balance the two clay lumps so they match exactly; mould one into a blunt brick and one into a tapered fish/canoe shape with the same volume.
2. Float each hull; add the small stone inside if one lists or swamps. **Checkpoint:** each hull floats level with the same waterline — a swamped hull measures sinkage, not drag.
3. Tie the string to the hull's nose and lay the string along the pull line.
4. Pull the blunt hull the length of the basin at steady speed; partner reads the steady force. Repeat three times; record the middle value.
5. Repeat identically for the streamlined hull — same speed as best you can. **Checkpoint:** watch the wake: the blunt hull drags a frothy trail, the streamlined one leaves a clean V — the wake IS the wasted energy.
6. Report both forces to the board table.

### Common Mistakes
- **Different clay masses** — heavier hull sits deeper and drags more; match lumps before shaping.
- **Pulling too fast** — splashing wastes energy and scatters readings; slow steady pulls.
- **Pointy-bottom "fish"** — a hull must float; flatten the keel slightly.
- **Reading during the start splash** — read only during steady motion, as in Lesson 037.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Which is harder to sweep through water: flat palm or edge-on? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why are fish shaped like fish? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Which of our two hulls needed the bigger pull, and what did its wake look like?
4. Give one reason water drag exceeds air drag at the same speed.
5. Challenge: a dugout canoe has a pointed bow AND a tapered stern. Why taper the back too?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Flat palm — it presents a big face that must shove water aside; edge-on presents a thin face.
2. Streamlined: pointed head parts the water, tapered tail lets it close behind with little eddy, so less drag for the same effort.
3. The blunt brick; it dragged a frothy eddying wake while the fish hull left a clean V.
4. Water is far denser than air (1000 kg/m³ vs ~1.2 kg/m³), so every metre moved shoves aside far more mass of fluid.
5. Water must also fill in cleanly behind the canoe; a cut-off stern leaves dragging eddies that suck the boat backwards, so tapering the stern closes the flow smoothly.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Explain in particle terms why water drag exceeds air drag at the same speed.
   **Worked Answer:** Drag comes from colliding with fluid particles and flinging them aside. Water has about 1000 kg in every cubic metre versus about 1.2 kg for air — roughly 800 times more mass per volume, and its particles are packed touching, so they cannot dodge. At the same speed you sweep aside ~800 times more mass per second in water, so the resisting force is enormously larger — which is why walking waist-deep in water is a workout.
2. **Q:** Our group measured 2.4 N for the blunt hull and 0.9 N for the streamlined hull at the same speed. By what factor did streamlining cut the drag, and where had the blunt hull's extra effort gone?
   **Worked Answer:** Factor = 2.4 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 2.7 times less drag. The extra 1.5 N of pull was being spent accelerating water forward and sideways and spinning it into the eddying wake — ordered pull energy became churned, slightly warmed water.
3. **Q:** A fisherman paddles his dugout at 1 m/s against 30 N of drag. What power does he supply? (P = F × v)
   **Worked Answer:** P = F × v = 30 N × 1 m/s = 30 W. For comparison, that is like lifting a 3 kg jerrican 1 metre every second — sustained for hours, which is why streamlining matters to him.

## Safety Notes
- Water and floors: define the splash zone, keep basins on the ground or low benches, and mop spills immediately — wet cement is a slip trap.
- No head-dunking games; this is a pull test, not a swimming lesson.
- Clay may contain sharp grit — knead and check before learners handle; learners with hand cuts wear a bag over the hand or skip moulding.
- Empty basins at the end onto bare soil, not paths; carry basins with two hands.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 038 (air drag); today the same drag idea moves into a denser fluid, and streamlining becomes explicit.
- Younger learners (10–13): hand sweeps and the wake observation are the lesson. Older learners: factor calculations, the power estimate, and particle-level density reasoning.
- If clay is scarce, bar soap carves beautifully into hulls and floats — a good substitute; candle wax also works.
- A Lake Victoria fisherman or boatyard visit photo set makes the hull-shape discussion vivid; dugout canoes show exactly the tapered stern from Q5.
- CBE assessment note: the same-mass, same-speed hull comparison is fair-test evidence; record which groups controlled it properly.
