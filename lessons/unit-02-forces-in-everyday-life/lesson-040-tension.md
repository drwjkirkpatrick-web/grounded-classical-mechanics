# Lesson 040: Tension: Forces in Ropes and Strings

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define tension as the pulling force carried along a rope or string.
2. Measure tension with a force meter placed anywhere in a rope.
3. Explain why a rope pulled by 50 N at each end carries 50 N, not 100 N.
4. Relate knots, clothesline sag and fraying to how ropes carry tension.

## Materials
- Sisal rope, 3–4 m, plus a short offcut for knot work
- Spring balance / force meter (the calibrated rubber-strip meter works)
- A clothesline (or rope strung between two posts) with a few cloths
- A strong wall hook, post or tree for anchoring pulls
- Chalk for the board diagram

## Vocabulary
- **tension** (mvutano) — the pulling force transmitted along a stretched rope
- **rope** (kamba) — fibres twisted together, strong in pulling, useless in pushing
- **knot** (fundiko) — a bend that jams rope on itself; weakens the rope there
- **sag** — the dip of a loaded line pulled sideways by weight
- **anchor** — the fixed point a rope pulls against

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Two learners pull a rope in opposite directions, each with 50 N. What do you think the tension in the rope is: 0 N, 50 N or 100 N?
2. Can a rope push something? Explain your answer.
3. Why does a loaded clothesline dip in the middle?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept all three answers — this is the classic misconception and the lesson will resolve it (the truth: 50 N).
2. No — it buckles; ropes only pull. Accept any honest attempt.
3. The weight pulls the middle down. Accept simple answers.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; rope and meter staged.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the rope: *I will ask two of you to pull this as hard as you can — and the meter will tell us a surprising number.* Run the pre-test vote on Q1 publicly; tally the three camps on the board.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — (a) Anchor pull: one learner pulls the rope with the meter against the wall anchor at 50 N; read the meter. (b) Replace the wall with a second learner pulling 50 N the other way — the meter still reads 50 N, not 100 N. Discuss hard: the wall was pulling back all along; the second learner just replaced it. (c) Move the meter to different positions along the rope — same reading everywhere: tension is the same all along a light rope. (d) Clothesline: hang one cloth, watch the sag; tighten the line, less sag; sketch the angled tensions. (e) Knot test: tie an overhand knot in the offcut and pull — note the rope kinks and is weakened there.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: tension is one force value carried along the rope; each end feels that same value pulling on it. A rope only pulls. Sag happens because an angled tension needs a vertical part to hold the weight, and a perfectly straight line has no vertical part — so every real line must sag a little. Confront the 100 N camp directly with the wall-anchor comparison.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a rope is a messenger that only delivers pulls.* Homework: find a rope under tension at home (clothesline, tethered goat, well bucket) and name what pulls each end.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Two learners pull a rope in opposite directions with 50 N each. The tension in the rope is…? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Can a rope push? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. The meter reads the same anywhere in the rope. State that as a rule about tension.
4. Why can a clothesline never be pulled perfectly straight once a cloth hangs on it?
5. Challenge: why does a knot weaken a rope?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 50 N. Each end pulls with 50 N; tension is the pull at one end, not the sum. (The wall demo proved it.)
2. No. It buckles — only a stretched rope transmits a force, and stretch means pulling.
3. Tension is the same at every point along a (light) rope.
4. The cloth's weight needs an upward part of the tension; only an angled (sagged) line gives the tension a vertical part. A straight line's tension is purely horizontal and holds nothing up.
5. The knot bends fibres sharply; the outside fibres carry most of the pull and snap first, so the rope fails at the knot well below its straight strength.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Settle the classic argument: a rope in a tug-of-war has 50 N pulls on both ends. Is the tension 50 N or 100 N? Give the decisive explanation.
   **Worked Answer:** 50 N. Cut the rope anywhere and hold the cut: each hand needs 50 N to keep things unchanged — that 50 N is what the rope was carrying. The two 50 N pulls are not added because they are the *same* pull seen from the two ends: each person pulls the rope with 50 N and the rope pulls each person back with 50 N. (Equivalently: replace one person with a wall — the wall supplies 50 N and nobody claims the tension "doubled" when the person replaced the wall.)
2. **Q:** A 20 N jerrican hangs from the middle of a line whose two halves each make 10° with the horizontal. Estimate the tension in the line.
   **Worked Answer:** Each side's vertical part holds half the weight: T sin 10° = 10 N. sin 10° ≈ 0.17, so T ≈ 10 ÷ 0.17 ≈ 59 N — about three times the load, which is why over-tight clotheslines snap posts and why you must never sit on a tightrope.
3. **Q:** A sisal rope is rated at 600 N straight. Its knot typically costs about 40% of strength. Is it safe to hoist a 300 N jerrican with a knotted sling?
   **Worked Answer:** Knotted strength ≈ 0.60 × 600 = 360 N. The 300 N load is under 360 N, so it holds — but with a safety factor of only 1.2, far below the usual rule of 5× for lifting. Verdict: it may hold once, but it is not *safe*; use a thicker rope or proper hitches and keep people clear.

## Safety Notes
- Hard pulling on anchored ropes: check the anchor (post, hook, tree) can take it; a pulling-out hook becomes a missile.
- Never wrap rope around hands or wrists during pulls — sudden slip crushes and burns.
- Frayed sisal sheds sharp fibres — learners with cuts on their hands handle the smooth offcut only.
- The tensioned clothesline can snap back if it breaks or the knot gives — keep faces out of the recoil line and do not over-tighten.
- No hoisting of loads over anyone, ever; the knot-strength discussion is theory, not permission.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 032–033 (balanced forces, diagrams); tension is the first of three "special named forces" (compression 041, normal 042, elastic 043).
- Younger learners (10–13): the wall-vs-partner demo is the whole lesson; let them vote again after it and watch the 100 N camp collapse. Older learners: run the clothesline trigonometry qualitatively, then quantitatively in the advanced questions.
- The goat tether and well rope are the strongest local anchors for this idea — send learners to look, not to pull.
- Jua kali tip: sisal rope from the market varies hugely; for demos buy the 8–10 mm twisted grade, and inspect for rot by bending — rotten fibres crackle.
- CBE assessment note: Q1 in pre/post is a pure growth measure — record the swing from guesses to the correct 50 N answer.
