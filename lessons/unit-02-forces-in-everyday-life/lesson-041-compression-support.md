# Lesson 041: Compression and Support Forces

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define compression as a squeezing force and support force as the push that holds things up.
2. Contrast tension (pulling apart) with compression (squeezing together).
3. Describe how pillars, poles, stool legs and soil carry compression.
4. Predict how a piece of chalk fails when bent versus squeezed.

## Materials
- Sticks of chalk (several, plus one thick piece)
- Foam cushion, old mattress piece or folded blanket
- A stool or chair with four legs
- A patch of bare soil and a flat stone
- A wooden ruler or flat stick for the bending demo

## Vocabulary
- **compression** (ubanaji) — squeezing force that shortens or compacts
- **support force** — the upward push from a surface or structure holding a load
- **tension** (mvutano) — pulling force that stretches (from Lesson 040)
- **pillar** (nguzo) — a vertical compression member
- **load** (mzigo) — the weight a structure carries

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Stand on a cushion, then on the floor. Which one squishes, and what does that squish tell you?
2. A rope and a wooden pole — which can push, which can only pull?
3. The legs of a stool — are they being stretched or squeezed when someone sits?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The cushion squishes; the squish is the cushion pushing back (support). Accept simple answers.
2. Pole can push and pull; rope only pulls. Accept partial answers.
3. Squeezed (compressed). Accept guesses.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; foam and stool at the front.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — two volunteers: one stands on the foam (it sinks), one on the bare floor (it does not). *Both are being held up — so where is the "push" on the hard floor?* Invisible forces today.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — (a) Foam vs floor: press a hand into foam — see compression; press the desk — same force, too small to see. (b) Soil test: press the flat stone onto bare soil, lift it — the dent is compressed soil; that is how foundations carry houses. (c) Chalk squeeze vs bend: squeeze a chalk stick between fingers along its length — very strong; now hold its ends and bend it — it snaps easily. (d) Stool anatomy: sit on the stool and trace the forces — seat pushes legs, legs push floor, floor pushes back; every leg is in compression.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate with a two-column board: TENSION stretches (ropes, strings), COMPRESSION squeezes (pillars, legs, soil, bones). Both are pushes/pulls measured in newtons; the difference is direction relative to the material. Bent chalk fails on the stretched side: bending puts one face in tension, and chalk is weak in tension though strong in compression — the crack opens on the outside of the bend. Confront the misconception: "the floor does nothing" — the floor pushes up exactly as hard as you push down; it just does not dent visibly.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *ropes pull, pillars push.* Homework: find one thing in compression and one in tension at home; name each with its force.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. What does the squish of a cushion under your foot tell you about forces? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Which can push, a rope or a pole? Explain in one sentence. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name the force type in each: (a) a clothesline holding cloths, (b) a house pillar, (c) stool legs with someone seated.
4. Why did the chalk survive squeezing but snap when bent?
5. Challenge: look at the crack in a bent stick of chalk — on which side of the bend does it start, and why?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The cushion is being compressed, and its squish pushing back is the support force holding you up.
2. A pole. It stays rigid when squeezed (compression); a rope buckles and can only transmit pulls (tension).
3. (a) tension, (b) compression, (c) compression.
4. Chalk is strong in compression (particles pressed together) but weak in tension (particles pulled apart); bending creates tension on one face, so it fails there.
5. On the outside of the bend — that face is stretched (tension) while the inside is squeezed; the crack opens where the material is being pulled apart.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Why does chalk fail in tension before compression? Link your answer to the crack opening at the bent side.
   **Worked Answer:** Chalk is a packed powder held by weak bonds between particles. Compression just presses the particles harder together — the bonds are barely challenged, so chalk carries large squeezing loads. Tension pulls particles apart, which the weak bonds resist poorly, so small pulls open cracks. In bending, the outer face of the bend is stretched (tension) while the inner face is squeezed (compression); the crack starts on the outer, tension side and runs across — exactly what we observed.
2. **Q:** A 600 N learner stands on a cushion. What support force does the cushion supply, and what force does the learner's feet exert on the cushion?
   **Worked Answer:** The learner is in balance, so the cushion's support force = 600 N upward. By the same push-back logic (and Newton's third law), the feet push the cushion down with 600 N. The cushion's job is to supply that 600 N while deforming visibly — the floor supplies it too, just without visible dent.
3. **Q:** A four-legged stool carries a 700 N person plus its own 100 N. If the load shares equally, what compression does each leg carry? Why might one leg actually carry more?
   **Worked Answer:** Total = 800 N; shared equally: 800 ÷ 4 = 200 N per leg. In reality uneven floor, a slightly short leg, or sitting off-centre concentrates load — one leg may carry well over 200 N, which is why stools wobble and why the short leg's corner of the seat cracks first.

## Safety Notes
- Chalk snapping: hold with both ends pointing away from faces; small pieces are choking hazards for younger siblings — sweep up all fragments.
- Standing demos: one learner on the foam at a time, hands free, a partner spotting for balance — a toppling learner is the only real hazard.
- The soil press must be away from foundations and paths; do not undermine veranda edges.
- Do not sit load tests beyond one person on the stool; stools are not rated for stacks.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 040 (tension); today is its mirror image. The tension/compression pair runs straight into structures, bridges and the Moments unit later.
- Younger learners (10–13): the felt difference (foam vs floor) and the chalk snap carry it; vocabulary is "squish = compression". Older learners: bending as mixed tension+compression — that idea explains every crack in every mud wall and beam.
- Local anchors: house pillars and veranda posts (compression), sisal ties and cables (tension), the fundi's bench legs, mabati roof battens.
- If no foam exists, a heap of dry grass or a folded kanga compresses visibly and works fine.
- CBE assessment note: Q3 (classify forces) is the growth check; record classification accuracy per learner.
