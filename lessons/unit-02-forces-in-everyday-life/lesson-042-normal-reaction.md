# Lesson 042: The Normal Force: Surfaces Push Back

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.5  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define the normal force as the perpendicular push a surface gives back.
2. Read a bathroom scale as a normal-force meter, not a "mass machine".
3. Draw the normal force on diagrams: flat ground, walls, and slopes.
4. Explain why "normal" means perpendicular, not ordinary.

## Materials
- Bathroom scale (market spring type or digital)
- A clear wall section
- A stack of exercise books
- A plank and a brick/stone to make a ramp
- Chalk for force diagrams

## Vocabulary
- **normal force** (nguvu ya msimamo) — the perpendicular push a surface returns
- **perpendicular** (wima) — at right angles (90°) to the surface
- **scale** (mizani) — a spring device reading the push on it
- **ramp** (mteremko) — a sloped surface
- **reaction** — the push-back from a surface when you press it

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. A 5 kg book sits on a table. Gravity pulls it down with 50 N — why does it not fall through?
2. You lean hard on a wall. Does the wall push on you? How do you know?
3. What does a bathroom scale actually measure — your mass or a force? Guess.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The table pushes up on it (support). Accept any version.
2. Yes — you feel it in your hand/body; if it did not, you would fall through. Accept honest answers.
3. A force (the push on its spring). Accept guesses.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; scale at the front.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — stand on the scale, then push down hard with hands on the desk while standing on it: the reading jumps. *Your mass did not change in one second — so what is this machine really reading?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — (a) Scale games: stand normally (read it), hold books (reading rises by the books' weight), lift a foot onto a stool halfway (reading unchanged while still), squat slightly and watch it swing during motion. (b) Wall lean: lean at increasing angle — the wall pushes back harder the more you commit; sketch it. (c) Ramp: rest the plank on a brick, slide a book down slowly; draw the book on the slope and ask which way the surface pushes — not "up", but perpendicular to the plank.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: every surface pushes back at right angles to itself — that push is the normal force. "Normal" is the maths word for perpendicular (like the fundi's square). On flat ground at rest, normal = weight; leaning on a wall, the wall's normal is horizontal; on a ramp, the normal is tilted and smaller than the weight. Confront the misconception: the scale does not measure mass — it measures the normal push, which only equals your weight when you stand still on the level.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *surfaces answer every push with a push.* Homework: stand on the home scale and raise your arms overhead slowly — does the reading change? Write what happens and why.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. A 5 kg book rests on a table. Name the upward force on it and give its size. (mirrors pre-test)
2. What does a bathroom scale really measure? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Draw a box on a ramp and show the normal force arrow correctly.
4. You stand on a scale and press down on a desk with your hands. The reading rises, though your mass is unchanged. Explain.
5. Challenge: on a steep ramp, is the normal force bigger, smaller, or equal to your weight? Why?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The normal force (table's support push); 50 N upward, balancing weight.
2. The normal force pressing on its spring — equal to your weight only when you stand still and level.
3. Arrow drawn perpendicular (at 90°) out of the ramp surface, not straight up.
4. The desk pushes your hands up (normal from the desk), so you push the scale down harder; the scale reads the total push — weight plus the desk's reaction. Mass never changed.
5. Smaller. Only the part of your weight pressing straight into the ramp counts; the rest is a part pulling you down the slope. The steeper the ramp, the smaller the normal.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** On a 30° ramp, which is bigger — your weight or the normal force? Resolve the vectors qualitatively, then compute both for a 60 kg learner.
   **Worked Answer:** The normal is smaller. Weight W = mg = 60 × 10 = 600 N acts straight down; split it into a part into the ramp (W cos 30° = 600 × 0.87 ≈ 520 N) and a part along the ramp (W sin 30° = 300 N). The ramp only answers the into-ramp part, so normal N ≈ 520 N < 600 N. The 300 N along-ramp part is what accelerates you downhill unless friction holds it.
2. **Q:** A 50 kg learner stands on a scale in a stationary position, then squats down quickly. Describe and explain the scale reading during (a) standing still, (b) accelerating downward into the squat.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Still: normal = weight = 500 N, scale reads 50 kg. (b) Accelerating downward: the net force must be downward, so normal < weight; the scale dips below 50 kg while she speeds up into the squat, then overshoots above as she brakes to a stop at the bottom, settling back at 50 kg. Mass never changed — only the push.
3. **Q:** Explain why you can push a wall "as hard as you like" yet the wall never accelerates — where do the forces balance?
   **Worked Answer:** Your hands push the wall (into it); the wall's normal pushes back on you equally. For you, leaning balance is: wall's normal forward, ground friction backward at your feet, weight down, ground normal up — net zero, so you stay. For the wall, your push is passed into the wall's structure and foundations, whose compression and the ground's reactions balance it; the whole Earth takes the momentum and its motion change is unmeasurably tiny.

## Safety Notes
- Scale games: no jumping onto the scale — it breaks and the edge can flip onto toes; step on gently, one learner at a time.
- Wall leaning: palms flat, elbows soft, feet not too far back — a slipping lean ends in a face-plant; pick a clean wall free of nails and peeling paint.
- Ramp: low angle only, brick stable, book slides not learners; nobody walks the plank.
- The scale stays on flat ground; a tilted scale misreads and slides.
