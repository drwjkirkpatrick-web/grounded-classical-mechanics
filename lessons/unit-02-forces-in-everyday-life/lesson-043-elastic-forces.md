# Lesson 043: Elastic Forces: Things That Spring Back

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe elastic force as the pull-back a stretched or bent elastic object exerts.
2. Show that the restoring force grows as the stretch grows.
3. Distinguish elastic behaviour (springs back) from plastic behaviour (stays deformed).
4. Connect stored elastic energy to the slingshot (manati) and the bow — previewing energy units.

## Materials
- Rubber strips (inner-tube bands — boda fundi discards are perfect)
- An old spring (from a mattress, lock, or shock absorber)
- A green stick bent as a small bow (or a flexible ruler)
- String and a few stones or water bottles as known weights
- Ruler or tape measure for stretch readings
- Chalk for the force-vs-stretch sketch

## Vocabulary
- **elastic** (kunjufu) — springs back to shape when released
- **restoring force** — the pull-back trying to return the object to its shape
- **extension** (kuenea) — how much longer the band/spring got, in cm
- **plastic deformation** — a permanent bend or stretch that stays
- **stored energy** — work hidden in a stretched elastic, ready to return

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. Stretch a rubber band gently, then hard. When is it harder to hold? What does that tell you?
2. You bend a green stick and let go. What happens, and what made it happen?
3. Stretch a rubber band past a certain point and it stays baggy or snaps. Name that limit in your own words.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Harder to hold when stretched more — the pull-back grows with stretch. Accept felt descriptions.
2. It springs back (and can throw things); the bent stick pulls itself straight. Accept simple answers.
3. The point where it stops springing back — accept any honest wording (elastic limit).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; rubber strips cut before class.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — shoot a crumpled paper ball across the room with a rubber strip manati-style (away from everyone). *The strip just did work on that paper. Where was that energy kept a second ago?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — (a) Feel the law: each learner stretches a rubber strip 5 cm, 10 cm, 15 cm by hand and rates the pull — it clearly grows. (b) Quantify: hang the strip, add known weights (stones/bottles: 1 L = 1 kg → 10 N), measure extension for each; plot force vs extension — a straight line at first, then it steepens and finally the strip stays baggy. (c) Spring squeeze and pull — same story in compression and tension. (d) Bend the green stick: small bend springs back, big bend stays bent — plastic past the limit.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: elastic materials pull back with a restoring force that grows with stretch — for many springs, force = k × extension (Hooke's law; name it for the older learners, use "the more stretch, the more force" for everyone). Past the elastic limit, bonds slip and the change is permanent (plastic). Stretching stores energy that returns when released — the manati and the bow are energy stores. Confront the misconception: "rubber pulls because it wants to" — it pulls because stretched polymer chains are straighter than their natural coiled state, and random jiggling pulls them back.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *stretch stores force, and force is released as motion.* Homework: find one elastic thing at home (mattress spring, sandal strap, elastic in clothes) and say what it does when released.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. The more you stretch a rubber band, the harder it pulls back. State that as a rule about elastic force. (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is the "elastic limit" — describe it. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Our strip extended 4 cm under 10 N and 8 cm under 20 N. What extension do you predict under 30 N, and why?
4. Give one example of elastic behaviour and one of plastic deformation from today's demos.
5. Challenge: a drawn bow holds an arrow still, then releases it fast. Describe the force and energy story.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Restoring force increases with extension (older learners: F = kx).
2. The stretch beyond which the material no longer springs back fully — it stays deformed or breaks.
3. About 12 cm — the pattern is proportional: each 10 N adds 4 cm (while inside the elastic range).
4. Elastic: the rubber strip springing back / spring recovering. Plastic: the over-bent green stick staying bent / the baggy over-stretched band.
5. Drawing the bow stretches/bends it against its elastic force, storing energy; at release the restoring force accelerates the arrow, converting stored elastic energy into the arrow's kinetic energy.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Sketch force vs extension for a rubber band up to breaking, and explain why the curve bends upward at large extension. (Hint: polymer chains.)
   **Worked Answer:** The curve starts as a straight line (Hooke's region: chains uncoiling easily, force ∝ extension), then steepens: once most coils are pulled nearly straight, further stretch must pull the chains' own bonds, which resist far more strongly, so each extra cm costs much more force. Finally the band necks and snaps (curve ends). The S-shape — soft start, stiff finish — is the signature of rubber, unlike a steel spring which stays linear much further.
2. **Q:** Our strip extended 4 cm per 10 N. Find its spring constant k in N/m, and the force needed for 20 cm extension (within the elastic range).
   **Worked Answer:** k = F/x = 10 N ÷ 0.04 m = 250 N/m. For x = 0.20 m: F = kx = 250 × 0.20 = 50 N — the weight of a 5 kg jerrican.
3. **Q:** A manati strip stores about ½kx² of energy. With k = 250 N/m stretched 0.20 m, estimate the stored energy and the launch speed of a 10 g stone if all of it converts to kinetic energy.
   **Worked Answer:** E = ½ × 250 × (0.20)² = 0.5 × 250 × 0.04 = 5 J. Then ½mv² = 5 → v² = 2 × 5 ÷ 0.010 = 1000 → v ≈ 32 m/s. Real launches are slower (the band itself must move, air drag, imperfect transfer) — but this is why eye-level manati games are banned in this school.

## Safety Notes
- Rubber strips under tension snap back viciously: stretch along the ground or toward a wall, never toward faces; eye protection (or turned backs) for the launch demo, which the TEACHER performs alone, aimed at empty space.
- Manati/slingshot discussion is physics, not permission: state clearly that firing stones at people, animals or windows is dangerous and forbidden at school.
- Old springs have sharp cut ends — wrap them in tape before handling.
- The green-stick bow is a hand demo only — no arrows, no aiming.
- Over-stretched inner-tube rubber can have hidden tears; inspect each strip before class and retire cracked ones.
