# Lesson 044: Forces Come in Pairs: A First Look at Reaction

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that a push on an object produces a push back from that object.
2. Describe at least three everyday examples of force pairs (wall, ground, water).
3. Explain that the two forces in a pair act on *different* bodies.
4. Predict what happens when you push off a body that is free to move.

## Materials
- A clear stretch of classroom or veranda wall
- A strong plank, 2 m or longer (borrow from a fundi or building site)
- 3–4 round stones or short logs, 15–25 cm across, to act as rollers
- An open, swept floor space free of stones and sharp objects
- Chalk or charcoal for marking start positions

## Vocabulary
- **force** (nguvu) — a push or a pull
- **reaction** (mwitikio) — the push-back an object gives when you push it
- **pair** (jozi) — two things that come together; forces always come in pairs
- **body** (mwili) — the object a force acts on
- **roller** — a round stone or log that lets a heavy thing roll instead of slide

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. You push hard on a wall. Does the wall push on you? How do you know?
2. A boy steps forward off a floating log onto the river bank. Predict what the log does.
3. Two people push each other with equal force at the same moment. Who moves?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Yes — you feel pressure back on your hands; if it did not push back your hands would sink into it. (Accept any honest prediction.)
2. The log moves backwards, away from the bank.
3. Both move — each feels a push (the forces act on different bodies).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out pre-test slips; collect before any demonstration.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — one volunteer pushes the wall with two hands, leaning in. Ask the class: *the wall is not moving — so is anything pushing back?* Then ask the volunteer what they feel in their palms and arms. Board the words "push" and "push-back".
3. **Explore (15 min)** — three stations, learners rotate in groups:
   - *Wall push:* palms flat on the wall, push gently then harder. Feel the wall pressing back; lean so far that only the wall holds you up.
   - *Plank on rollers:* plank laid across the round stones. A volunteer stands on the plank and walks forward — the plank rolls backwards under them. Mark start and stop points with chalk.
   - *Step-off:* volunteer stands still on the plank, then steps off forward onto the ground. The plank shoots backwards. Everyone observes who moved and in which direction.
   Teacher questions throughout: *which body did you push? Which body pushed you? Did they move the same way?*
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board: when body A pushes body B, body B pushes body A back at the same time. Write it as a pair: *feet push plank backwards; plank pushes feet forwards.* Key point: the two forces act on **different bodies** — one on the plank, one on the person. Confront the misconception that "the wall does nothing because it does not move" — it pushes back, that is exactly why you do not fall through it.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect for marking.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *you cannot touch without being touched back.* Homework: find one push–push-back pair at home (sitting on a stool, leaning on a fence, a jembe striking soil) and write one sentence naming both bodies.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. You push on a wall. Does the wall push back on you? Explain in one sentence. (mirrors pre-test)
2. A girl steps forward off a log into shallow water. What does the log do, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. In the plank-on-rollers activity, name the two forces in the pair and the body each one acts on.
4. A fisherman pushes his canoe away from the river bank with a pole. Describe the force pair.
5. Challenge: the wall pushes you back with a force exactly equal to yours, yet you can still lean on it without the wall or you flying apart. Why doesn't the pair cancel out to "nothing happens"?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Yes — the wall exerts an equal push back on my hands; that is why I feel pressure and do not pass through.
2. The log moves backwards; her feet pushed the log backwards, so the log pushed her forwards — the pair acts on different bodies.
3. Feet push plank backwards (force on the plank); plank pushes feet forwards (force on the person).
4. Pole pushes bank backwards (on the bank); bank pushes pole — and through it the canoe — forwards (on the canoe/pole).
5. Equal and opposite forces cancel only when they act on the *same* body. This pair acts on two different bodies (person and wall), so each body keeps its own force — both feel a real push.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** If every force has an equal and opposite partner, why does anything ever accelerate? Should they not all cancel?
   **Worked Answer:** Cancellation needs both forces on the *same* body. A pair never is: when you push the plank backwards, the plank pushes *you* forwards. Your acceleration comes from the plank's force on you; the plank's acceleration comes from your force on it. Each body responds to the force acting *on it* alone, so both can — and do — accelerate in opposite directions.
2. **Q:** A 60 kg learner stands on a 15 kg plank resting on rollers. She pushes off and the plank shoots backwards at 2 m/s. Using momentum thinking (mass × velocity balanced), estimate how fast she moves forward.
   **Worked Answer:** Before, total momentum is zero. After: plank momentum = 15 kg × 2 m/s = 30 kg·m/s backwards, so the learner must carry 30 kg·m/s forwards. Her speed = 30 / 60 = 0.5 m/s forwards. The lighter body gets the bigger speed — the same reason the plank flew and she only stepped.
3. **Q:** A classmate says: "When I sit still on a stool, my weight pushes down on the stool and the stool pushes up on me — those two cancel, so I should feel nothing." Where is the error?
   **Worked Answer:** The forces act on different bodies — weight-pair ideas aside, the upward push of the stool acts on *him*, and he does feel it: as pressure on his body. "Feeling nothing" would mean no force on him at all, i.e. falling. Comfort is not zero force; it is force balanced by another force on the *same* body.

## Safety Notes
- Roller stones can pinch fingers and toes — learners place the plank slowly and keep hands on top, never under the edges; sandals with closed toes preferred.
- Only one person on the plank at a time; spotters stand to the *side*, never behind where the plank shoots backwards.
- Wall push: palms flat, no pushing with the head or shoulder; stop if the surface crumbles (wattle-and-daub walls can give way).
- Sweep the floor first — a dropped roller on a stone can chip and throw fragments.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should already name pushes and pulls (Unit 1 measurement ideas are not required). This lesson seeds Lessons 045–047 (Newton's laws) — keep language of "push and push-back" consistent so the formal laws land easily.
- Ages 10–13: keep it physical and verbal — act out the pairs, name the two bodies aloud. Ages 14–17: push the "different bodies" rule hard and use the momentum-style advanced question with real numbers.
- Local tips: a jua kali fundi will lend a plank for a day; choose round river stones of similar diameter, or cut short sections of a straight log. If no plank exists, a wide mabati offcut laid flat works but bends — reduce the walker's weight.
- Assessment: the clearest growth signal is pre-test Q1 vs post-test Q1 ("does the wall push back?"). Record per-learner growth for the CBE learner record under scientific reasoning.
