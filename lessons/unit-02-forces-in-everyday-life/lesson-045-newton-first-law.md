# Lesson 045: Newton's First Law: Inertia

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State Newton's first law in their own words: things keep doing what they are doing unless a force changes it.
2. Demonstrate inertia with the coin-and-card flick.
3. Explain everyday lurches (braking matatu, starting mkokoteni) using inertia.
4. Describe how inertia increases with mass, and connect it to seatbelts.

## Materials
- One coin per pair (any size; a 5- or 10-shilling coin is ideal)
- Stiff card pieces, about 10 × 6 cm (cut from a carton box)
- One glass or plastic bottle per pair, with a mouth slightly wider than the coin
- A second bottle filled with water or sand (the "heavy twin")
- Chalkboard or chart paper for the law in words

## Vocabulary
- **inertia** (ussi) — the "laziness" of matter: its resistance to changing its motion
- **rest** (mapumziko) — not moving
- **motion** (mwendo) — moving; a body's speed and direction
- **mass** (uzani) — the amount of matter; more mass means more inertia
- **balanced force** (nguvu sawa) — pushes that cancel, so motion does not change

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. A coin sits on a card over a bottle mouth. You flick the card away fast. Predict: does the coin fly with the card, or drop into the bottle?
2. A matatu brakes suddenly. Which way do standing passengers lurch, and why?
3. Which is harder to start pushing: an empty debe or a full debe? Give a reason.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. It drops into the bottle. (Accept any prediction — the demo will settle it.)
2. Forward — their bodies keep moving at the matatu's old speed.
3. The full debe — it has more mass, so more "laziness" to overcome.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — pre-test slips out and collected before any demo.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — tell the braking-matatu story with feeling: passengers lurch forward, someone's shopping slides off the seat, a standing man grabs the rail. Ask: *what pushed them? Nobody pushed them!* Board the mystery.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs run three quick investigations:
   - *Coin and card:* coin on card over the bottle mouth; flick the card sharply and horizontally. The card flies, the coin drops straight into the bottle. Repeat until every pair succeeds twice.
   - *Slow vs fast pull:* now pull the card slowly — the coin rides along with it. Compare: fast flick leaves no time for friction to drag the coin; slow pull gives friction time to act.
   - *Light vs heavy bottle:* try to slide the empty bottle across the desk with a finger flick, then the full twin with the same flick. The full one barely moves — same force, more mass, less change of motion.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: write the first law in plain words on the board — *a body at rest stays at rest, and a body moving keeps moving straight and steady, unless an unbalanced force acts.* The coin was at rest and stayed at rest (gravity pulled it down once the card left). Passengers kept moving when the matatu stopped. Inertia is not a force — it is a property, and it grows with mass. Confront the misconception head-on: *nothing needs a force to keep moving; things stop only because friction and other forces stop them.*
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark if time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *matter is lazy — it keeps doing what it was doing.* Homework: watch one journey today (matatu, boda, wheelbarrow) and write one sentence describing inertia in action.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. Coin on card over a bottle; the card is flicked away fast. What happens to the coin, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A matatu brakes suddenly. Which way do standing passengers lurch, and what property explains it? (mirrors pre-test)
3. When the card was pulled *slowly*, the coin moved with it. Explain the difference from the fast flick.
4. A full jerrican and an empty jerrican sit on a handcart. The cart hits a bump and stops dead. Which jerrican is more likely to keep going and topple forward? Why?
5. Challenge: use inertia to explain why a seatbelt (or holding the rail) matters even at town speeds.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The coin drops into the bottle; it was at rest and its inertia kept it in place while the card flew out, then gravity pulled it down.
2. Forward — inertia: their bodies kept the matatu's forward motion after the bus slowed.
3. A slow pull lets friction act on the coin for longer, dragging it along; the fast flick removes the card before friction can transfer much push.
4. The full one — more mass means more inertia, so it resists stopping more strongly and topples forward.
5. In a sudden stop your body keeps moving at the old speed; the belt supplies the stopping force to your body, instead of the windscreen or the seat ahead doing it.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Use inertia to explain why a fully loaded lorry needs a longer braking distance than an empty one at the same speed, even with equally good brakes.
   **Worked Answer:** Brakes can only apply a limited force (set by tyre grip and brake strength). Stopping means removing momentum, and the loaded lorry carries far more mass at the same speed, so far more momentum. With force limited, more mass means less deceleration (F = ma → a = F/m), so it takes more time and more road to shed the same speed. Its inertia — resistance to changing motion — is simply bigger.
2. **Q:** A 70 kg passenger rides a matatu at 20 m/s (72 km/h). The matatu stops in 1 second in an emergency. What average force must act on the passenger to stop him with the vehicle? What stops him if he is not belted?
   **Worked Answer:** a = Δv/Δt = 20/1 = 20 m/s². F = ma = 70 × 20 = 1,400 N — about the weight of 140 kg, far more than arms can hold. Unbelted, the only things available to supply that force are the seat in front, the rail, or the windscreen — supplied suddenly, to the head and chest. This is why "I will just hold on" fails.
3. **Q:** A tablecloth is whipped from under a full sufuria without spilling. Explain, and state the two conditions that make the trick work.
   **Worked Answer:** The sufuria's inertia keeps it at rest while the cloth slides out beneath it. Conditions: (1) the pull must be very fast, so friction has little time to act; (2) friction between cloth and sufuria must be small (smooth cloth, no snagging hem). The coin-and-card demo is the same physics at desk scale.

## Safety Notes
- Glass bottles can chip or topple — keep them on desks, not floor edges; use plastic bottles for the youngest learners.
- Flick cards horizontally, away from faces; a flicked card corner can sting an eye.
- No standing on desks for "braking" role-play — act the lurch gently while seated or standing with feet planted.
- Keep water-filled bottles capped; spills make floors slippery.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 044 (forces come in pairs) helps but is not required; this lesson stands alone and leads directly into Lesson 046 (F = ma).
- Ages 10–13: keep the law in the plain-words version and let them master the coin trick to demonstrate at home. Ages 14–17: introduce the formal statement ("uniform motion in a straight line") and run the force calculation in Advanced Q2 with their own mass.
- Local tips: cards cut from a soap carton are stiff enough; a 10-shilling coin drops cleanly into a standard soda-bottle mouth. If coins are scarce, a smooth flat stone works.
- The seatbelt discussion lands hard — keep it factual, not frightening; frame it as "physics you can use to protect your family."
- Assessment: pre vs post Q1 and Q2 give a clean growth measure; note in the CBE learner record under physical science reasoning.
