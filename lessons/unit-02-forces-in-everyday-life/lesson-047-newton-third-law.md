# Lesson 047: Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State Newton's third law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction.
2. Demonstrate action–reaction with a balloon racing along a string line.
3. Identify the force pair — and the two bodies involved — in five everyday scenarios.
4. Explain walking and rowing as pushing the world backwards to go forwards.

## Materials
- Round balloons (two per pair, plus spares — they burst)
- Plastic drinking straws, cut in half (or rolled paper tubes)
- Smooth string or fishing line, 5–6 m per line (sisal works if smooth)
- Cellotape
- Two chairs or posts per line to tension the string
- A chart on the board listing five scenarios for the pair hunt

## Vocabulary
- **action** (kitendo) — the first push in a pair
- **reaction** (mwitikio) — the equal, opposite push back
- **pair** (jozi) — forces always arrive as two, on two different bodies
- **thrust** (msukumo) — the forward push from escaping air or a spinning propeller
- **opposite** (kinyume) — facing the other way

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for measuring growth against the post-test.*

1. You release an inflated balloon with its neck open. Which way does the air go, and which way does the balloon go?
2. When you walk forward, which way do your feet push the ground?
3. A canoe paddle pushes water backwards. What pushes the canoe forwards?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Air rushes backwards; the balloon shoots forwards.
2. Backwards (accept "down and back" — the detail comes in the lesson).
3. The water — it pushes the paddle, and so the canoe, forwards.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected before any balloon appears.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — release an untied inflated balloon and let it zigzag around the room to general delight. Ask: *what pushed it? Nothing touched it!* Board the words "action" and "reaction".
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the balloon line (see Hands-On Build) and watch straight, controlled action–reaction. Then the class walks through the five-scenario pair hunt on the board: (a) walking, (b) rowing a canoe, (c) a boda tyre on the road, (d) a chapati pressed flat by a rolling pin, (e) a learner leaning on a desk. For each, groups name both forces and both bodies before the teacher reveals the answer.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: Newton's third law — *if A pushes B, B pushes A back, equal size, opposite direction, at the same instant.* Walking: foot pushes Earth backwards, Earth pushes foot forwards — the planet is too massive to notice its share. Rowing: paddle pushes water back, water pushes paddle forward. Confront the misconception that the balloon is "pushed by the room's air" — it works because the balloon pushes its *own* air backwards; the air pushes back.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark if time allows.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *to go forward, push something backwards.* Homework: find one action–reaction pair at home and write one sentence naming both bodies.

## Hands-On Build: Balloon Rocket Line
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; cut straws and string the day before.*

### Parts
- Balloon — 2 per pair — market or shop; round balloons work best
- Straw — 1, cut in half — or a rolled paper tube as substitute
- String — 5–6 m — fishing line or smooth sisal; rough rope adds friction
- Cellotape — two small pieces — to join balloon to straw
- Chairs or posts — 2 per line — to tension the string

### Workflow
1. Thread the string through the straw half.
2. Tie the string tightly between two chairs, as level as you can. **Checkpoint:** pluck it — it should twang, not sag; a sagging line stalls the rocket.
3. Inflate the balloon and pinch the neck shut with fingers — do not tie it.
4. Tape the balloon to the straw so the neck points backwards along the line.
5. **Checkpoint:** with the straw at one end, confirm the neck points *away* from the direction of travel — air must leave backwards.
6. Release and watch the straw shoot along the line. Repeat once with a fuller balloon and once half-full; compare distances.

### Common Mistakes
- **Neck pointing forward** — the rocket flies backwards; flip the balloon round.
- **Tape over the neck** — it chokes the airflow; tape only the balloon's body.
- **Sagging string** — the straw climbs a hill it cannot manage; re-tie tighter.
- **Releasing the straw but not the neck** — both must go free in the same instant.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test (growth measure); 3–5 probe the day's new idea.*

1. A released balloon: which way does the air go, and which way does the balloon go? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A canoe paddle pushes water backwards. What pushes the canoe forwards? (mirrors pre-test)
3. State Newton's third law in your own words.
4. Name the action–reaction pair when you walk, and say why we never notice Earth's share of it.
5. Challenge: a book rests on a table. Gravity pulls it down, and the table pushes it up. Is that the third-law pair? Explain carefully.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Air leaves backwards; balloon moves forwards.
2. The water pushes the paddle — and through it the canoe — forwards.
3. Every force has an equal and opposite partner, acting at the same time on the other body.
4. Foot pushes Earth backwards; Earth pushes foot forwards. Earth's mass is enormous, so the same force gives it an acceleration far too small to detect.
5. No — both of those forces act on the *same* body (the book). The true pairs are: Earth pulls the book down / the book pulls Earth up; and the book pushes the table down / the table pushes the book up.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Quantitative or multi-step reasoning. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A boda and rider weigh 500 N and accelerate forwards. Identify the action–reaction pair at the tyre–road contact, and state which force actually accelerates the boda.
   **Worked Answer:** The tyre pushes the road *backwards* (action, on the road); the road pushes the tyre *forwards* (reaction, on the boda). It is the road's forward push on the tyre that accelerates the boda — the engine only makes the tyre push the road. On mud or ice the tyre cannot push the road backwards effectively (little grip), so the reaction is small and the wheel spins: the pair still exists, just weak.
2. **Q:** The boda above accelerates at 2 m/s². Its mass is 50 kg (500 N ÷ 10 N/kg). What forward force does the road apply to the tyre?
   **Worked Answer:** F = ma = 50 × 2 = 100 N forwards on the tyre — and by the third law, the tyre applies 100 N backwards on the road surface.
3. **Q:** "A horse pulls a cart, but by Newton's third law the cart pulls the horse back equally — so how can they ever move?" Resolve this classic puzzle.
   **Worked Answer:** The horse–cart pair acts on two different bodies, so it does not cancel for either one. What matters for the *horse* is the ground pushing its hooves forwards (reaction to hooves pushing ground backwards); if that ground force exceeds the cart's backward drag, the horse accelerates. Likewise the cart moves when the horse's forward pull exceeds the cart's friction. Draw the forces on each body separately and the paradox disappears.

## Safety Notes
- Burst balloon fragments are a choking hazard — sweep up every piece immediately, especially with younger siblings nearby.
- Do not over-inflate near faces; turn away when a balloon is straining.
- Tensioned string can snap back if a knot fails — check knots and keep faces away from the line ends.
- Clear the flight path; a speeding straw can poke an eye.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 044–046 (pairs, inertia, F = ma) feed straight in — this lesson names the third law formally. It prepares the project build in Lesson 051, where the same rocket line becomes a measured investigation.
- Ages 10–13: act out the pairs (two learners palm-to-palm pushing) and master the five-scenario hunt. Ages 14–17: the book-on-table challenge question and the horse-and-cart paradox are the real test of understanding.
- Local tips: fishing line from the market outperforms sisal; buy balloons fresh the same week — heat perishes them. The boda tyre example lands well: ask learners who have seen a boda spin its wheel in mud.
- For the pair hunt, localise freely: jembe striking soil, mkokoteni wheel on the path, posho-mill belt on its pulley.
- Assessment: pre vs post Q1 and Q2 measure growth; the five-scenario hunt is evidence for the CBE scientific-reasoning competency.
