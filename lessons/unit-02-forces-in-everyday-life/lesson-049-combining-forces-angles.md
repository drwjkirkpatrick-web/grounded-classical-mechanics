# Lesson 049: Combining Forces at Angles

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.5  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that two pulls at an angle move a load less effectively than one straight pull.
2. Explain that the resultant of two angled forces is less than their simple sum.
3. Estimate a resultant using a parallelogram scale drawing.
4. Give everyday examples where ropes are angled (towing, stretchers, clotheslines).

## Materials
- 20-litre jerrican, half full of water (10 kg — one per demo group)
- Two ropes, about 1.5 m each (sisal rope or old skipping ropes)
- Two spring balances (market type reading to 25 N or more)
- Chalkboard protractor (large, or a card semicircle marked in degrees)
- Exercise books and rulers for scale drawing
- Optional: a mkokoteni (handcart) to view its two handle pulls

## Vocabulary
- **resultant** (nguvu jumla) — the single force that has the same effect as all the forces together
- **component** (sehemu ya nguvu) — the part of a force acting in one chosen direction
- **parallelogram** (pembene sawa) — the four-sided drawing used to add two angled forces
- **angle** (pembe) — the opening between the two ropes, measured in degrees

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Two students pull a jerrican, each with 30 N, in exactly the same direction. What is the total pull?
2. The same two students now pull with their ropes spread wide apart. Is the forward pull still 60 N? Guess yes or no.
3. Why do two oxen ploughing wear a yoke that keeps them side by side rather than spread far apart?
4. Draw an arrow 4 cm long standing for a 40 N force. What does 1 cm represent?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. 60 N.
2. No — it is less than 60 N (accept any honest guess; the lesson proves it).
3. Accept ideas like: so their pulls add straight ahead and none is wasted sideways.
4. 1 cm represents 10 N.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out and collected.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — two volunteers pull the jerrican across the floor with ropes held together (parallel). Easy. Now spread the ropes to a wide V and pull again — same effort, the jerrican crawls. Ask: *where did your force go?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups rotate through three quick demos: (a) spring balances on both ropes at 0°, 60°, 90° and 120° apart — read each pull and feel how hard it is to slide the jerrican; (b) tie both ropes to one balance to read the true forward resultant; (c) sketch the two pulls as arrows from one point and close the parallelogram to predict the diagonal. Teacher circulates asking: *which angle gives the biggest resultant for the same effort?*
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board work: forces are vectors; angled pulls partly cancel sideways and only the forward components add. Draw the parallelogram for two 40 N pulls at 60° — diagonal comes out near 69 N, not 80 N. Confront the misconception that "force is force, just add the numbers" — direction matters as much as size.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *angled forces add by the parallelogram rule, not by arithmetic*. Homework: find one V-shaped rope at home or in the market (a towing rope, a swing) and sketch its two pulls.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Two students pull a jerrican with 30 N each in the same direction. Total pull? (mirrors pre-test)
2. When the same two ropes spread to a wide angle, does the forward pull stay 60 N? Explain in one sentence. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Two 20 N pulls act at 120° to each other. Is the resultant nearer to 40 N, 20 N, or 0 N? Choose and justify briefly.
4. A matatu is towed by a single straight chain. A second matatu is towed by a V-shaped chain. Which tow wastes more of the tractor's pull, and why?
5. Challenge: on a scale drawing of 1 cm = 10 N, two 5 cm arrows at 60° give a diagonal of 8.7 cm. State the resultant in newtons.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 60 N.
2. No — part of each pull cancels sideways; only the forward parts add, so the resultant is less than 60 N.
3. Nearer to 20 N — at 120° the two pulls mostly oppose each other; the parallelogram diagonal equals exactly 20 N.
4. The V-shaped chain wastes more: the angled legs pull partly sideways against each other, so less of each leg's tension pulls forward.
5. 8.7 cm × 10 N/cm = 87 N (accept 85–90 N from careful drawing).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Two students each pull with 40 N, their ropes at 60° to each other. Find the resultant by scale drawing and compare it with a single straight 80 N pull.
   **Worked Answer:** Draw arrows 4 cm long (1 cm = 10 N) at 60° apart; complete the parallelogram (a rhombus here). The diagonal measures about 6.9 cm → R ≈ 69 N. Check: R = 2F cos(θ/2) = 2 × 40 × cos 30° = 80 × 0.866 ≈ 69 N. So the pair delivers only about 69 N forward — 11 N short of the 80 N a single straight pull would give.
2. **Q:** The same two 40 N pulls are now at 120°. What is the resultant? What general rule links the angle to the wasted force?
   **Worked Answer:** R = 2 × 40 × cos 60° = 80 × 0.5 = 40 N. Rule: as the angle between the ropes grows, cos(θ/2) shrinks, so more of the effort cancels sideways; at 180° the pulls fully cancel (R = 0).
3. **Q:** A clothesline sags so each side makes 10° below horizontal and carries 100 N of tension on each side. How much of that tension actually holds the washing up?
   **Worked Answer:** Upward component from each side = 100 × sin 10° ≈ 17 N; both sides ≈ 34 N of support. The rest (100 × cos 10° ≈ 98 N each side) pulls the posts toward each other — which is why tight clotheslines bend posts inward.

## Safety Notes
- Jerricans of water are heavy — lift with bent knees, and keep toes clear when setting down.
- A rope under tension can snap or slip off a handle — inspect knots, no frayed sisal, and never stand in line with a taut rope.
- Spring balances jerk back when a knot gives — keep faces away from the pulling line.
- Clear the floor of bags and stones before dragging the jerrican to avoid trips.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 033 (force diagrams) has run; this lesson leads to components and eventually to moments in Lesson 050.
- Younger learners (10–13): keep it qualitative — "spread ropes waste force" — and let them feel the difference; older learners do the full scale drawing and the cos(θ/2) check.
- Market tips: two 25 N spring balances are cheap at the hardware stall; if only one exists, measure each rope in turn with the same jerrican load.
- A mkokoteni parked outside is a perfect live exhibit — its two handles are naturally parallel, which is exactly why it pulls well.
- CBE note: G10-PHY-1.5 requires vector addition by drawing — file one labelled parallelogram per learner as evidence.
