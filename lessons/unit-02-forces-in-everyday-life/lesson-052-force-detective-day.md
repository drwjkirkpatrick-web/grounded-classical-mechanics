# Lesson 052: Unit Review: Force Detective Day

**Unit:** 2 — Forces in Everyday Life  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Identify and name forces acting on real objects around the school (push, pull, weight, tension, friction, normal reaction).
2. Measure a force with a spring balance and record it with units.
3. Draw a labelled force diagram for an everyday object.
4. Review the whole unit through a short quiz and peer discussion.

## Materials
- Clipboards (or exercise books pressed against a mabati offcut) — one per pair
- Spring balances (force meters) — one per station group
- Ropes and a strong hook for the gate and water-tank stations
- Pencils, rulers, printed station cards
- School sites used as "crime scenes": the gate, the flagpole, the raised water tank, a seesaw or bench
- Whistle or bell for station rotation

## Vocabulary
- **tension** (mvutano) — the pulling force carried by a rope, cable or chain
- **weight** (uzito) — the pull of gravity on a mass: W = mg, with g = 10 N/kg
- **normal reaction** (nguvu ya msingi) — the push back from a surface supporting an object
- **force diagram** (mchoro wa nguvu) — arrows drawn from an object showing every force on it
- **evidence** (ushahidi) — a measurement or observation that supports a claim

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book. Purpose: baseline for the unit review.*

1. Name the force that pulls a raised water tank downward, and calculate it for a 200-litre tank of water.
2. A rope holds up a flag. What do we call the pulling force in the rope?
3. A book rests on a desk, not moving. Name the two forces on it and say how they compare.
4. State Newton's first law in your own words.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Weight (gravity); 200 L = 200 kg, so W = mg = 200 × 10 = 2000 N.
2. Tension.
3. Weight downward and normal reaction upward; they are equal and opposite (balanced).
4. Accept: an object keeps still or keeps moving straight at steady speed unless an unbalanced force acts on it.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — slips out; collect before heading out.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — teacher announces: *forces have been committing "crimes" all over the school — bending gates, stretching ropes, sinking posts. You are the detectives; your clipboard is your notebook; the spring balance is your magnifying glass.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs rotate through four stations (about 3–4 min each): **Gate** — hang the balance on the gate latch, pull it open slowly, record the force, and name what resists (friction in hinges). **Flagpole** — identify tension in the halyard and the weight of the flag; sketch both arrows. **Water tank** — estimate the water's weight (litres = kilograms), name the forces on the stand, and point to the compression in the legs. **Seesaw/bench** — two learners balance, then one moves inward; describe how the turning effect changed and name the pivot. Every station card demands: one named force, one measurement, one arrow diagram.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — back inside, board a master table: object / force name / size in newtons / direction. Confront the classic misconception found at the tank station — that "the stand pushes down on the ground only" — by asking what pushes up on the tank (normal reaction) and what stretches if the stand fails (tension in any bracing wire).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — unit quiz, individual.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *forces are everywhere once you have eyes to see them*. Homework: detective work at home — sketch one force diagram of a jiko, a jembe, or a mkokoteni with at least three labelled arrows.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe the review ideas.*

1. A 200-litre water tank sits on a stand. Name the downward force and calculate it. (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is the pulling force in a stretched rope called? (mirrors pre-test)
3. At the gate station, a pair measured 25 N to start the gate moving but only 10 N to keep it moving. Explain the difference.
4. A seesaw balances with a 30 kg child 1 m from the pivot. Where should a 40 kg child sit on the other side to balance? Show your working.
5. Challenge: the tank stand has four legs dug into soil. After heavy rain one leg sinks. Using force language, describe what changed.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Weight; W = mg = 200 kg × 10 N/kg = 2000 N.
2. Tension.
3. Starting must overcome static (sticking) friction in the hinges, which is larger than the moving friction once the gate swings.
4. Moments balance: 30 kg × 1 m = 40 kg × d → d = 30/40 = 0.75 m from the pivot.
5. Wet soil gives less support, so the normal reaction under that leg dropped; the leg's load shifted to the other legs (more compression there) and the stand tilted until forces balanced again.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** Design your own fifth station for Force Detective Day. It must have a measurable force question and a marking scheme.
   **Worked Answer:** Example — *Station: the water tap.* Question: "What force opens the tap fully?" Learners hook a spring balance to the tap handle, pull tangentially, and record the peak force (typically 5–15 N). Marking scheme: 1 mark naming the force pair (hand's pull vs valve friction), 1 mark correct unit (N), 1 mark a clean arrow diagram, 1 mark noting that the long handle works as a lever reducing the force needed. Any station with a clear measurement and a fair 4-mark scheme earns full credit.
2. **Q:** At the tank station a learner claims: "The stand feels the full 2000 N, so each leg carries 500 N exactly." Critique this.
   **Worked Answer:** Only if the tank is perfectly level and centred. If the stand tilts or the tank sits off-centre, load shifts: legs on the low side carry more (moments about the high legs). Real stands are never perfect, so 500 N per leg is the ideal average, not a guarantee — which is why stands are overbuilt with bracing.
3. **Q:** Your spring balance reads 45 N when a knot on the flagpole halyard finally slips. Estimate the coefficient of static friction if the rope wraps the cleat with a normal grip of about 90 N.
   **Worked Answer:** μ = F_friction / N = 45 / 90 = 0.5. A reasonable value for sisal on a wooden cleat; wrapping the rope twice raises the effective grip so a smaller holding force works.

## Safety Notes
- Stations are outdoors — set boundaries, a whistle signal, and a no-running rule between stations.
- Never hang a spring balance on anything that can fall (tank pipes, gate at full swing); test anchors yourself before class.
- Gate station: fingers away from hinge gaps; one learner holds the gate steady while another measures.
- Water tank: learners stay on the ground — no climbing the stand, ever.
- Seesaw: dismount one side at a time; a suddenly released end slams down.

## Teacher Notes
- This is the Unit 2 review; it assumes Lessons 027–050. It prepares learners for the unit test and for Project work in the next unit.
- Younger learners (10–13): grade their station cards on named forces and arrows only. Older learners: require measurements with units and the advanced critique question.
- Prepare the day before: walk the route, anchor the ropes, and check each spring balance against a known 1 kg mass (should read 10 N).
- Rain plan: move stations indoors — classroom door, window louvres, a desk lid, and a hanging debe make a full circuit.
