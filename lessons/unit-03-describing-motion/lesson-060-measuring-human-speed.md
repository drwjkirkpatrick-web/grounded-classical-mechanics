# Lesson 060: Measuring Human Speed: The 20-Metre Dash

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Time a 20 m sprint accurately and calculate speed from the data.
2. Recognise that reaction time introduces error in stopwatch measurements.
3. Suggest ways to make a speed measurement more repeatable and reliable.

## Materials
- A 30 m tape measure
- A stopwatch or phone timer (one per group)
- Chart paper or a blackboard grid for class results
- Chalk for marking start and finish lines
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Sprint** *(mbio)* — a short, fast run.
- **Repeatable** *(inayoweza kurudiwa)* — a measurement that gives a similar result when done again under the same conditions.
- **Reliable** *(inayoweza kutumika)* — a measurement that is trustworthy because it is consistent.
- **Reaction time** *(muda wa kujibu)* — the delay between seeing an event and responding to it.
- **Systematic error** *(kosa la kawaida)* — a consistent mistake in measurement that affects all readings in the same way.

## Pre-Test
1. You time your friend running 20 m. You press the stopwatch when they start, but you are a little late. Will your measured time be too long or too short?
2. Why is it better to time three dashes and take an average rather than using one result?
3. A learner runs 20 m in 4 s. What is their speed?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Too long — the stopwatch started late, so it measured extra time.
2. One result might be affected by a mistake; an average smooths out random errors.
3. Speed = 20 m / 4 s = **5 m/s**.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Quick oral check for Q1.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "Who thinks they are the fastest in the class? How could we prove it fairly?" (Expected: race them, time them.) Introduce the idea that measurement needs to be fair and repeatable.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, mark a 20 m straight course with chalk. One learner sprints; another times with a stopwatch; a third records; the fourth acts as a starter.
   - Each learner in the group completes three dashes. Record all nine times (three per learner).
   - Calculate speed for each dash: v = 20 m / time.
   - **Key discovery:** Times vary — even for the same runner. Discuss why: tired legs, different start responses, stopwatch delays.
   - **Error discussion:** The timer has reaction time (~0.2 s). For a 4 s dash, this is a 5 percent error. For a 3.5 s dash, the error is larger (~6 percent).
   - Groups post their average speeds on the class chart.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Repeatability:** A good experiment can be repeated with similar results. If one learner's times are 3.8 s, 5.2 s, and 4.1 s, something went wrong in the 5.2 s dash — perhaps a stumble or a late start.
   - **Reducing error:** Use a clap or whistle for a clear start signal. Have the same timer for all dashes to reduce systematic error. Take the median (middle value) if one result is very different.
   - **Reaction time:** A human takes about 0.2 s to react. At the start, late pressing adds time. At the finish, early pressing subtracts time. These partly cancel, but not perfectly.
   - Formula reminder: v = d/t. A shorter time means a higher speed.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states how to make a sprint measurement more reliable. Homework: time a family member walking 20 m. Do three trials, calculate average speed, and note any sources of error.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* A timer starts the stopwatch 0.3 s after the runner starts. Is the measured time too long or too short? Will the calculated speed be too high or too low?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* A learner's three dash times are 4.2 s, 4.5 s, and 6.1 s. Which time is probably an outlier? What should they do with it?
3. A learner runs 20 m in 3.5 s. Calculate their speed.
4. Suggest two ways to reduce the effect of reaction time on the measured speed.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The measured time is **too long**. The calculated speed is **too low** (v = d/t; larger t gives smaller v).
2. **6.1 s** is the outlier — much longer than the other two. They should repeat the dash or use the median (4.2 s, 4.5 s → median 4.35 s) or average of the two closest (4.35 s).
3. Speed = 20 m / 3.5 s = **5.71 m/s** (accept 5.7 m/s).
4. Any two of: use a loud starting signal; have the same person time all dashes; use electronic timing if available; take multiple trials and average; start the stopwatch on a signal rather than a visual cue.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A stopwatch operator has a reaction time of 0.2 s on both the start and the finish of a 20 m dash. The true time is 3.5 s. Estimate the speed error caused by reaction time and explain whether the measured speed is likely too high or too low.

**Worked Answer:**
If the timer is late at the start (+0.2 s) and late at the finish (+0.2 s), the errors partly cancel: measured time ≈ true time + 0.2 − 0.2 = **3.5 s**.
However, the start is usually harder to catch than the finish (the runner is moving fastest at the finish, making the moment clearer). A more realistic model: start error +0.2 s, finish error +0.1 s. Measured time = 3.5 + 0.2 − 0.1 = **3.6 s** (the finish was caught closer to the true moment).
True speed = 20 / 3.5 = **5.71 m/s**.
Measured speed = 20 / 3.6 = **5.56 m/s**.
Speed error = 5.71 − 5.56 = **0.15 m/s** (about 2.6 percent too low).
The measured speed is likely **too low** because the start delay dominates.

**Q:** Design an improved method for measuring sprint speed that minimises human reaction error. Describe the setup, the procedure, and how you would verify that your method is more reliable than a hand-held stopwatch.

**Worked Answer:**
**Setup:** Mark the 20 m course clearly. Place two observers: one at the start line with a whistle, one at the finish with a stopwatch. The starter blows the whistle and simultaneously drops a flag. The timer starts the stopwatch on the whistle sound (faster reaction than visual) and stops on the runner crossing the line.

**Procedure:**
1. Runner takes position at the start.
2. Starter blows whistle and drops flag.
3. Timer starts on the whistle; stops when the runner's torso crosses the finish line.
4. Record time; repeat three times; calculate average.

**Verification:** Compare the spread (range) of times using the whistle method versus the visual method. The whistle method should have a smaller range (more consistent) because sound travels faster than human visual reaction. Alternatively, use a video recording and frame-count (if a phone with slow-motion video is available) as a "gold standard" and compare both manual methods against it. The method with the smallest average deviation from the video time is the most reliable.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the 20 m course is straight and free of holes, stones, or slippery ground.
- Learners should warm up with a gentle jog before sprinting.
- No pushing or competitive racing between groups — stagger starts to avoid collisions.
