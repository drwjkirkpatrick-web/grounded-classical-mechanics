# Lesson 061: Distance–Time Graphs I: Plotting a Steady Walk

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Plot a distance–time graph from measured position data.
2. Identify that a straight-line graph indicates steady speed.
3. Extract speed from the gradient of a distance–time graph and compare it with the calculated value.

## Materials
- Chalk for marking a straight course on open ground
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- Graph paper or squared paper (one sheet per learner)
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Distance–time graph** *(mchoro wa umbali na muda)* — a graph showing how distance travelled changes with time.
- **Gradient** *(mteremko)* — the steepness of a line on a graph; for a distance–time graph, gradient equals speed.
- **Steady speed** *(kasi thabiti)* — moving at the same rate all the time; produces a straight line on a distance–time graph.
- **Axis** *(mhimili)* — the horizontal (time) or vertical (distance) line on a graph.

## Pre-Test
1. What does the horizontal axis of a distance–time graph represent?
2. If a learner walks at a steady speed, what shape will their distance–time graph have?
3. A graph shows 20 m covered in 10 s. What is the speed?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Time.
2. A straight line going up from left to right.
3. Speed = 20 m / 10 s = **2 m/s**.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Draw a simple graph on the board: time across the bottom, distance up the side. Ask, "If I walk away from the tree at the same pace, where do the points go?" Mark three points and connect them. Introduce the idea that the graph tells a story of motion.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** Mark a 20 m straight course with chalk every 2 m. One learner walks at a steady pace; another calls out positions every 5 s; a third records.
   - Data table: time (0, 5, 10, 15, 20 s) vs distance (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 m... depending on pace).
   - Each learner plots their group's data on graph paper: time on the horizontal axis, distance on the vertical axis.
   - **Key discovery:** The points form a straight line. A steeper line means a faster walker. A flatter line means a slower walker.
   - Compare groups: who walked fastest? Whose line is steepest?
   - **Extracting speed from the graph:** Pick two points on the line. Gradient = rise / run = change in distance / change in time = speed.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - Formalise: on a distance–time graph, **gradient = speed**. A straight line means steady speed; a curve means changing speed.
   - Example calculation: Point A at (5 s, 4 m) and Point B at (15 s, 12 m). Gradient = (12 − 4) / (15 − 5) = 8 / 10 = **0.8 m/s**.
   - Compare with raw data: total distance / total time = 12 m / 15 s = 0.8 m/s. They agree because both measure the same rate.
   - Why they agree: the gradient is just a visual way of doing Δd/Δt, which is the definition of speed.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states the link between gradient and speed. Homework: draw a distance–time graph for a journey from home to the market at a steady speed, then a stop at the shop.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* A learner runs at a steady speed. What shape is their distance–time graph?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* A graph shows 30 m in 10 s. What is the speed?
3. On a distance–time graph, what does a steeper line mean?
4. A straight line passes through (0 s, 0 m) and (10 s, 15 m). Calculate the gradient and state what it represents.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A **straight line** sloping upward from left to right.
2. Speed = 30 m / 10 s = **3 m/s**.
3. A steeper line means a **faster speed**.
4. Gradient = (15 − 0) / (10 − 0) = **1.5 m/s**. This represents the **speed** of the object.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** You plot a distance–time graph from a steady walk. You pick two points and calculate the gradient. You also calculate speed from total distance divided by total time. Why do the two values agree? Under what conditions might they differ?

**Worked Answer:**
The gradient of a distance–time graph is defined as Δd/Δt — the change in distance divided by the change in time. This is identical to the formula for average speed: total distance / total time. They agree because both compute the same ratio.

They would differ if:
- The speed is **not steady** (the graph is curved). The total-distance/total-time method gives average speed, while the gradient at a specific point gives instantaneous speed. On a curve, these are different.
- The two points chosen for the gradient are **not on the straight line** (measurement error). Averaging all data points smooths this out.
- There is a **stop** in the journey (horizontal section on the graph). The total-time method includes the stop; a gradient calculated between two moving points excludes it.

**Q:** Two learners walk the same 20 m course. Learner A's distance–time graph is a straight line from (0, 0) to (20 s, 20 m). Learner B's graph is a straight line from (0, 0) to (10 s, 20 m). Sketch both graphs on the same axes and explain which learner is faster and by what factor.

**Worked Answer:**
Learner A: speed = gradient = 20 m / 20 s = **1 m/s**.
Learner B: speed = gradient = 20 m / 10 s = **2 m/s**.

Learner B is faster by a factor of **2** (twice as fast).

Sketch description:
- Both lines start at the origin (0, 0).
- Line A rises gently to (20, 20).
- Line B rises steeply to (10, 20) and then becomes horizontal (if the walk ends) or continues.
- Line B is twice as steep as Line A. At any given time before 10 s, Learner B has covered more distance.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the chalk course is on flat ground away from paths.
- Learners walking backward to observe the timer should do so carefully.
