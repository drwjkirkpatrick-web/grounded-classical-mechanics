# Lesson 063: Speed–Time Graphs

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Plot a speed–time graph from measured data.
2. Interpret the gradient as acceleration and a flat section as steady speed.
3. Estimate distance travelled from the area under a speed–time graph.

## Materials
- Graph paper or squared paper (one sheet per learner)
- Ruler
- Coloured pencils (optional)
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Speed–time graph** *(mchoro wa kasi na muda)* — a graph showing how speed changes with time.
- **Gradient** *(mteremko)* — steepness of the line; on a speed–time graph, gradient = acceleration.
- **Area under the graph** *(eneo chini ya mchoro)* — the space between the graph line and the time axis; equals distance travelled.
- **Steady speed** *(kasi thabiti)* — a flat horizontal line on a speed–time graph.

## Pre-Test
1. What does a flat horizontal line on a speed–time graph mean?
2. What does the gradient of a speed–time graph represent?
3. A speed–time graph shows a triangle from 0 to 10 s, reaching 4 m/s. What is the distance travelled?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The object is moving at a constant (steady) speed.
2. Acceleration.
3. Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 10 × 4 = **20 m**.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "Yesterday we drew distance–time graphs. Today we flip the idea: what if we graph speed against time instead?" Show a simple speed–time graph on the board: rising, flat, falling. Ask learners to describe the motion in words.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** Using data from Lesson 060 (the 20 m dash), learners plot speed against time.
   - Since the dash data gives only one average speed per run, learners estimate how speed changed: starting from rest (0 m/s), speeding up, then perhaps steady for a moment, then slowing.
   - Groups sketch a realistic speed–time graph for a sprinter: a curve rising from zero, flattening briefly, then falling to zero.
   - **Key discovery:** The gradient of a speed–time graph tells you how quickly speed is changing — that is acceleration.
   - Count squares: learners count the squares under their graph (each square = 1 m/s × 1 s = 1 m) to estimate distance. Compare with the known 20 m dash.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Gradient = acceleration.** A steep rising line means large positive acceleration. A falling line means negative acceleration (deceleration).
   - **Flat = steady speed.** No acceleration; the object maintains its velocity.
   - **Area = distance.** For simple shapes:
     - Rectangle: area = base × height (distance at steady speed).
     - Triangle: area = ½ × base × height (distance while accelerating from rest).
     - Trapezium: area = average of parallel sides × height.
   - Example: A car accelerates from 0 to 10 m/s in 5 s, then cruises at 10 m/s for 5 s. Distance = triangle (½ × 5 × 10 = 25 m) + rectangle (5 × 10 = 50 m) = **75 m**.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states the two key facts about speed–time graphs (gradient = acceleration, area = distance). Homework: draw a speed–time graph for a matatu journey with three phases.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What does a horizontal line on a speed–time graph tell you?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* What does the gradient of a speed–time graph represent?
3. A speed–time graph shows a rectangle: 8 m/s high and 6 s wide. What distance was covered?
4. Sketch a speed–time graph for a car that accelerates from rest, cruises at a steady speed, then brakes to a stop.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The object is moving at a **steady (constant) speed**.
2. **Acceleration** (rate of change of speed).
3. Distance = area = 8 m/s × 6 s = **48 m**.
4. Rising curve/line from origin to a peak, flat horizontal section, falling curve/line back to the time axis.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A speed–time graph shows constant acceleration of 2 m/s² for 6 seconds, starting from rest. Sketch the graph and calculate (a) the final speed, and (b) the distance travelled by finding the area under the graph.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) Final speed: v = u + at = 0 + (2 m/s² × 6 s) = **12 m/s**.
   The graph is a straight line from (0, 0) to (6, 12).

(b) The area under the graph is a triangle:
   Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 6 s × 12 m/s = **36 m**.
   This is the distance travelled.

Alternatively, using kinematics: s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 2 × 36 = **36 m**.
Both methods agree because the area under a v–t graph always equals displacement (distance, for straight-line motion).

**Q:** Explain why the area under a speed–time graph gives distance, using the idea of counting squares. What would happen to your estimate if you used very large squares compared to very small squares?

**Worked Answer:**
Each small square on a speed–time graph represents speed × time = (m/s) × s = metres. The total number of squares under the curve equals the total distance travelled.

With **large squares**, many parts of the curve are approximated crudely — squares that are partly under the curve are counted as whole or ignored, introducing error. The estimate is rough and may overcount or undercount.

With **small squares**, the approximation is finer. The curve is traced more accurately, and partial squares are closer to their true fraction. The estimate converges to the true area as the squares get smaller. This is the same idea as integration in higher mathematics — the limit of finer and finer rectangles.

## Teacher Notes
- **Prior knowledge:** Lessons 061–062 (distance–time graphs), 059 (acceleration formula).
- **Differentiation:** Ages 10–13 should focus on recognising shapes (flat = steady, rising = speeding up, falling = slowing down) and counting whole squares for area. Ages 14–17 should calculate gradients and areas for triangles and trapezia, and connect to kinematic equations.
- **Local sourcing:** Graph paper can be squared exercise book paper.
