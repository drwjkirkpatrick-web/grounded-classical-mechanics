# Lesson 066: Motion of Vehicles: Odometers, Speedometers and Limits

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain how an odometer counts wheel rotations and converts them into distance travelled.
2. Explain how a speedometer converts rotations per second into road speed (rotations × circumference × time factor).
3. Calculate distance and speed from a bicycle wheel of known circumference.
4. Justify why NTSA sets speed limits, linking speed to stopping distance and road safety.

## Materials
- Bicycle wheel (a fundi's spare, or a whole bicycle turned upside down)
- Chalk or a white paint dab to mark one spoke or the tyre valve
- Tape measure or a 2 m sisal string marked in metres
- Masking tape or cellotape to fix a "counting flag" on the fork
- Chart showing NTSA speed-limit signs (hand-drawn: 50 in town, 80–110 on highways)
- Exercise books, pencil, calculator (phone calculator is fine)

## Vocabulary
- **odometer** (kikao cha umbali) — instrument that counts total distance a vehicle has travelled
- **speedometer** (kikao cha kasi) — instrument showing how fast the vehicle moves right now
- **circumference** (mzunguko) — distance around the wheel; one rotation rolls the vehicle exactly one circumference forward
- **rotation** (mzunguko wa gurudumu) — one full turn of the wheel
- **speed limit** (kikomo cha kasi) — maximum legal speed set by NTSA for a road

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What does the odometer in a matatu measure?
2. What does the speedometer show?
3. A wheel turns once. How far does the bicycle move forward?
4. Why do you think driving slower near a school is the law?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Total distance travelled (in km).
2. The current speed (in km/h).
3. Exactly one circumference of the wheel.
4. Accept: slower speed means shorter stopping distance and more time to see children crossing.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before the demo.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the chalk-marked wheel. Ask: *how does the boda's little meter "know" how far you have travelled, when it never touches the ground?* Board the mystery: it counts turns.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — class activity on the veranda or open ground. Measure the wheel's circumference directly: chalk the tyre bottom, roll one full turn along the floor, measure between chalk marks with the tape (expect ~2.1 m for a 26-inch bicycle wheel). Then groups solve on paper: (a) 100 rotations = how many metres? (b) the wheel spins 3 times per second — what distance per second, and what speed in km/h? (c) a mkokoteni wheel of circumference 1 m turns 1,000 times on the way to market — how far is that? Faster groups convert all answers to km and km/h.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board: distance = rotations × circumference; speed = rotations per second × circumference. An odometer is a counter of rotations; a speedometer is a counter of rotations *per second* — old ones used a spinning magnet and cable from the wheel, new ones use a sensor. Then safety physics: at double speed, stopping distance is roughly four times longer (it grows with v²), and a driver has less time to react — that is why NTSA posts 50 km/h near schools and towns and why the NTSA Act penalises speeding.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark from the board key.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *every meter on the dashboard is just a wheel-counter doing our lesson's maths*. Homework: ask a boda or matatu driver to read the odometer before and after a trip and bring the difference to class.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What does an odometer measure, and in what units? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A wheel rolls once with no slipping. How far does the vehicle move? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A bicycle wheel has circumference 2 m. It turns 500 times on the way to the duka. Find the distance in metres and in km.
4. The same wheel turns 4 times per second. Find the bicycle's speed in m/s and in km/h.
5. Challenge: state one physical reason and one legal reason for speed limits near schools.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Total distance travelled, in kilometres.
2. One circumference of the wheel.
3. Distance = 500 × 2 m = 1,000 m = 1 km.
4. Speed = 4 × 2 = 8 m/s; 8 × 3.6 = 28.8 km/h.
5. Physical: higher speed means much longer stopping distance (and less reaction time). Legal: NTSA sets and enforces limits (e.g., 50 km/h in towns); breaking them is an offence.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A wheel of circumference 2.1 m turns 3 times per second. Compute the road speed in km/h.
   **Worked Answer:** Speed = rotations per second × circumference = 3 × 2.1 = 6.3 m/s. Convert: 6.3 × 3.6 = 22.68 ≈ 22.7 km/h.
2. **Q:** A matatu tyre has circumference 2.2 m. Its odometer cable counts rotations. How many rotations must it count for the odometer to advance 1 km? Give your answer to the nearest whole turn.
   **Worked Answer:** Rotations = distance / circumference = 1,000 m / 2.2 m ≈ 454.5, so about 455 turns per kilometre. Real odometers gear this count down before driving the number drum.
3. **Q:** A worn tyre is 4% smaller in diameter than a new one. The speedometer still assumes the new circumference. At a true speed of 100 km/h, what does the speedometer read, and why does this matter for NTSA checks?
   **Worked Answer:** Smaller wheel = smaller circumference, so the wheel must spin MORE times per second for the same road speed: the speedometer over-reads. Reading = 100 / 0.96 ≈ 104 km/h. A driver with bald tyres can be over the limit while the meter seems legal — one reason NTSA inspects tyres.

## Safety Notes
- Spinning spokes catch fingers and braids — only the teacher spins the wheel; learners count at arm's length.
- An upside-down bicycle can topple — two learners steady the frame before any spinning.
- Roll-the-wheel measurements happen away from the road; mark the area with stones and keep to the veranda or field.
- Chalk and paint dab only on the tyre, never on brake surfaces.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 061–064 (speed = distance/time) and Lesson 065 (uniform motion); this lesson applies v = d/t to rolling wheels and feeds UP-FE and JS-FE numeracy outcomes.
- Younger learners (10–13): keep everything in metres and whole-number rotations; the story of "the meter counts turns" is the win. Older learners (14–17): full m/s ↔ km/h conversions (×3.6, ÷3.6) and the Advanced Questions, including the v² stopping-distance idea.
- Sourcing: any bicycle fundi will lend a wheel for a lesson; a 26-inch wheel gives a circumference near 2.1 m — measure, don't assume, because tyre size varies.
- No wheel at all? Use a debe lid rolled along the floor — same physics, smaller numbers.
- Local anchor: matatu odometers matter when buying second-hand vehicles (high km = worn engine); learners can ask why a low-km vehicle costs more.
- CBE note: the rotation-counting worksheet is evidence for the "application of measurement" competency; collect one per group.
