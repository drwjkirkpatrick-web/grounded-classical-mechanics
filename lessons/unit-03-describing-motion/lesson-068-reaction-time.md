# Lesson 068: Reaction Time: The Ruler-Drop Test

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define reaction time as the delay between a signal appearing and the body responding.
2. Catch a dropped ruler cleanly and read the distance it fell before the catch.
3. Convert catch distance to reaction time using d = ½gt² (rearranged: t = √(2d/g)).
4. Collect class data, chart it, and state a typical human reaction time (~0.15–0.25 s).

## Materials
- 30 cm ruler, one per pair (wooden or plastic; a flat mbao offcut marked in cm works)
- Chart paper or the board, for the class results table and bar chart
- Marker pens or charcoal for the chart
- Conversion table prepared by the teacher: catch distance (cm) → reaction time (s), from t = √(2d/g) with g = 10
- Exercise books, pencil, calculator (phone calculator is fine)
- Optional: stool so the dropper's arm rests at a steady height

## Vocabulary
- **reaction time** (muda wa kuitikia) — delay between seeing a signal and responding
- **free fall** (kuanguka huru) — falling under gravity alone, speeding up at g
- **d = ½gt²** (kanuni ya kuanguka) — distance fallen from rest in time t under gravity
- **catch distance** (umbali wa kunasa) — how far the ruler fell before your fingers closed
- **average** (wastani) — sum of trials divided by number of trials; smooths out lucky catches

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What is reaction time?
2. A stone falls from rest. Does it cover equal distances in equal times, or more distance each second?
3. A driver sees a child run into the road. Name two things that must happen before the car begins to brake.
4. Rearrange d = ½gt² to make t the subject.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The delay between a signal appearing and the body responding.
2. More distance each second — it accelerates (d grows with t²).
3. The brain must see and decide (reaction time), then the foot must move and press the brake.
4. t = √(2d/g).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before the demo.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — tell the braking story: at 20 m/s (about 72 km/h), a matatu travels 4 m during even a quick 0.2 s reaction time — before the brake is touched. Ask: *can we measure that 0.2 s in class?* Hold up a 30 cm ruler: *yes, with this.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs run the ruler-drop test (see Hands-On Build). Each learner catches 5 times, records each catch distance, and takes their average. Younger learners read their reaction time straight off the teacher's conversion table; older learners compute t = √(2d/g) themselves. Pairs report average times to the board, and the class builds a bar chart of everyone's results.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate: the ruler is in free fall, covering d = ½gt² before the catch — so a shorter catch means a faster brain. Typical class values cluster around 0.15–0.25 s; tiredness, distraction and age all shift it. Link back to the road: reaction distance = speed × reaction time, which is why NTSA says don't use a phone while driving — a glance away adds a second or more.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; peer-mark from the board key.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the faster you catch, the shorter the fall — your brain has a speedometer made of gravity*. Homework: test two people at home (a grandparent, a sibling) and bring their average catch distances.

## Hands-On Build: Ruler-Drop Reaction Tester
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; prepare the conversion table the day before.*

### Parts
- 30 cm ruler — 1 per pair — any duka; a flat mbao offcut marked in cm is a fine substitute
- Conversion chart — 1 per class — teacher-made from t = √(2d/g), g = 10 (e.g., 5 cm → 0.10 s, 10 cm → 0.14 s, 15 cm → 0.17 s, 20 cm → 0.20 s, 25 cm → 0.22 s)
- Chart paper — 1 sheet — for the class bar chart; the board works too
- Stool or desk edge — optional — steadies the dropper's arm height

### Workflow
1. Catcher sits with forearm on the desk, thumb and first finger open about 3 cm apart, hanging over the edge.
2. Dropper holds the ruler vertically so the 0 cm mark sits level with the top of the catcher's open fingers.
3. **Checkpoint:** do two practice drops where the catcher is told "now" — confirm the fingers close cleanly and the ruler is caught, not slapped. Only then start blind drops.
4. Without warning, the dropper releases the ruler (no countdown, no warning eyes). Catcher closes fingers the instant they SEE it move.
5. Read the mark level with the top of the fingers: that is the catch distance d. Record it.
6. Repeat 5 times per catcher, then swap roles. Compute each person's average d.
7. Convert average d to reaction time with the chart or with t = √(2d/g), and add your result to the class chart.

### Common Mistakes
- **Starting the ruler too high** — the 0 mark must sit right at the fingers, or every distance is inflated.
- **Counting down "3-2-1"** — the catcher anticipates; drops must be silent and unannounced for a true reaction.
- **Grabbing early with a half-closed hand** — fingers start OPEN; a ready-pinched hand measures nothing.
- **Using one lucky catch** — a single fast grab is luck, not speed; always average 5 trials.
- **Reading the bottom of the fingers** — read the top edge consistently, or results shift by 1–2 cm.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Define reaction time in one sentence. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Rearrange d = ½gt² to make t the subject. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A learner catches the ruler at 20 cm. Use the chart (or t = √(2d/g)) to find the reaction time.
4. Class results: 0.18, 0.20, 0.22, 0.19, 0.21 s. Find the class average reaction time.
5. Challenge: a boda travels at 15 m/s and the rider's reaction time is 0.3 s. How far does the boda move before the rider even touches the brake?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The delay between a signal appearing and the body responding to it.
2. t = √(2d/g).
3. d = 0.20 m: t = √(2 × 0.20 / 10) = √0.04 = 0.20 s.
4. Sum = 1.00 s; average = 1.00 / 5 = 0.20 s.
5. Reaction distance = speed × time = 15 × 0.3 = 4.5 m — travelled before braking even starts.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
*For older or faster learners. Worked solutions included for the teacher.*

1. **Q:** A learner catches the ruler at 18 cm. Convert this into a reaction time using d = ½gt² with g = 10 m/s². Show the full working.
   **Worked Answer:** d = 18 cm = 0.18 m. From d = ½gt², t = √(2d/g) = √(2 × 0.18 / 10) = √0.036 = 0.19 s (0.036 = 0.19² ≈ 0.0361 ✓).
2. **Q:** Learner A averages 0.16 s and learner B averages 0.25 s. They ride pillion at 25 m/s (90 km/h). How many MORE metres does B's bike travel during B's reaction time than A's during A's?
   **Worked Answer:** A: 25 × 0.16 = 4.0 m. B: 25 × 0.25 = 6.25 m. Difference = 2.25 m — roughly one car length, decided entirely by 0.09 s of brain speed.
3. **Q:** Why does doubling the catch distance NOT double the reaction time? Use the numbers 5 cm and 20 cm to make your point.
   **Worked Answer:** Because d grows with t², so t grows with √d, not with d. t(5 cm) = √(2 × 0.05/10) = √0.01 = 0.10 s; t(20 cm) = √(2 × 0.20/10) = √0.04 = 0.20 s. Four times the distance gives only double the time.

## Safety Notes
- Only lightweight wooden or plastic rulers — no metal rulers, which can pinch skin when caught.
- Clear the desk edge of pens, water bottles and phones before dropping; a flailing catch knocks things over.
- Catcher keeps eyes on the ruler, not the dropper's face — but dropper must never push the ruler DOWN, only release it.
- Sit during the test; standing catchers bend and jerk, risking bumped heads on desk corners.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lessons 061–065 (speed, acceleration ideas) and the rearrangement t = √(2d/g) is introduced here fresh — walk it through once on the board before Explore.
- Younger learners (10–13): skip the square root entirely; they measure catch distances, average them, and read times from the teacher's chart — the ranking "who is quickest" is the learning. Older learners (14–17): full t = √(2d/g) computation and the Advanced Questions.
- Prepare the conversion chart the day before (5 cm steps, g = 10): it is the bridge that lets 10-year-olds touch a real physics formula.
- No rulers? Mark centimetres on a 30 cm mbao offcut or a strip of stiff mabati edging — anything straight and light works.
- Local anchors: reaction time is why matatu touts bang the roof as a signal, why phone use while riding a boda is deadly, and why goalkeepers train reflexes.
- CBE note: the 5-trial table, average and class bar chart are evidence for the "data collection and representation" competency; file each pair's table.
