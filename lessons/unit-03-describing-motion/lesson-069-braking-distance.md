# Lesson 069: Thinking and Braking Distance

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Distinguish between thinking distance and braking distance.
2. Explain why reaction time increases thinking distance.
3. Calculate total stopping distance from given data.

## Materials
- Toy car or small wheeled object
- A ramp or inclined plank
- Rulers or tape measures
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Thinking distance** *(umbali wa kufikiria)* — the distance travelled while the driver reacts before braking.
- **Braking distance** *(umbali wa kukaza)* — the distance travelled while the brakes are applied.
- **Stopping distance** *(umbali wa kusimama)* — thinking distance + braking distance.
- **Reaction time** *(muda wa kujibu)* — the delay between seeing a hazard and starting to brake.

## Pre-Test
1. A driver sees a child step into the road. What happens before the brakes are applied?
2. What two distances make up the total stopping distance?
3. Does a faster vehicle have a longer or shorter stopping distance?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The driver sees the child, the brain processes the information, and the foot moves to the brake pedal — this takes reaction time.
2. Thinking distance and braking distance.
3. Longer — both thinking distance and braking distance increase with speed.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "A boda-boda rider sees a chicken run into the road. How far does the boda travel before it stops?" (Expected guesses.) Explain that stopping involves two phases.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, set up a toy car on a gentle ramp.
   - Roll the car down the ramp onto a flat surface. Place a "hazard" (a stone or chalk mark) 1 m from the ramp base.
   - Measure where the car stops when released from different heights (simulating different speeds).
   - **Key discovery:** Higher release = faster speed = longer stopping distance. The car travels some distance before "reacting" (the ramp end represents the reaction point), then continues to the stop.
   - Discuss: at what speed would the car hit the hazard? What if the hazard was closer?
   - Groups sketch the two phases on paper: thinking distance (car still at ramp speed) and braking distance (car slowing down).

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Thinking distance** = speed × reaction time. At 20 m/s with 0.7 s reaction time: thinking distance = 20 × 0.7 = **14 m**.
   - **Braking distance** depends on speed squared and deceleration. Rough rule: double the speed = quadruple the braking distance.
   - **Stopping distance** = thinking distance + braking distance.
   - Kenyan context: matatus tailgating on highways; bodas weaving through traffic. At 30 m/s (108 km/h), even a 0.5 s reaction time means 15 m of thinking distance before brakes engage.
   - Safety message: keep safe following distances. The "two-second rule" — stay at least two seconds behind the vehicle in front.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains why tailgating is dangerous in terms of stopping distance. Homework: observe vehicles on a road — estimate the gap between a matatu and the vehicle in front. Is it enough?

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* A driver sees a hazard. What happens during the thinking distance?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* A car doubles its speed. What happens to its stopping distance?
3. Calculate the thinking distance for a vehicle at 15 m/s with a reaction time of 0.8 s.
4. A boda-boda travels at 10 m/s. The rider's reaction time is 0.6 s, and the braking distance is 8 m. What is the total stopping distance?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The driver sees the hazard, processes it, and moves to brake — the vehicle continues at constant speed during this time.
2. The stopping distance **more than doubles** — thinking distance doubles, and braking distance approximately quadruples.
3. Thinking distance = 15 m/s × 0.8 s = **12 m**.
4. Thinking distance = 10 m/s × 0.6 s = 6 m. Total stopping distance = 6 m + 8 m = **14 m**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A vehicle travels at 20 m/s. The driver's reaction time is 0.7 s, and the vehicle can decelerate at 4 m/s². Calculate (a) the thinking distance, (b) the braking distance, and (c) the total stopping distance.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) Thinking distance = speed × reaction time = 20 m/s × 0.7 s = **14 m**.

(b) Braking distance: using v² = u² + 2as, where v = 0 (stopped), u = 20 m/s, a = −4 m/s²:
0 = 20² + 2(−4)s
0 = 400 − 8s
8s = 400
s = **50 m**.

(c) Total stopping distance = 14 m + 50 m = **64 m**.

This is why tailgating at highway speeds is lethal — at 20 m/s (72 km/h), you need over 60 m to stop. Most drivers follow at only 20–30 m.

**Q:** Explain why thinking distance increases linearly with speed, while braking distance increases with the square of speed. Use the formulas to justify your answer.

**Worked Answer:**
**Thinking distance:** d_think = v × t_reaction. Since t_reaction is roughly constant for a given driver, d_think is directly proportional to v. Double the speed → double the thinking distance (linear).

**Braking distance:** Using v² = u² + 2as with final velocity v = 0:
0 = u² + 2as → s = −u² / 2a.
Since a (deceleration) is constant for a given vehicle and road surface, s_brake ∝ u². Double the speed → quadruple the braking distance (quadratic).

This is why high-speed crashes are so much worse than low-speed ones: the stopping distance grows faster than the speed. A small increase in speed causes a large increase in the distance needed to stop safely.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the toy car ramp area is clear of feet and obstacles.
- Do not roll cars toward other learners.
- The hazard demonstration is theoretical — do not place actual obstacles in traffic.

## Teacher Notes
- **Prior knowledge:** Lessons 057 (average vs instantaneous speed), 060 (reaction time measurement).
- **Differentiation:** Ages 10–13 should understand the two-phase model and why faster means longer stopping distance. Ages 14–17 should calculate stopping distances using formulas and understand the linear vs quadratic relationships.
- **Local sourcing:** Toy cars from the market or home-made wheeled bottles work. Any plank or mabati sheet serves as a ramp.
- **CBE assessment note:** Aligns with JS-FE "analysing stopping distances" and UP-FE "applying kinematic equations to road safety."
