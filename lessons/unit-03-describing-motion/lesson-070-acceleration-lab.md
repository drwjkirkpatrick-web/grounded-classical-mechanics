# Lesson 070: Acceleration Lab: Toy Car on a Ramp

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Measure the time a toy car takes to travel down ramps of different steepness.
2. Conclude that steeper ramps produce greater acceleration.
3. Plot results and estimate acceleration from distance and time data.

## Materials
- A ramp board or plank (at least 1 m long)
- Books or bricks for adjusting ramp height
- A toy car or home-made wheeled bottle
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- A tape measure or ruler
- Graph paper
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Ramp steepness** *(ukali wa mteremko)* — how steep the incline is; greater steepness means greater acceleration.
- **Acceleration** *(kasi ya kuharakisha)* — rate of change of velocity; here, caused by gravity along the ramp.
- **Controlled variable** *(kigezo kinachodhibitiwa)* — a factor kept the same to ensure a fair test.
- **Independent variable** *(kigezo kinachobadilishwa)* — the factor deliberately changed; here, ramp steepness.

## Pre-Test
1. What happens to a toy car's speed as it rolls down a ramp?
2. If you make the ramp steeper, will the car take more or less time to reach the bottom?
3. What should you keep the same each time to make the test fair?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. It increases — the car accelerates down the ramp.
2. Less time — steeper ramp means greater acceleration.
3. The same car, the same release point, the same distance measured.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Show the ramp and car. Ask, "Will the car go faster down a steep ramp or a gentle ramp? How can we prove it?" Introduce the idea of a controlled experiment.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, set up the ramp at three different steepnesses (e.g., 1 book, 2 books, 3 books under one end).
   - Mark a start line and a finish line 80 cm apart on the ramp.
   - Release the car from rest at the start line. Time how long it takes to reach the finish line.
   - Repeat three times for each steepness. Record all times.
   - **Key discovery:** Steeper ramps produce shorter times. The car accelerates more on steeper ramps.
   - Calculate average speed for each steepness: v_avg = 0.80 m / average time.
   - Groups sketch a simple graph: steepness (number of books) vs average speed.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Why steeper = faster:** Gravity pulls the car down the ramp. The steeper the ramp, the larger the component of gravity along the ramp, so the greater the acceleration.
   - **Fair test:** Only change one thing (steepness). Keep the same: car, distance, release method, surface.
   - **Estimating acceleration:** Using the kinematic equation s = ut + ½at² with u = 0 (released from rest): a = 2s / t².
   - Example: if s = 0.80 m and t = 1.6 s, then a = 2 × 0.80 / (1.6)² = 1.6 / 2.56 = **0.625 m/s²**.
   - Connect to Lesson 059: acceleration is the rate of change of velocity; here we see it caused by gravity.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states what makes a ramp experiment fair and what was discovered. Homework: predict how a heavier car would compare on the same ramp.

## Hands-On Build: Ramp Acceleration Lab

### Parts
- Plank or mabati sheet — 1 m — classroom or jua kali
- Books or bricks — 3–5 — classroom
- Toy car or wheeled bottle — 1 — home or market
- Stopwatch — 1 — phone or classroom
- Tape measure — 1 — classroom
- Graph paper — 1 sheet — exercise book

### Workflow
1. Place the plank flat on a table. Mark a start line 10 cm from one end and a finish line 80 cm from the start.
2. Place one book under the end near the start line to create a gentle slope.
3. Release the car from rest at the start line. Time how long it takes to reach the finish line.
4. Repeat three times. Calculate the average time.
5. Add a second book under the end to steepen the slope. Repeat the timing three times.
6. Add a third book. Repeat again.
7. Calculate average speed for each steepness: v_avg = 0.80 m / t_avg.
8. **Checkpoint:** Before calculating, check that your times are consistent (all three within 0.3 s of each other). If one time is very different, repeat the run.

### Common Mistakes
- **Pushing the car instead of releasing from rest:** This adds extra speed not caused by gravity. Fix: hold the car gently and release without pushing.
- **Changing the release point:** This alters the distance and makes comparisons unfair. Fix: always release from the marked start line.
- **Timing from different points:** The timer should start when the car is released and stop when the front wheels cross the finish line. Fix: agree on the start and stop signals before timing.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What happens to a toy car's speed as it rolls down a ramp?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* A ramp is made steeper. Does the car take more or less time to reach the bottom?
3. A car travels 0.80 m down a ramp in 2.0 s. Calculate its average speed.
4. Why is it important to use the same car each time in this experiment?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The speed **increases** — the car accelerates.
2. **Less time** — steeper ramp means greater acceleration.
3. Average speed = 0.80 m / 2.0 s = **0.40 m/s**.
4. Different cars have different masses and wheel friction. Using the same car keeps these factors constant, so only steepness affects the result.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A toy car rolls 0.80 m down a ramp in 1.6 s, starting from rest. Using s = ut + ½at², estimate the acceleration. Explain why this is only an estimate, not the true acceleration.

**Worked Answer:**
Given: s = 0.80 m, t = 1.6 s, u = 0 m/s.
Using s = ut + ½at²:
0.80 = 0 + ½ × a × (1.6)²
0.80 = ½ × a × 2.56
0.80 = 1.28a
a = 0.80 / 1.28 = **0.625 m/s²**.

This is an **estimate** because:
- The equation assumes constant acceleration, but friction and air resistance may cause slight variations.
- The car may not be released exactly from rest (a tiny push changes u).
- Reaction time in starting and stopping the stopwatch introduces timing error.
- The ramp may not be perfectly straight or uniform.

For a true acceleration, one would need motion sensors or video analysis to measure instantaneous velocities at multiple points.

**Q:** Using v² = 2as with your measured distance and the time above, estimate the final speed of the car at the bottom of the ramp. Compare this with the average speed and explain why they differ.

**Worked Answer:**
v² = 2as = 2 × 0.625 m/s² × 0.80 m = 1.0 m²/s²
v = √1.0 = **1.0 m/s** (final speed at bottom).

Average speed = total distance / total time = 0.80 m / 1.6 s = **0.50 m/s**.

They differ because:
- **Final speed (1.0 m/s)** is the speed at the instant the car reaches the bottom — its maximum speed.
- **Average speed (0.50 m/s)** is the mean speed over the whole journey. For constant acceleration from rest, average speed = (initial speed + final speed) / 2 = (0 + 1.0) / 2 = 0.50 m/s. This confirms the result.

The car starts slow and ends fast, so the average is halfway between the start and end values. This is a general result for motion with constant acceleration.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the ramp landing area is clear and flat.
- Do not stand at the bottom of the ramp where the car may roll out.
- Books used to prop the ramp should be stable and not slide.
- If using a plank with splinters, sand the edges beforehand.
