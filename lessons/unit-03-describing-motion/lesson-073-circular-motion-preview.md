# Lesson 073: Circular Motion Preview: Speed Constant, Velocity Changing

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State that circular motion involves changing velocity even when speed is constant.
2. Explain that the direction of motion continuously changes in a circle.
3. Identify circular motion in everyday Kenyan contexts.

## Materials
- A string (about 1 m)
- A soft ball or stone wrapped in cloth
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Circular motion** *(mazunguko)* — motion along a circular path.
- **Tangent** *(mstari wa kugusa)* — a line that touches a circle at one point; the direction of velocity in circular motion.
- **Centripetal acceleration** *(kasi ya kielekeo cha kati)* — acceleration directed toward the centre of a circular path.
- **Constant speed** *(kasi isiyobadilika)* — moving at the same rate, but direction may change.

## Pre-Test
1. A child spins a ball on a string in a circle. Is the ball's speed changing?
2. Is the ball's velocity changing? Explain.
3. Name three examples of circular motion you see every day.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Not necessarily — the speed can be constant.
2. Yes — the direction keeps changing, so the velocity changes.
3. Examples: bicycle wheel, spinning top, satellite dish pointing, merry-go-round, clock hands.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Swing the ball on the string in a horizontal circle. Ask, "Is the ball accelerating?" (Expected: no — it is not speeding up or slowing down.) Then ask, "Is its velocity changing?" (Expected: confusion.) Introduce the idea that velocity has direction.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, one learner swings the ball on a string in a circle. Another learner traces the path on paper. A third marks the direction of motion at four points on the circle (tangent directions). The fourth records observations.
   - **Key discovery:** At each point on the circle, the ball is moving in a different direction. The velocity vector is always tangent to the circle.
   - Groups draw arrows showing velocity at four points. All arrows have the same length (same speed) but different directions.
   - Discuss: if velocity is changing, there must be acceleration (from Unit 2: F = ma, acceleration requires force). The string pulls the ball toward the centre — this is the force causing the circular motion.
   - **Connection:** A boda-boda turning a corner leans inward because the tyres must push the rider toward the centre of the turn. A matatu on a roundabout experiences the same force.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - In circular motion, **speed is constant** but **velocity is not** — direction changes continuously.
   - **Acceleration toward the centre:** Called centripetal acceleration. It is caused by a force (tension in the string, friction on tyres, gravity for satellites).
   - Formula (teaser): a = v²/r. Faster speed or smaller radius means larger acceleration.
   - Kenyan examples: a bicycle wheel spinning, a posho mill stone rotating, a satellite orbiting Earth, a child on a merry-go-round, a matatu navigating a roundabout.
   - Preview: Unit 10 will explore circular motion in more depth.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains why circular motion is accelerated motion. Homework: find three examples of circular motion at home and describe what provides the centre-pointing force.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* A bicycle wheel spins at constant speed. Is the rim accelerating?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Why is circular motion considered accelerated motion even when speed does not change?
3. Draw velocity arrows (same length) at four points on a circle, each tangent to the circle.
4. What force keeps a ball moving in a circle when swung on a string?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Yes** — the direction of each point on the rim keeps changing.
2. Because **velocity includes direction**. Even though speed is constant, the direction changes, so velocity changes → acceleration exists.
3. Four arrows of equal length, each touching the circle at one point and pointing along the tangent.
4. **Tension in the string** pulling toward the centre.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Argue why "no acceleration at constant speed" fails for circular motion, using velocity vectors drawn at two opposite points on the circle.

**Worked Answer:**
At two opposite points on a circle (e.g., top and bottom), the velocity vectors point in **opposite directions**.

- At the top: velocity points horizontally right (tangent).
- At the bottom: velocity points horizontally left (tangent).

Even though the speed (magnitude) is the same at both points, the velocity vectors are different. Since velocity is changing, there must be acceleration.

The average acceleration between these two points points toward the centre. For a full circle, this happens at every instant — the acceleration is always directed inward. This is centripetal acceleration.

The "no acceleration at constant speed" rule only applies to **straight-line motion**. As soon as the path curves, velocity changes direction and acceleration appears.

**Q:** A boda-boda navigates a roundabout of radius 10 m at 5 m/s. Calculate the centripetal acceleration using a = v²/r. Explain why the rider feels pushed outward, even though the acceleration is inward.

**Worked Answer:**
a = v² / r = (5 m/s)² / 10 m = 25 / 10 = **2.5 m/s²** toward the centre.

The rider feels pushed outward because of **inertia** (Newton's First Law). The rider's body wants to continue in a straight line, but the boda is turning. The tyres push the boda inward, but the rider's body resists this change and appears to "lean" or be pushed outward. This is not a real outward force — it is the rider's body trying to maintain straight-line motion while the vehicle turns. The real force is the inward friction between tyres and road, and the inward push of the seat/seatback on the rider.

This is why passengers in a turning matatu are thrown toward the outside of the turn — their bodies continue straight while the vehicle curves.

## Safety Notes
- Use a soft ball wrapped in cloth; avoid hard stones that could injure if the string breaks.
- Swing in an open area away from other learners.
- Ensure the string is strong and securely tied.
- Do not swing rapidly — a gentle, steady circle is sufficient.
