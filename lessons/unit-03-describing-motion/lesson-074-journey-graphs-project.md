# Lesson 074: Project: Graph Your Journey to School

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** journey-graph

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Record time and position data for a real journey.
2. Plot a distance–time graph from collected data.
3. Identify the fastest and slowest legs of the journey from the graph.

## Materials
- A notebook for recording times and positions
- A watch or phone for timing
- Graph paper or squared paper
- A pencil and ruler

## Vocabulary
- **Landmark** *(alama)* — a fixed point along a journey used to record position and time.
- **Leg** *(sehemu)* — one section of a journey between two landmarks.
- **Journey graph** *(mchoro wa safari)* — a distance–time graph showing an entire trip.
- **Presentation** *(mawasiliano)* — sharing results with the class.

## Pre-Test
1. What landmarks might you pass on your way to school?
2. How could you measure the time between landmarks without a stopwatch?
3. What would a very steep section of your journey graph mean?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Examples: home gate, borehole, mango tree, church, school gate.
2. Counting seconds ("one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi"), using a phone clock, or counting steps at a steady pace.
3. A very steep section means you were moving very fast during that leg.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands and list landmarks on the board.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "Who walks to school? Who takes a boda? Who rides a bicycle?" Explain that today's project turns any journey into a physics graph.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In pairs, learners plan their journey to school (or another familiar route).
   - They identify 4–6 landmarks along the way (e.g., home → corner shop → bridge → market → school).
   - For each landmark, they estimate the distance from home and the time taken to reach it.
   - If data was collected beforehand (homework from Lesson 061), they use real measurements.
   - Pairs plot the distance–time graph on graph paper.
   - **Key discovery:** The steepest section of the graph is the fastest leg. A horizontal section means a stop (e.g., waiting for a friend).
   - Groups compare graphs: who has the fastest leg? Who has the longest stop?

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - The gradient of each leg = speed for that section.
   - Compare legs: "From home to the shop took 5 minutes for 200 m → speed = 40 m/min. From the shop to the bridge took 3 minutes for 300 m → speed = 100 m/min. The second leg was faster."
   - Discuss why some legs are slower: hills, rough ground, crowds, carrying loads.
   - Kenyan context: a mkokoteni cart journey is slower than a boda-boda journey for the same distance. Walking on a dirt road after rain is slower than on a dry day.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One pair presents their graph to the class for 30 seconds. Homework: refine the graph with actual timings on tomorrow's journey to school.

## Hands-On Build: Journey Graph

### Parts
- Notebook — 1 — classroom
- Watch or phone — 1 — home
- Graph paper — 1 sheet — exercise book
- Pencil and ruler — 1 each — classroom

### Workflow
1. Choose a familiar journey (e.g., home to school, or home to market).
2. List 4–6 landmarks along the route.
3. Walk the journey and record the time at each landmark. Use a watch or count seconds steadily.
4. Estimate or measure the distance from home to each landmark (using pacing or known distances).
5. Plot a distance–time graph: time on the horizontal axis, distance from home on the vertical axis.
6. Label each leg with its speed (gradient = distance/time).
7. Identify the fastest and slowest legs.
8. **Checkpoint:** Before presenting, check that your graph starts at (0, 0) — home at time zero — and that each point is labelled with its landmark name.

### Common Mistakes
- **Starting the graph at the wrong point:** The first point should be (0 min, 0 m) — you start at home. Fix: always begin at the origin.
- **Mixing up axes:** Putting time on the vertical axis. Fix: remember "time goes across, distance goes up."
- **Forgetting a stop:** If you waited at a stage, the graph should have a horizontal section. Fix: record the waiting time and plot it.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* A section of your journey graph is very steep. What does this mean?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* List three landmarks you might use for a journey graph from your home to the market.
3. Your graph shows a horizontal section between 10 minutes and 15 minutes. What happened during this time?
4. You walk 400 m in 8 minutes, then wait 2 minutes, then walk 600 m in 12 minutes. Calculate the speed of each walking leg.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. You were moving **very fast** during that leg.
2. Examples: home gate, borehole, crossroads, market entrance.
3. You were **stationary** (stopped) for 5 minutes.
4. First leg: 400 m / 8 min = **50 m/min** (or 0.83 m/s). Second leg: 600 m / 12 min = **50 m/min** (same speed). The wait has zero speed.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** You record your journey to school with times accurate to ±30 seconds over 8-minute legs. Estimate the percentage uncertainty in your speed measurement and explain how you could reduce it.

**Worked Answer:**
Percentage uncertainty in time = (30 s / 480 s) × 100 = **6.25 percent**.

Since speed = distance / time, the percentage uncertainty in speed is approximately the same as the percentage uncertainty in time (assuming distance is measured accurately): **±6 percent**.

For a leg of 400 m in 8 min (480 s), speed = 400 / 480 = 0.833 m/s. Uncertainty = ±6 percent of 0.833 = **±0.05 m/s**.

To reduce uncertainty:
- Use a stopwatch instead of counting seconds (±1 s instead of ±30 s).
- Measure distance with a tape measure instead of pacing.
- Take multiple journeys and average the times.
- Use shorter legs (e.g., 2-minute legs instead of 8-minute) — the ±30 s error becomes a smaller percentage of the total time.

**Q:** Design a zero-instrument speed survey for vehicles on your local road with ±20 percent accuracy. Describe the method, the validation checks, and the limitations.

**Worked Answer:**
**Method:**
1. Mark two points on the road verge exactly 50 m apart (use pacing calibrated against a known distance).
2. Stand safely at the first point. When a vehicle passes, start counting seconds ("one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi...").
3. Stop counting when the vehicle passes the second point.
4. Speed = 50 m / time in seconds.

**Validation checks:**
- Calibrate your counting: time yourself counting to 60 and compare with a watch. Adjust your pace if needed.
- Test on a known speed: ask a friend to walk at a known pace and verify your timing.
- Repeat the measurement three times for the same vehicle (if possible) and check consistency.

**Limitations:**
- Human reaction time: starting and stopping the count introduces ±0.3 s error. For a vehicle taking 4 s to cover 50 m, this is ±7.5 percent.
- Distance error: pacing may be off by ±2 m over 50 m (±4 percent).
- Combined error: √(7.5² + 4²) ≈ ±8.5 percent — within the ±20 percent target.
- Cannot measure individual vehicle types accurately; best for average traffic speed.
- Dangerous to stand near busy roads; choose a quiet section.

## Safety Notes
- Learners should plan journeys on safe, familiar routes.
- Do not time vehicles while standing in the road — use the verge.
- If walking to school in the dark, pair up with a buddy.
