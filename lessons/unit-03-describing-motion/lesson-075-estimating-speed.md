# Lesson 075: Estimating Speed Without Instruments

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Estimate speed using calibrated pacing and counting methods.
2. Use kilometre markers and time counting to estimate vehicle speeds.
3. Judge whether an estimate is reasonable for a given situation.

## Materials
- A tape measure or known distance (e.g., 100 m)
- A watch or phone timer
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Calibrated pacing** *(hatua zilizohakikiwa)* — walking at a known stride length to measure distance.
- **Kilometre marker** *(alama ya kilomita)* — a post on the roadside showing distance along a highway.
- **Estimate** *(kukisia)* — a rough but sensible calculation.
- **Order of magnitude** *(kipimo cha ukubwa)* — the approximate size of a number (e.g., tens, hundreds).

## Pre-Test
1. How can you estimate the length of your stride without a ruler?
2. If you count 60 seconds while a matatu passes two kilometre markers, what is its speed?
3. Is a bicycle speed of 100 km/h reasonable?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Walk a known distance (e.g., 10 m) and count steps; stride length = distance / steps.
2. Speed = 1 km / 1 min = 60 km/h.
3. No — that is highway speed for a car, not a bicycle.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "You are standing by the road. A boda-boda passes. How fast is it going? You have no speedometer — what do you do?" Brainstorm methods.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners calibrate their pacing.
   - Walk 20 m at normal pace. Count steps. Calculate stride length = 20 m / number of steps.
   - Now estimate: how many steps to the borehole? Multiply by stride length.
   - **Activity:** Stand by a road with kilometre markers (or use a known 100 m distance). Time vehicles between markers.
   - Example: a matatu takes 45 s between two markers 1 km apart. Speed = 1 km / 45 s = 1 km / 0.0125 h = **80 km/h**.
   - Groups compare estimates for different vehicles: boda, bicycle, matatu, lorry.
   - **Key discovery:** Bodas are faster than bicycles but slower than matatus. Lorries on highways are fastest but slowest uphill.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Calibrated pacing:** Know your stride (e.g., 0.7 m). Count steps to estimate distance.
   - **Time counting:** "One-Mississippi, two-Mississippi..." is roughly 1 second per word. Practice against a watch.
   - **Kilometre markers:** Common on major roads in Kenya. Time between markers gives speed directly.
   - **Reasonableness checks:**
     - Walking: 4–6 km/h (about 1 m/s).
     - Cycling: 15–25 km/h.
     - Boda-boda: 30–60 km/h.
     - Matatu: 40–100 km/h depending on road.
     - Lorry: 60–90 km/h on highways.
   - If your estimate is outside these ranges, recheck your timing or distance.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states their most useful estimation trick. Homework: estimate the speed of three vehicles on your road using the kilometre marker method.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* You need to measure 50 m but have no tape. How can you use your stride?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* A bicycle passes two kilometre markers 90 s apart. Estimate its speed.
3. A boda-boda takes 20 s to pass a 100 m stretch. What is its speed in m/s and km/h?
4. List one reason why your estimate might be inaccurate.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Calibrate your stride (e.g., 0.7 m), then count the steps needed to cover 50 m (≈71 steps).
2. Speed = 1 km / 90 s = 1 km / 0.025 h = **40 km/h**.
3. Speed = 100 m / 20 s = **5 m/s** = 5 × 3.6 = **18 km/h**.
4. Examples: reaction time error, miscounting steps, uneven stride, vehicle speeding up or slowing down.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Design a zero-instrument speed survey for vehicles on your local road with ±20 percent accuracy. Describe the method, validation checks, and limitations.

**Worked Answer:**
**Method:**
1. Mark two points on the road verge exactly 50 m apart (use pacing calibrated against a known distance).
2. Stand safely at the first point. When a vehicle passes, start counting seconds ("one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi...").
3. Stop counting when the vehicle passes the second point.
4. Speed = 50 m / time in seconds.

**Validation checks:**
- Calibrate counting: time yourself counting to 60 and compare with a watch. Adjust pace if needed.
- Test on a known speed: ask a friend to walk at a known pace and verify your timing.
- Repeat three times for the same vehicle and check consistency.

**Limitations:**
- Human reaction time: ±0.3 s error. For a vehicle taking 4 s to cover 50 m, this is ±7.5 percent.
- Distance error: pacing may be off by ±2 m over 50 m (±4 percent).
- Combined error: √(7.5² + 4²) ≈ ±8.5 percent — within the ±20 percent target.
- Cannot measure individual vehicle types accurately; best for average traffic speed.
- Dangerous to stand near busy roads; choose a quiet section.

**Q:** You estimate a matatu's speed as 80 km/h using kilometre markers and a phone timer. The timer has ±1 s accuracy, and your distance estimate (between markers) has ±5 percent uncertainty. Calculate the combined percentage uncertainty in your speed estimate.

**Worked Answer:**
For speed = distance / time, percentage uncertainties add in quadrature (square root of sum of squares):

- Distance uncertainty: ±5 percent.
- Time uncertainty: for a vehicle covering 1 km at 80 km/h, time = 1/80 h = 45 s. Timer accuracy ±1 s → percentage = (1/45) × 100 = ±2.22 percent.

Combined uncertainty = √(5² + 2.22²) = √(25 + 4.93) = √29.93 ≈ **±5.47 percent**.

So the speed estimate is **80 ± 4.4 km/h**.

This is well within the ±20 percent target. The dominant source of error is the distance uncertainty, not the timer.

## Safety Notes
- Never stand in the road to time vehicles. Use the verge or a safe distance back.
- Pair up — one learner watches traffic while the other counts.
- Avoid busy highways; choose quieter roads.
- If no kilometre markers exist, use a known distance (e.g., between two trees) calibrated with a tape measure.
