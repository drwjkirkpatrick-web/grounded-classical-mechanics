# Lesson 076: Motion of Animals: Cheetahs, Goats and Us

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** UP-FE;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Compare the speeds and gaits of different animals and humans.
2. Explain why body shape affects maximum speed and endurance.
3. Calculate whether a predator can catch prey given speed and distance data.

## Materials
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- A tape measure or known distance
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Gait** *(mwenendo)* — the pattern of movement of the limbs during locomotion.
- **Sprint** *(mbio)* — a short burst of maximum speed.
- **Endurance** *(uvumilivu)* — the ability to maintain speed over a long time.
- **Stride** *(hatua)* — the distance covered in one complete cycle of leg movement.

## Pre-Test
1. Which is faster: a cheetah or a human?
2. Why can a goat climb steep hills better than a human?
3. A cheetah runs at 30 m/s. How far does it cover in 5 seconds?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A cheetah — it is the fastest land animal.
2. Goats have evolved for climbing: flexible joints, split hooves for grip, and a low centre of mass.
3. Distance = 30 m/s × 5 s = **150 m**.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "Who has seen a goat run? A chicken? A dog? How do their movements differ?" List observations on the board.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners research (from memory, observation, or discussion) the gaits of different animals.
   - **Cheetah:** Flexible spine, long legs, claws grip the ground. Sprint: 30 m/s for ~15 s. Cannot maintain speed for long — overheats.
   - **Human:** Bipedal, endurance runner. Speed: 10 m/s (elite sprinters) for ~10 s. Marathon: 5.5 m/s for 2 hours.
   - **Goat:** Quadrupedal, sure-footed. Speed: 5 m/s on flat ground, but climbs steep terrain where humans struggle.
   - **Ostrich:** Bipedal, long legs. Speed: 20 m/s, maintains for long distances.
   - **Chicken:** Burst flight + running. Speed: 5 m/s for short bursts.
   - Groups discuss: why does each animal have its particular speed and gait? (Evolution: predators need speed; prey need endurance or agility.)
   - **Field observation:** If a goat is available, time it across a known distance (e.g., 20 m). Calculate its speed.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Speed vs endurance trade-off:** Cheetahs are sprinters; humans are endurance runners. Evolution shaped each for survival.
   - **Body shape:** Long legs = longer stride = higher speed. Flexible spine = greater stride extension. Light body = less mass to accelerate.
   - **Kenyan context:** Kenyan runners dominate long-distance races because of altitude training, body shape, and cultural emphasis on running.
   - **Predator-prey chase:** Predators rely on speed bursts; prey rely on endurance or agility. A cheetah must catch a gazelle within 15 s or it overheats.
   - Formula application: if predator speed = 30 m/s, prey speed = 20 m/s, and prey has 100 m head start, can the cheetah catch it?

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states the speed-endurance trade-off. Homework: observe an animal's gait and estimate its stride length and speed.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* A cheetah sprints at 30 m/s. A human sprints at 10 m/s. How many times faster is the cheetah?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* A goat runs at 5 m/s. How far does it cover in 12 seconds?
3. Why can a cheetah not maintain its top speed for more than about 15 seconds?
4. An ostrich runs at 20 m/s. How long does it take to cover 1 km?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 30 / 10 = **3 times faster**.
2. Distance = 5 m/s × 12 s = **60 m**.
3. The cheetah's body generates too much heat. It must stop to cool down, or it risks overheating.
4. Time = 1000 m / 20 m/s = **50 s**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A cheetah sprints at 30 m/s with a maximum sprint time of 15 s. Its prey (a gazelle) runs at 20 m/s with a 100 m head start. Does the cheetah catch the gazelle before it overheats? Show your working.

**Worked Answer:**
**Method 1: Time to catch**
Relative speed = 30 − 20 = **10 m/s**.
Time to catch = head start / relative speed = 100 m / 10 m/s = **10 s**.
Since 10 s < 15 s, the cheetah catches the gazelle **before overheating**.

**Method 2: Distance check**
In 10 s, cheetah covers: 30 m/s × 10 s = **300 m**.
In 10 s, gazelle covers: 20 m/s × 10 s = **200 m**, plus head start = **300 m**.
They meet at 300 m from the cheetah's start (or 200 m from the gazelle's start).

**Conclusion:** The cheetah catches the gazelle in 10 seconds. This is why gazelles rely on zig-zag running and early detection — a straight chase is fatal.

**Q:** Explain why the cheetah's flexible spine and long legs contribute to its speed, using the relationship between stride length and speed.

**Worked Answer:**
Speed = stride length × stride frequency.

The cheetah's **flexible spine** acts like a spring. As the spine flexes and extends, it increases the effective leg reach. This makes each stride longer than the leg length alone would suggest.

The cheetah's **long legs** directly increase stride length. A longer leg covers more ground per step.

Combined effect:
- Cheetah stride length: ~7 m.
- Stride frequency: ~4 strides per second.
- Speed: 7 m × 4/s = **28 m/s** (close to the observed 30 m/s).

A human sprinter:
- Stride length: ~2.5 m.
- Stride frequency: ~4 strides per second.
- Speed: 2.5 m × 4/s = **10 m/s**.

The cheetah's advantage comes primarily from **stride length**, not frequency. The flexible spine adds ~2 m to each stride, giving the cheetah its enormous speed advantage.

## Safety Notes
- If timing a live animal, ensure it is in a safe, enclosed area.
- Do not chase or harass animals.
- Maintain a safe distance from large animals.
