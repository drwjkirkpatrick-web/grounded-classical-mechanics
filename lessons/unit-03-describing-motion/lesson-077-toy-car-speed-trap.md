# Lesson 077: Build: A Two-Gate Speed Trap for Toy Cars

**Unit:** 3 — Describing Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** speed-trap

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a two-gate timing system using chalk lines and a stopwatch.
2. Measure the time a toy car takes to pass between two gates.
3. Calculate speed and compare results with a class leaderboard.

## Materials
- A ramp board or plank (at least 1 m long)
- Books or bricks for propping
- Toy cars or home-made wheeled objects (at least 2 different designs)
- Chalk for marking gates
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- A tape measure or ruler
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Gate** *(lango)* — a marked line used to start or stop a timer.
- **Speed trap** *(tego la kasi)* — a device or method for measuring the speed of a moving object.
- **Leaderboard** *(orodha ya washindi)* — a ranked list of results.
- **Reaction error** *(kosa la kujibu)* — timing error caused by human reaction time.

## Pre-Test
1. Why is a two-gate system better than timing from start to finish by hand?
2. If Gate 1 is at 20 cm and Gate 2 is at 80 cm, what distance does the car travel between gates?
3. A car takes 0.5 s to travel 0.60 m between gates. What is its speed?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The two-gate system reduces reaction error because the timer only needs to react at two clear points, not follow the whole run.
2. 80 cm − 20 cm = **60 cm** (or 0.60 m).
3. Speed = 0.60 m / 0.5 s = **1.2 m/s**.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Show two toy cars. Ask, "Which is faster? How can we prove it fairly?" Introduce the idea of a "speed trap" — two gates and a timer.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, set up a ramp with a gentle slope (1–2 books).
   - Mark Gate 1 at 20 cm from the top and Gate 2 at 80 cm from the top (distance between gates = 60 cm).
   - One learner releases a car from the top. Another starts the stopwatch when the front wheels cross Gate 1 and stops when they cross Gate 2.
   - Each car is timed three times. Calculate average speed: v = 0.60 m / average time.
   - **Key discovery:** Different car designs produce different speeds. Heavy cars may be faster on steep ramps; light cars with good wheels may be faster on gentle slopes.
   - Groups compare results on a class leaderboard: car name / design vs speed.
   - **Error discussion:** If reaction time is ±0.15 s, what is the speed error for a 1.0 s run? (±15 percent — significant!)

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Why two gates:** Timing over a short, known distance reduces the effect of errors at start and finish. The start and finish are clearly defined by the gates.
   - **Reaction error:** Human reaction time (~0.15 s) is a large fraction of short runs. For a 2.0 s run, the error is ~7.5 percent. For a 0.5 s run, it is ~30 percent.
   - **Which speeds can we trust:** Runs longer than 1.0 s are more reliable. Very fast runs (<0.5 s) have large percentage errors.
   - **Design improvements:** Staggered starts, electronic gates (if available), or video frame-counting.
   - Kenyan context: police speed traps use radar (electronic) rather than manual timing because reaction error is too large.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states which car won and why. Homework: design a faster car (sketch it) and predict its speed.

## Hands-On Build: Two-Gate Speed Trap

### Parts
- Plank or mabati sheet — 1 m — classroom or jua kali
- Books or bricks — 2–3 — classroom
- Toy cars or wheeled bottles — 2–3 — home or market
- Chalk — 1 piece — classroom
- Stopwatch — 1 — phone or classroom
- Tape measure — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Prop one end of the plank on two books to create a ramp.
2. Measure 20 cm from the top and mark Gate 1 with chalk across the width of the plank.
3. Measure 80 cm from the top and mark Gate 2 with chalk. Distance between gates = **60 cm**.
4. Release a car from rest at the top of the ramp.
5. Start the stopwatch when the car's front wheels cross Gate 1. Stop when they cross Gate 2.
6. Record the time. Repeat three times.
7. Calculate average time and speed: v = 0.60 m / t_avg.
8. Test a second car design. Record and compare.
9. **Checkpoint:** Before calculating, check that your three times are consistent (within 0.2 s of each other). If one time is very different, repeat the run.

### Common Mistakes
- **Gate marks not straight:** The car crosses at different points, changing the distance slightly. Fix: draw straight, wide lines.
- **Timer watches the car, not the gate:** Reaction error increases. Fix: focus eyes on the gate line, not the car.
- **Different release heights:** Changes the speed entering Gate 1. Fix: always release from the same point.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Why is a two-gate timing system better than timing the whole ramp by eye?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* A car takes 1.2 s to travel 0.60 m between gates. What is its speed?
3. Gate spacing is 1.0 m and reaction error is 0.15 s. For a car taking 2.0 s, what is the approximate percentage error?
4. Suggest one way to reduce reaction error in this experiment.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. It defines clear start and stop points, reducing the subjectivity and error of eyeball timing.
2. Speed = 0.60 m / 1.2 s = **0.50 m/s**.
3. Percentage error = (0.15 / 2.0) × 100 = **7.5 percent**.
4. Any of: use electronic gates, use a longer gate spacing (reduces percentage error), have the same timer for all runs, use video frame-counting.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Gate spacing is 1.0 m and reaction error is 0.15 s. Which car speeds can you trust to two significant figures? Show your reasoning.

**Worked Answer:**
For two significant figures, we need the uncertainty to be less than ~5 percent (so the first two digits are reliable).

Percentage error = (0.15 / t) × 100.
Set this ≤ 5 percent:
0.15 / t ≤ 0.05
t ≥ 0.15 / 0.05
t ≥ **3.0 s**.

So cars that take **3.0 s or longer** to pass through 1.0 m can be trusted to two significant figures.

For a 0.60 m gate spacing:
t ≥ 0.15 / 0.05 = **3.0 s** (same calculation, but this is a very slow car — 0.20 m/s).

For typical gate spacing (0.60 m), the practical threshold is:
- 1.0 s run: 15 percent error → unreliable.
- 2.0 s run: 7.5 percent error → borderline.
- 3.0 s run: 5 percent error → acceptable.

**Practical conclusion:** Use gate spacing of at least 1.0 m and time cars that take 2.0 s or longer. For faster cars, use electronic timing or video analysis.

**Q:** Compare the accuracy of your manual two-gate speed trap with the ticker timer from Lesson 064. Which is more accurate for measuring speed at a specific point, and why?

**Worked Answer:**
The **ticker timer** is more accurate for measuring speed at a specific point because:
- It records position at regular, precise intervals (controlled by a metronome or beat).
- The time interval is fixed and known, eliminating reaction-time error.
- Dot spacing directly shows speed at each point — no start/stop timing needed.

The **two-gate speed trap** is more accurate than single-point timing because:
- It averages over a known distance, reducing the effect of a single reaction error.
- However, it still has two reaction errors (start and stop), which partly cancel but do not eliminate.

**When to use which:**
- Use the ticker timer for detailed speed profiles (e.g., how speed changes down a ramp).
- Use the two-gate trap for comparing average speeds of different cars on the same ramp.
- For highest accuracy, combine both: use the ticker timer to find where the car reaches steady speed, then place gates at that section.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the ramp landing area is clear of feet.
- Do not stand where cars may roll out.
- Keep fingers away from the gate lines when cars are rolling.
- If cars are heavy or made of hard materials, use a catch box at the bottom.
