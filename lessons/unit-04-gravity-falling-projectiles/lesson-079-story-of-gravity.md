# Lesson 079: The Story of Gravity: Aristotle, Galileo, Newton

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe how ideas about falling objects changed from Aristotle to Galileo to Newton.
2. Explain Galileo's thought experiment with chained balls.
3. Recognise that scientific ideas evolve through evidence and reasoning.

## Materials
- Story cards (prepared by teacher: Aristotle, Galileo, Newton)
- Two stones of different sizes
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Gravity** *(mvutano wa ardhi)* — the force that pulls objects toward the Earth.
- **Natural motion** *(mwenendo wa asili)* — Aristotle's idea that heavy objects naturally seek the centre of the universe.
- **Thought experiment** *(jaribio la fikira)* — an imaginary test used to explore ideas without physical equipment.
- **Universal gravitation** *(mvutano wa ulimwengu)* — Newton's idea that every object attracts every other object.

## Pre-Test
1. Who thought heavy objects fall faster than light ones?
2. What did Galileo imagine would happen if a heavy ball and a light ball were dropped together?
3. What force keeps the Moon orbiting the Earth?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Aristotle.
2. They would hit the ground at the same time (if air resistance is ignored).
3. Gravity (Newton's universal gravitation).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Hold up two stones — one large, one small. Ask, "Which falls faster?" Most learners will say the big one. Explain that for thousands of years, everyone thought so.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners receive story cards describing three scientists' ideas.
   - **Card 1: Aristotle (384–322 BC):** Believed heavy objects fall faster because they naturally seek the centre of the universe. "A stone falls because it is in its nature to be on the ground."
   - **Card 2: Galileo (1564–1642):** Dropped objects from the Leaning Tower of Pisa (legend). Used a thought experiment: if a heavy ball and a light ball are tied together, do they fall faster than the heavy ball alone? The answer is no — they fall at the same rate. Air resistance, not weight, makes light objects fall slower.
   - **Card 3: Newton (1643–1727):** Showed that gravity is universal — the same force that makes an apple fall also keeps the Moon in orbit. Every mass attracts every other mass.
   - Groups discuss: which idea was closest to the truth? Why did it take so long to figure out?
   - **Key discovery:** Science progresses through questioning old ideas, testing them, and building better explanations.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Aristotle's view:** Based on everyday observation. A feather falls slower than a stone, so heavier = faster. But Aristotle never controlled for air resistance.
   - **Galileo's breakthrough:** Used reasoning, not just observation. The chained-balls argument: if heavy falls faster, tying a light ball to it should either speed it up (the light ball pulls) or slow it down (the light ball drags). Both conclusions are absurd, so the premise must be wrong.
   - **Newton's synthesis:** Gravity is not just an Earth force — it is universal. The same g that pulls an apple pulls the Moon. F = Gm₁m₂/r².
   - **Modern view:** In a vacuum, all objects fall at the same rate (g ≈ 10 m/s²). Apollo astronauts demonstrated this on the Moon with a hammer and a feather.
   - Kenyan connection: The same gravity that pulls a mango from a tree pulls a satellite around Earth.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner summaries how ideas about gravity changed. Homework: find an example of an old belief that was later corrected by evidence.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Who believed that heavier objects fall faster?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* In Galileo's thought experiment, what would happen if a heavy ball and a light ball were dropped in a vacuum?
3. What did Newton discover about gravity that was different from Galileo?
4. Why did Aristotle's idea seem correct in everyday life?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Aristotle**.
2. They would fall at the **same rate** and hit the ground together.
3. Newton showed that gravity is **universal** — it acts between all masses, not just on Earth.
4. Because air resistance makes light objects (like feathers) fall slower than heavy ones (like stones). Aristotle did not separate the effects of gravity and air resistance.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Explain Galileo's chained-balls argument against Aristotle's claim that heavier objects fall faster. Why does the argument show that the claim leads to a contradiction?

**Worked Answer:**
Galileo's argument:
1. **Assume Aristotle is correct:** a 10 kg ball falls faster than a 5 kg ball.
2. **Now tie them together** with a light chain.
3. **From Aristotle's view:** the combined mass (15 kg) is heavier than either ball alone, so it should fall **faster** than the 10 kg ball.
4. **But:** the 5 kg ball is lighter and should fall **slower** than the 10 kg ball. The chain means the 5 kg ball should **drag back** on the 10 kg ball, slowing the whole system down.
5. **Contradiction:** the combined system must both fall faster (because it is heavier) and fall slower (because the lighter ball drags). These cannot both be true.
6. **Conclusion:** the original assumption (heavier falls faster) must be wrong.

This is a **proof by contradiction** — one of the most powerful tools in mathematics and physics. Galileo did not need to drop balls from a tower; he could refute Aristotle with pure logic.

**Q:** Newton said the force that makes an apple fall is the same force that keeps the Moon in orbit. Explain why this was revolutionary, using the idea of universal gravitation.

**Worked Answer:**
Before Newton, people believed:
- **Earthly motion** (falling apples) was governed by one set of rules.
- **Celestial motion** (planets, Moon) was governed by different rules — divine or perfect circular motion.

Newton's revolutionary insight:
- The **same force** (gravity) acts on both.
- The apple falls because Earth pulls it.
- The Moon does not fall to Earth because it is moving sideways fast enough. It is constantly falling toward Earth, but its sideways motion carries it forward. The result is an orbit — "falling around" the Earth.

Newton calculated that the Moon's acceleration toward Earth is about 1/3600 of g at Earth's surface. Since the Moon is about 60 Earth radii away, and 60² = 3600, the force follows an inverse-square law: F ∝ 1/r².

This unified Earth and sky under one law — **universal gravitation**. It was the first time a single equation described both an apple and the Moon.
