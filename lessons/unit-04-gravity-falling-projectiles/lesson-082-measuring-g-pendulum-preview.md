# Lesson 082: Measuring g: The Pendulum Promise

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State that a pendulum's swing period depends on its length, not its mass.
2. Predict that a longer pendulum has a longer period.
3. Rearrange the pendulum formula to make g the subject.

## Materials
- String or sisal twine (about 1 m)
- A small stone or nut to act as a bob
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- A ruler or tape measure
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Pendulum** *(kifaa cha kuringa)* — a mass on a string that swings back and forth under gravity.
- **Period** *(kipindi)* — the time for one complete swing (back and forth).
- **Amplitude** *(ukubwa)* — the maximum angle or distance from the rest position.
- **Frequency** *(mara)* — the number of complete swings per second.

## Pre-Test
1. What do you think affects how fast a pendulum swings: its length, its mass, or both?
2. If you make the string longer, will the pendulum swing faster or slower?
3. A pendulum takes 2 seconds for one swing. What is its period?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Its length (mass does not matter for small swings).
2. Slower — longer pendulums have longer periods.
3. **2 seconds**.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Swing a stone on a string as a pendulum. Ask, "What could I change to make it swing faster or slower?" (Expected: shorten the string, use a heavier bob, push harder.) Test the ideas today.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners build three pendulums with different lengths (e.g., 20 cm, 40 cm, 60 cm).
   - Use the same bob for all three.
   - Time 10 complete swings for each. Calculate the period (time for one swing = total time / 10).
   - **Key discovery:** Longer pendulums have longer periods. The 60 cm pendulum swings slower than the 20 cm one.
   - **Mass test:** Build two pendulums of the same length (40 cm) but different bobs (small stone vs large stone). Time 10 swings. Periods are the same.
   - **Conclusion:** Period depends on length, not mass.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Pendulum formula:** T = 2π√(L/g) where T = period, L = length, g = acceleration due to gravity.
   - **Rearranging for g:** g = 4π²L / T².
   - If you measure L and T, you can calculate g.
   - **Preview:** Unit 11 will cover the full method for measuring g with a pendulum, including graphing T² vs L.
   - Example: L = 1 m, T = 2 s. g = 4π² × 1 / 4 = π² ≈ **9.87 m/s²** (close to 10 m/s²).
   - Kenyan context: Grandfather clocks use pendulums. A longer pendulum means a slower tick. Swings in church towers use very long pendulums for slow, steady motion.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states what affects a pendulum's period. Homework: predict the period of a 1 m pendulum and check with a string and stone at home.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* You make a pendulum's string longer. Does its period increase or decrease?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* A pendulum completes 10 swings in 20 seconds. What is its period?
3. Does the mass of the pendulum bob affect its period?
4. Using T = 2π√(L/g), predict what happens to the period if you double the length.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The period **increases** (longer string = slower swing).
2. Period = 20 s / 10 = **2 s**.
3. **No** — the period is independent of mass (for small swings).
4. T ∝ √L, so doubling L makes T increase by √2 ≈ **1.41 times**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Rearrange T = 2π√(L/g) to make g the subject. If a pendulum of length 0.5 m has a period of 1.4 s, calculate g.

**Worked Answer:**
**Rearrangement:**
T = 2π√(L/g)
T/2π = √(L/g)
(T/2π)² = L/g
g = L / (T/2π)² = **4π²L / T²**

**Calculation:**
g = 4π² × 0.5 / (1.4)² = 4 × 9.87 × 0.5 / 1.96 = 19.74 / 1.96 ≈ **10.07 m/s²**.

This is very close to g = 10 m/s², confirming the formula.

**Q:** Explain why the period of a simple pendulum depends on length but not mass, using the idea of restoring force and inertia.

**Worked Answer:**
**Restoring force:** For a pendulum displaced by angle θ, the restoring force is F = mg sin θ (component of gravity pulling it back). This force is proportional to mass.

**Inertia:** The resistance to acceleration is also proportional to mass (F = ma → a = F/m).

**Cancellation:** When calculating acceleration:
a = F/m = (mg sin θ)/m = g sin θ.

The mass **cancels out** — the acceleration depends only on g and θ, not on mass. Since the period depends on the acceleration, and the acceleration is independent of mass, the period is also independent of mass.

The period **does** depend on length because a longer pendulum has a longer arc to travel at the same angular speed, and the restoring force is weaker for a given displacement angle (the same sideways displacement produces a smaller angle for a longer string, hence a smaller restoring force component).

Mathematically: T = 2π√(L/g). The L in the numerator means longer = slower.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the pendulum bob does not hit other learners.
- Use a soft bob (wrapped stone or nut) rather than a hard metal weight.
- Secure the string firmly to an overhead support.
- Do not swing the pendulum wildly — small angles (under 30°) are sufficient.

## Teacher Notes
- **Prior knowledge:** Lessons 079–081 (gravity concepts, free fall).
- **Differentiation:** Ages 10–13 should focus on observing that longer = slower and mass does not matter. Ages 14–17 should rearrange the formula and explain the mass-independence physically.
- **Local sourcing:** String, stone, and a stopwatch are sufficient. A classroom door frame or tree branch works as a support.
- **CBE assessment note:** Aligns with JS-FE "exploring factors affecting pendulum period" and "rearranging formulas to solve for unknowns."
