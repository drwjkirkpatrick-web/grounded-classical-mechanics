# Lesson 083: Measuring g: The Two-Metre Drop

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Measure the time of a dropped object's fall over a known distance.
2. Calculate g from distance and time data.
3. Evaluate sources of error in a free-fall measurement.

## Materials
- A ball or stone
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- A tape measure or marked 2 m stick
- A ladder or raised platform (e.g., a sturdy table or balcony)
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Free-fall time** *(muda wa kuduruma)* — the time an object takes to fall under gravity alone.
- **Measured g** *(g iliyopimwa)* — the value of acceleration due to gravity found by experiment.
- **Theoretical g** *(g ya nadharia)* — the accepted value (≈10 m/s²).
- **Percentage error** *(kosa la asilimia)* — the difference between measured and accepted values, expressed as a percentage.

## Pre-Test
1. What formula links distance fallen, time, and g?
2. If an object falls 2 m in 0.6 s, is g = 10 m/s² accurate?
3. Why is it hard to time a falling object accurately by hand?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. d = ½gt² (dropped from rest).
2. Let's check: d = ½ × 10 × 0.36 = 1.8 m. No, the object fell farther than predicted, so g might be higher or timing was off.
3. Reaction time — the human delay in starting and stopping the stopwatch.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "How could we measure g without fancy equipment?" (Expected: drop something and time it.) Explain the plan: drop a ball from 2 m, time the fall, calculate g.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, one learner drops a ball from a known height (e.g., 2 m from a table or balcony).
   - Another learner times the fall with a stopwatch.
   - Repeat three times. Record: height (m), time (s).
   - Calculate g using d = ½gt² → g = 2d/t².
   - **Key discovery:** Timing is hard! Reaction error makes the measured time too long, which makes calculated g too small.
   - Groups compare: who got closest to 10 m/s²? What went wrong for those far off?
   - **Error analysis:** If reaction time adds 0.2 s to a 0.6 s fall, the error is ~33 percent — huge!

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Formula derivation:** d = ½gt² → g = 2d/t².
   - **Why timing is hard:** Human reaction time (~0.2 s) is a large fraction of short falls. A 2 m fall takes ~0.63 s (t = √(2d/g) = √0.4 = 0.63 s). A 0.2 s error is ~30 percent.
   - **Improving accuracy:**
     - Drop from a greater height (longer fall time = smaller percentage error).
     - Use electronic timing (light gates, sound sensors).
     - Time many drops and take an average.
     - Have the timer focus on the sound of impact rather than the sight (sound travels fast, but the ear is quicker than the eye).
   - **Calculation check:** If d = 2.0 m and t = 0.63 s: g = 2 × 2.0 / (0.63)² = 4.0 / 0.397 ≈ **10.1 m/s²**.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states the biggest source of error and how to reduce it. Homework: calculate g from a drop you time at home.

## Hands-On Build: Two-Metre Drop

### Parts
- Ball or stone — 1 — classroom or ground
- Stopwatch — 1 — phone or classroom
- Tape measure — 1 — classroom
- Raised platform (table, balcony, or ladder) — 1 — school

### Workflow
1. Measure and mark a drop height of exactly 2.0 m from the platform to the ground.
2. One learner stands on the platform holding the ball.
3. Another learner stands below with the stopwatch.
4. The dropper says "go" and releases the ball. The timer starts on "go" and stops on impact.
5. Record the time. Repeat three times.
6. Calculate g for each trial: g = 2d/t² = 4.0/t².
7. Calculate the average g.
8. **Checkpoint:** Before calculating, check that your three times are within 0.1 s of each other. If one is very different, repeat.

### Common Mistakes
- **Delayed start:** The timer starts after the ball is released. Fix: use a clear signal ("go") and practise the timing.
- **Delayed stop:** The timer stops after hearing the impact (sound delay). Fix: for short drops, this is negligible (~6 ms for 2 m). Focus on visual impact if possible.
- **Height error:** Measuring from the platform edge instead of the ball's starting point. Fix: hold the ball over the measured mark.
- **Pushing the ball:** Releasing with a downward push adds initial velocity. Fix: hold the ball still, then open your fingers without pushing.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Write the formula for distance fallen in terms of g and t.
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* Why is it difficult to time a falling ball accurately by hand?
3. A ball falls 3.0 m in 0.78 s. Calculate g.
4. Suggest one way to reduce timing error in this experiment.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **d = ½gt²**.
2. Human **reaction time** adds error to both start and stop.
3. g = 2 × 3.0 / (0.78)² = 6.0 / 0.608 ≈ **9.87 m/s²**.
4. Any of: use a greater height, use electronic timing, repeat many times and average, time the sound of impact.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A ball is dropped from 2.0 m and timed with a hand-held stopwatch. The average of three trials is 0.70 s. Calculate the measured g and the percentage error compared to g = 10 m/s². Explain why the measured g is likely too low.

**Worked Answer:**
Measured g = 2d/t² = 2 × 2.0 / (0.70)² = 4.0 / 0.49 ≈ **8.16 m/s²**.

Percentage error = |(8.16 − 10)| / 10 × 100 = 1.84 / 10 × 100 = **18.4 percent**.

**Why too low:**
The measured time (0.70 s) is longer than the true time. True time for 2.0 m: t = √(2d/g) = √(4/10) = √0.4 = **0.632 s**.

The timer started late (after release) and/or stopped late (after impact). Both delays increase the measured time. Since g = 2d/t², a larger t gives a smaller g. The 0.07 s excess (11 percent) translates to an 18 percent error in g because the error is squared in the denominator.

**Q:** Explain why dropping from a greater height reduces the percentage error caused by reaction time, using the relationship between fall time and height.

**Worked Answer:**
Fall time: t = √(2d/g). As height d increases, t increases, but only as the square root.

**Example:**
- From 1 m: t = √(2/10) = 0.45 s. Reaction error 0.2 s → percentage = 0.2/0.45 = **44 percent**.
- From 4 m: t = √(8/10) = 0.89 s. Reaction error 0.2 s → percentage = 0.2/0.89 = **22 percent**.
- From 9 m: t = √(18/10) = 1.34 s. Reaction error 0.2 s → percentage = 0.2/1.34 = **15 percent**.

The **absolute** reaction error (0.2 s) stays the same, but it becomes a smaller **fraction** of the total time as the fall gets longer. Therefore, the percentage error in g decreases.

However, very high drops introduce other problems: air resistance becomes significant, and the ball is harder to see and time. There is an optimal height for this experiment — typically 2–5 m.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the landing area is clear of people and obstacles.
- Use a soft ball, not a heavy stone.
- Platforms must be sturdy and safe.
- Do not lean over edges.
- Have a spotter below to ensure the area is clear.
