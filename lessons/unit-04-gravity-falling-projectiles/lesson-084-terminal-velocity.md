# Lesson 084: Terminal Velocity: When Falling Stops Speeding Up

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that air resistance increases with speed until it balances gravity.
2. Define terminal velocity as the constant speed reached when air resistance equals weight.
3. Predict which objects reach terminal velocity faster and why.

## Materials
- A flat sheet of paper and a crumpled ball of paper
- A small stone
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Terminal velocity** *(kasi ya mwisho)* — the maximum speed an object reaches when falling, when air resistance equals its weight.
- **Air resistance** *(upinzani wa hewa)* — the upward force of air pushing against a falling object.
- **Balance of forces** *(usawa wa nguvu)* — when two forces are equal and opposite, producing no acceleration.
- **Drag** *(uvutaji)* — another word for air resistance.

## Pre-Test
1. A skydiver jumps from a plane. Does she keep speeding up forever?
2. What force pushes up on a falling object?
3. Why does a feather fall slower than a stone?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. No — she reaches a maximum speed (terminal velocity).
2. Air resistance (drag).
3. The feather has large air resistance relative to its weight.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "If you jump from a tall tree, do you keep getting faster and faster?" (Expected: yes, but...) Explain that in reality, air pushes back.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, drop a flat sheet of paper and a crumpled ball of the same paper simultaneously from the same height.
   - The flat paper flutters down slowly; the crumpled ball falls faster.
   - Drop the crumpled ball and a small stone together. The stone may win, or they may be close.
   - **Key discovery:** Shape and surface area affect falling speed because of air resistance. Large area = more air pushing back = slower fall.
   - Groups draw force diagrams:
     - At start: weight down, small air resistance up → net force down → acceleration.
     - As speed increases: air resistance grows.
     - At terminal velocity: weight = air resistance → net force = 0 → constant speed.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Why terminal velocity exists:** As an object falls faster, air resistance increases. Eventually, air resistance equals weight. No net force = no acceleration = constant speed.
   - **Factors affecting terminal velocity:**
     - **Mass:** Heavier objects have higher terminal velocity (need more air resistance to balance weight).
     - **Area:** Larger area = lower terminal velocity (more air resistance at any speed).
     - **Shape:** Streamlined shapes = higher terminal velocity (less air resistance).
   - **Examples:**
     - Human skydiver: ~50 m/s (belly-to-earth), ~90 m/s (head-down).
     - Raindrop: ~9 m/s (small, light).
     - Feather: very low terminal velocity (large area, light).
   - **In a vacuum:** No air resistance → no terminal velocity → objects keep accelerating (until they hit something).

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains terminal velocity in one sentence. Homework: find three objects and rank them by likely terminal velocity.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* A skydiver falls from a plane. Does her speed keep increasing forever?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* Why does a flat sheet of paper fall slower than a crumpled ball?
3. At terminal velocity, what is the relationship between weight and air resistance?
4. Predict: would a heavy book or a light sheet of paper have a higher terminal velocity?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **No** — she reaches terminal velocity.
2. The flat paper has **more surface area**, so more air resistance.
3. They are **equal** (balanced forces, no acceleration).
4. The **heavy book** — it needs more air resistance to balance its weight, which requires a higher speed.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Explain why a skydiver reaches a higher terminal velocity when falling head-down compared to belly-to-earth. Use the idea of surface area and air resistance.

**Worked Answer:**
**Belly-to-earth:** The skydiver presents a large surface area (like a flat paper). At any given speed, air resistance is large. Terminal velocity is reached when air resistance = weight. Since air resistance is large at relatively low speeds, terminal velocity is lower (~50 m/s).

**Head-down:** The skydiver presents a small surface area (like a crumpled ball). At any given speed, air resistance is smaller. To balance the same weight, a higher speed is needed. Terminal velocity is higher (~90 m/s).

**Key insight:** For the same weight, smaller area → higher terminal velocity. Skydivers control their descent by changing body position, adjusting their effective surface area.

**Q:** A raindrop and a hailstone of the same size fall through air. The hailstone is much denser (heavier). Explain why the hailstone reaches a higher terminal velocity and therefore causes more damage when it hits.

**Worked Answer:**
**Same size = same surface area = same air resistance at a given speed.**

For the raindrop:
- Weight = small (low density).
- At terminal velocity: air resistance = weight.
- Since weight is small, only a small air resistance is needed to balance it.
- This occurs at a low speed: ~9 m/s.

For the hailstone:
- Weight = large (high density).
- At terminal velocity: air resistance = weight.
- Since weight is large, a large air resistance is needed to balance it.
- This requires a higher speed: ~20–30 m/s.

**Damage:** Kinetic energy = ½mv². The hailstone has both larger mass and higher speed, so its kinetic energy is much greater. On impact, this energy is transferred to whatever it hits — causing dents on cars, damage to crops, or injury to people.

This is why hailstorms are dangerous even though hailstones are small: their high terminal velocity gives them destructive energy.

## Safety Notes
- No actual dropping from heights in the classroom.
- Use paper, not heavy objects, for demonstrations.
- Ensure all learners stand back from drop zones.

## Teacher Notes
- **Prior knowledge:** Lessons 080–081 (air resistance, free fall).
- **Differentiation:** Ages 10–13 should focus on the concept that air resistance increases with speed and eventually balances weight. Ages 14–17 should explain how surface area and mass affect terminal velocity and connect to real-world examples.
- **Local sourcing:** Paper and stones are sufficient.
- **CBE assessment note:** Aligns with JS-FE "explaining terminal velocity" and "analysing the balance of forces on falling objects."
