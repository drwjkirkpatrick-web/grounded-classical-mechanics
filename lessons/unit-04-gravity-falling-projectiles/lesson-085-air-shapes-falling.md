# Lesson 085: Designing for Air: Parachute Science

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Design and build a simple parachute using local materials.
2. Explain how a parachute increases air resistance to slow descent.
3. Compare the falling speeds of objects with and without parachutes.

## Materials
- A plastic bag or large leaf
- String or sisal twine (4 pieces, 30 cm each)
- A small stone or nut to act as a payload
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- Scissors
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Parachute** *(parashuti)* — a device that uses a large surface area to increase air resistance and slow a fall.
- **Canopy** *(kifuniko)* — the cloth or material part of a parachute that catches air.
- **Payload** *(mzigo)* — the object being carried and slowed by the parachute.
- **Suspension lines** *(nyuzi za kushikilia)* — the strings that connect the canopy to the payload.

## Pre-Test
1. Why does a parachute slow a fall?
2. Would a larger parachute fall faster or slower than a smaller one?
3. What would happen if you dropped the same stone with and without a parachute?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. It increases air resistance, which pushes upward against gravity.
2. Slower — larger area = more air resistance.
3. With a parachute: falls slowly. Without: falls fast.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "How do skydivers land safely from thousands of metres?" (Expected: parachutes.) Show a simple drawing of a parachute. Ask what makes it work.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners build parachutes using the Hands-On Build instructions below.
   - Test 1: Drop the payload (stone) without a parachute from shoulder height. Time the fall.
   - Test 2: Drop the same payload with a parachute. Time the fall.
   - **Key discovery:** The parachute dramatically increases fall time. Larger canopies slow the fall more.
   - Groups compare: whose parachute fell slowest? What design features helped?
   - Discuss: what happens if the strings are too short? Too long? What if the canopy is too small?

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **How parachutes work:** The canopy catches air, creating a large upward force (air resistance). When air resistance equals the payload's weight, terminal velocity is reached — but it is much lower than without the parachute.
   - **Design factors:**
     - **Canopy size:** Larger = more air resistance = slower fall.
     - **Canopy shape:** Circular or rectangular work well; holes can help stability.
     - **String length:** Longer strings let the canopy open fully; too short and the canopy collapses.
     - **Payload mass:** Heavier payloads need larger canopies to achieve the same slowing effect.
   - **Real-world connection:** Parachutes are used by skydivers, astronauts, and to drop supplies to remote areas. In Kenya, seeds like those of the "winged" maple tree use air resistance to glide away from the parent tree.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains how a parachute uses air resistance. Homework: design a parachute for an egg (thinking ahead to the egg drop challenge).

## Hands-On Build: Simple Parachute

### Parts
- Plastic bag or large leaf — 1 — home or market
- String or sisal twine — 4 pieces × 30 cm — classroom or home
- Small stone or nut — 1 — ground or home
- Scissors — 1 — classroom
- Stopwatch — 1 — phone or classroom

### Workflow
1. Cut the plastic bag or leaf into a square or circle about 30 cm across.
2. Make four small holes near the edges of the canopy, evenly spaced.
3. Tie one piece of string through each hole.
4. Gather the four strings together and tie them around the stone.
5. Hold the parachute by the canopy (not the stone) and let it fall from shoulder height.
6. Time the descent from release to landing.
7. Compare with the stone dropped without the parachute.
8. **Checkpoint:** Before timing, ensure the canopy is fully open and not twisted. A collapsed canopy will not catch air properly.

### Common Mistakes
- **Canopy too small:** Not enough air resistance; the stone still falls fast. Fix: use a larger bag or leaf.
- **Strings tangled:** The canopy cannot open fully. Fix: untangle strings before each drop.
- **Holes too close to edge:** The canopy tears. Fix: place holes at least 2 cm from the edge.
- **Holding by the stone:** The canopy may not open. Fix: hold by the canopy and release.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* How does a parachute slow a fall?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Would a parachute with a larger canopy fall faster or slower?
3. Why do parachute strings need to be long enough?
4. A stone with a parachute takes 3 s to fall 2 m. Without a parachute, it takes 0.6 s. Calculate the ratio of fall times.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. By **increasing air resistance**, which pushes upward against gravity.
2. **Slower** — more air resistance.
3. So the canopy can **open fully** and catch air. Short strings make the canopy collapse.
4. Ratio = 3 / 0.6 = **5** (the parachute makes it fall 5 times slower).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Explain why a parachute with a larger canopy creates more air resistance, and calculate the approximate terminal velocity of a 0.5 kg payload with a parachute that creates 5 N of air resistance at 2 m/s.

**Worked Answer:**
**Why larger canopy = more air resistance:**
Air resistance depends on the area pushing through the air. A larger canopy displaces more air molecules per second, creating a greater upward force. The air molecules collide with the canopy surface, transferring momentum upward. More surface area = more collisions = more force.

**Terminal velocity calculation:**
At terminal velocity: air resistance = weight.
Weight = mg = 0.5 kg × 10 N/kg = **5 N**.

If air resistance = 5 N at 2 m/s, and we need 5 N to balance weight, then terminal velocity ≈ **2 m/s**.

This is very slow compared to free fall (~14 m/s for 2 m drop), showing how effective parachutes are at reducing landing speed.

**Q:** Design an experiment to test how canopy size affects fall time. State your independent variable, dependent variable, and at least two controlled variables.

**Worked Answer:**
**Independent variable:** Canopy size (e.g., 20 cm, 30 cm, 40 cm diameter).

**Dependent variable:** Fall time from a fixed height (e.g., 3 m).

**Controlled variables:**
1. **Payload mass:** Use the same stone for all trials.
2. **Drop height:** Release from exactly 3 m each time.
3. **String length:** Keep strings at 30 cm for all parachutes.
4. **Canopy material:** Use the same plastic type for all.

**Procedure:**
1. Build three parachutes with different canopy sizes.
2. Drop each from 3 m and time the fall.
3. Repeat three times for each size.
4. Calculate average fall time.
5. Plot canopy size vs average fall time.

**Prediction:** Larger canopy → longer fall time → slower descent.

**Validation:** Check that the trend is consistent across all three trials. If one trial is very different, repeat it.

## Safety Notes
- Drop from shoulder height only — do not climb to dangerous heights.
- Ensure the landing area is clear.
- Use a soft payload (wrapped stone or clay), not a hard metal weight.
- Do not throw the parachute — release gently.
