# Lesson 089: How High Did It Go? Energy Preview

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Relate the height reached by a thrown object to its initial speed.
2. State that higher initial speed means higher maximum height.
3. Preview the connection between motion and energy for future units.

## Materials
- A ball
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- A tape measure or marked wall
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Kinetic energy** *(nishati ya harakati)* — the energy an object has because of its motion.
- **Potential energy** *(nishati ya uwezekano)* — the energy an object has because of its position (height).
- **Energy transformation** *(mabadiliko ya nishati)* — the conversion of one form of energy to another.
- **Maximum height** *(urefu wa juu kabisa)* — the highest point in a trajectory.

## Pre-Test
1. You throw a ball harder (faster). Does it go higher or lower?
2. At the top of its flight, does the ball have more or less energy than at the start?
3. What happens to the ball's speed energy as it rises?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Higher — more initial speed means more energy to convert to height.
2. The same total energy (ignoring air resistance) — it is transformed from motion energy to position energy.
3. It decreases — motion energy converts to position energy.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Throw a ball softly, then hard. Ask, "Which went higher? Why?" Introduce the idea that speed carries energy, and that energy becomes height.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners throw a ball upward at different speeds.
   - One learner throws; another marks the highest point reached (against a wall or by estimation).
   - A third times the flight. A fourth records.
   - **Key discovery:** Faster throws reach higher. The relationship is not linear — doubling speed more than doubles the height.
   - Groups calculate: if a throw at 10 m/s reaches 5 m, how high would 20 m/s reach? (Answer: 4× higher = 20 m.)
   - Discuss: where does the "extra" height come from? (More initial speed = more motion energy to convert.)

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Energy preview:** Kinetic energy (motion) converts to gravitational potential energy (height).
   - Formula preview: KE = ½mv², PE = mgh. At the top, KE = 0, PE = maximum.
   - Conservation: KE_start = PE_top → ½mu² = mgh → h = u²/2g.
   - This is the same result from Lesson 088, now seen through the lens of energy.
   - **Example:** u = 15 m/s → h = (15)²/20 = 225/20 = **11.25 m**.
   - **Future units:** Unit 6 will explore energy in detail. Unit 7 will cover work and power.
   - Kenyan context: A boda-boda's speed becomes climbing ability on a hill — motion energy becomes position energy.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states the relationship between speed and height. Homework: estimate how high you can throw a ball; calculate the required initial speed.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* You throw a ball faster. Does it reach a higher or lower maximum height?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* What happens to the ball's motion energy as it rises?
3. A ball thrown at 10 m/s reaches 5 m. How high would it reach if thrown at 20 m/s?
4. Explain why a heavier ball thrown at the same speed reaches the same height as a lighter ball.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Higher** — more initial speed means more energy to convert to height.
2. It **decreases** — motion energy converts to position energy.
3. Since h ∝ u², doubling speed quadruples height: 5 m × 4 = **20 m**.
4. From h = u²/2g, mass cancels out. Both balls have the same initial speed, so they reach the same height. The heavier ball has more energy overall, but it also needs more energy to reach the same height.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Using the energy equation ½mu² = mgh, derive the formula for maximum height and calculate the height for an initial speed of 25 m/s.

**Worked Answer:**
**Derivation:**
At launch: all energy is kinetic → KE = ½mu².
At maximum height: all energy is potential → PE = mgh.
By conservation of energy: KE = PE.
½mu² = mgh
Cancel m: ½u² = gh
h = u²/2g

**Calculation:**
h = (25)² / (2×10) = 625 / 20 = **31.25 m**.

**Check with kinematics:**
From Lesson 088: h = u²/2g = 625/20 = 31.25 m. Same result — energy and kinematics agree.

**Q:** A 0.5 kg ball is thrown upward at 20 m/s. Calculate (a) its initial kinetic energy, (b) its potential energy at maximum height, and (c) explain why the mass does not affect the maximum height.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) KE = ½mu² = ½ × 0.5 × (20)² = 0.25 × 400 = **100 J**.

(b) At maximum height, all KE has converted to PE:
PE = mgh = 0.5 × 10 × h.
But h = u²/2g = 400/20 = 20 m.
PE = 0.5 × 10 × 20 = **100 J**.

This confirms conservation: KE_start = PE_top = 100 J.

(c) **Why mass does not affect height:**
From h = u²/2g, mass does not appear in the formula.
Physically: a heavier ball has more kinetic energy (KE = ½mu²), but it also needs more potential energy to reach a given height (PE = mgh). The mass cancels in the energy balance equation.

This is a profound result: in a vacuum (no air resistance), all objects thrown upward at the same speed reach the same height, regardless of mass. This is the same principle as free fall — gravity affects all objects equally.

## Safety Notes
- Throw in an open area away from other learners.
- Use a soft ball.
- Ensure the landing area is clear.
- Do not throw near fragile objects.
