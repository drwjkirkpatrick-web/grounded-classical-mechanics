# Lesson 090: Projectile Motion I: Sideways and Down Are Independent

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State that horizontal and vertical motion are independent in projectile flight.
2. Demonstrate that a horizontally launched projectile falls at the same rate as one dropped vertically.
3. Predict the path of a projectile launched horizontally.

## Materials
- Two identical balls or stones
- A table or raised platform
- A ruler or tape measure
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Projectile** *(kitu kilichorushwa)* — an object thrown or launched into the air.
- **Horizontal motion** *(mwenendo wa mlalo)* — motion parallel to the ground.
- **Vertical motion** *(mwenendo wima)* — motion perpendicular to the ground (up or down).
- **Independence** *(uhuru)* — the property that two motions do not affect each other.

## Pre-Test
1. A ball is thrown horizontally from a table. At the same time, another ball is dropped from the same height. Which hits the ground first?
2. Does the horizontal speed of a projectile affect how fast it falls?
3. What shape is the path of a horizontally launched projectile?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. They hit at the same time — horizontal motion does not affect vertical falling.
2. No — vertical fall depends only on gravity.
3. A parabola (curved path).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "If I throw a ball forward from a table while another ball drops straight down, which lands first?" (Expected: the thrown ball takes longer because it travels farther.) Test this today.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, set up a table or platform about 1 m high.
   - **Test 1:** One learner holds two identical balls at the table edge. One ball is held out over the edge (ready to be pushed horizontally). The other is held at the same height but directly above the ground (ready to drop).
   - On a count of "three," the first ball is pushed horizontally while the second is dropped vertically.
   - Observers watch which hits the ground first. Repeat three times.
   - **Key discovery:** Both balls hit the ground at the same time! The horizontal motion of the first ball does not affect its vertical fall.
   - **Test 2:** Push the first ball harder (faster horizontal speed). It lands farther away, but still at the same time as the dropped ball.
   - Groups draw the two paths: one straight down, one parabolic curve.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Independence of motions:** Horizontal motion (sideways) and vertical motion (up/down) are completely separate.
   - **Horizontal:** No forces act (ignoring air resistance), so horizontal speed stays constant.
   - **Vertical:** Gravity acts downward, so the ball accelerates downward at g = 10 m/s².
   - **Result:** The path is a **parabola** — a combination of constant horizontal motion and accelerated vertical motion.
   - **Galileo's insight:** This was one of Galileo's great discoveries. The same physics applies to a thrown spear, a kicked ball, or a launched rocket.
   - Kenyan context: Throwing a jembe (hoe) to a friend across a field — the tool follows a parabolic path.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states why horizontal and vertical motion are independent. Homework: observe a thrown object and trace its parabolic path.

## Hands-On Build: Horizontal Launch Demonstration

### Parts
- Two identical balls or stones — 2 — ground or classroom
- Table or platform — 1 m high — classroom
- Ruler or tape measure — 1 — classroom
- Notebook — 1 per learner — classroom

### Workflow
1. Place the table on flat ground.
2. One learner stands at the table edge, holding one ball out horizontally.
3. Another learner stands beside the table, holding the second ball at the same height but directly above the ground.
4. On "go," the first ball is pushed horizontally while the second is released straight down.
5. Observers note which ball hits first.
6. Repeat with different horizontal speeds (gentle push vs hard push).
7. Measure the horizontal distance travelled by the launched ball.
8. **Checkpoint:** Before each trial, verify that both balls start at exactly the same height. Even a small difference affects the result.

### Common Mistakes
- **Different starting heights:** The dropped ball starts lower or higher. Fix: use a ruler to align the starting positions.
- **Pushing the ball downward:** This gives the ball an initial vertical velocity. Fix: push strictly horizontally, not down.
- **Uneven ground:** One ball rolls after landing. Fix: mark the landing spot and compare times, not distances after bounce.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* A ball is thrown horizontally while another is dropped from the same height. Which hits first?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Does horizontal speed affect the time a projectile takes to fall?
3. A ball is thrown horizontally at 5 m/s from a 2 m table. How long does it take to hit the ground?
4. Sketch the path of a horizontally launched projectile.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. They hit at the **same time**.
2. **No** — vertical fall time depends only on height and gravity.
3. d = ½gt² → 2 = ½ × 10 × t² → t² = 0.4 → t = **0.63 s**.
4. A smooth curve starting horizontally, then curving downward (parabola opening downward).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A ball is thrown horizontally at 10 m/s from a height of 5 m. Calculate (a) the time to hit the ground, (b) the horizontal distance travelled, and (c) the vertical speed at impact.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) Time to fall: d = ½gt² → 5 = ½ × 10 × t² → t² = 1 → t = **1.0 s**.

(b) Horizontal distance: x = v_horizontal × t = 10 m/s × 1.0 s = **10 m**.

(c) Vertical speed at impact: v_vertical = gt = 10 × 1.0 = **10 m/s** downward.

**Resultant speed at impact:**
v = √(v_horizontal² + v_vertical²) = √(10² + 10²) = √200 = **14.1 m/s**.

The direction is 45° below horizontal (since both components are equal).

**Q:** Explain why a bullet fired horizontally from a gun and a bullet dropped from the same height hit the ground at the same time, assuming no air resistance. What happens in reality due to air resistance?

**Worked Answer:**
**In a vacuum (no air resistance):**
Both bullets fall under gravity alone. The fired bullet has horizontal motion, but this does not affect vertical fall. Both accelerate downward at g = 10 m/s² from the same initial height. Therefore, they hit the ground simultaneously.

**In reality (with air resistance):**
The fired bullet experiences air resistance in both directions:
- **Horizontal:** Air resistance slows the bullet's forward motion. However, the bullet is still travelling very fast, so this effect is small over short distances.
- **Vertical:** Air resistance pushes upward against the falling bullet. This slightly reduces its downward acceleration, making it fall a tiny bit slower than the dropped bullet.

The dropped bullet also experiences upward air resistance, but since it has no horizontal motion, its vertical motion is affected only by vertical air resistance.

For typical gun heights (1–2 m), the time difference is microseconds — imperceptible. Over longer distances (e.g., a rifle bullet travelling 1 km), the horizontal air resistance significantly affects the trajectory, causing the bullet to drop more than the vacuum prediction.

## Safety Notes
- Ensure the landing area is clear.
- Use soft balls, not hard stones, for the demonstration.
- Do not throw objects toward other learners.
- The table must be stable.
