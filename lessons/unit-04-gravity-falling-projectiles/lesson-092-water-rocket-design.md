# Lesson 092: The Water Rocket Project I: Pressure and Design

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** water-rocket

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain how air pressure and water expulsion create thrust in a water rocket.
2. Design a water rocket using a plastic bottle and local materials.
3. Identify the key design features that affect rocket performance.

## Materials
- A plastic bottle (1.5 L or 2 L)
- A cork or rubber stopper that fits the bottle neck
- A bicycle pump with a needle adapter
- Water
- Cardboard or plastic for fins
- Tape or glue
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Thrust** *(msukumo)* — the pushing force that moves a rocket forward.
- **Pressure** *(shinikizo)* — force per unit area; compressed air pushes water out of the rocket.
- **Nozzle** *(mlango wa kutoa)* — the opening through which water is expelled.
- **Stability** *(utulivu)* — the ability of a rocket to fly straight without wobbling.

## Pre-Test
1. What pushes a water rocket upward?
2. Why does a rocket need fins?
3. What happens if too much water is in the rocket?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Expelled water pushed by compressed air.
2. To keep it stable and flying straight (like feathers on an arrow).
3. It may be too heavy to lift, or the air pressure may not be enough to expel it all.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Show a plastic bottle. Ask, "How can we make this fly?" (Expected: fill it with water and pump air.) Explain that today's lesson designs the rocket.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners examine the materials and brainstorm designs.
   - **Key physics:** Air is pumped into the bottle. The compressed air pushes water out through the nozzle. By Newton's Third Law, the downward push on the water creates an upward thrust on the rocket.
   - **Design features to consider:**
     - **Fins:** Add 3–4 fins near the base for stability. Shape: triangular or rectangular. Size: about 5 cm tall.
     - **Nose cone:** A pointed top reduces air resistance.
     - **Water amount:** Too little = low thrust; too much = heavy. Optimal is about 1/3 full.
     - **Nozzle size:** Smaller nozzle = higher pressure but slower expulsion; larger = lower pressure but faster. A cork with a small hole works.
   - Groups sketch their design and label key features.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **How it works:**
     1. Pump air into the bottle (increasing pressure).
     2. Release the cork — water shoots out downward.
     3. Newton's Third Law: downward force on water = upward force on rocket.
     4. Rocket accelerates upward until the water is gone.
     5. Then it coasts upward, slows, and falls back down.
   - **Energy transformation:** Pump work → pressure energy → kinetic energy of water → kinetic energy of rocket → gravitational potential energy → kinetic energy on descent.
   - **Safety:** Launch outdoors, away from people. Do not aim at anyone.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: Groups present their design sketch. Homework: gather materials for the build day.

## Hands-On Build: Water Rocket Design

### Parts
- Plastic bottle (1.5 L or 2 L) — 1 — home or market
- Cork or rubber stopper — 1 — market or fundi
- Bicycle pump with needle — 1 — home or school
- Water — ~500 mL — tap
- Cardboard or plastic for fins — 4 pieces — classroom
- Tape or glue — 1 roll — classroom
- Scissors — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Sketch the rocket design: bottle body, fins at base, nose cone at top.
2. Cut fins from cardboard (triangle shape, ~5 cm × 8 cm).
3. Attach fins evenly around the base of the bottle using tape or glue.
4. Create a nose cone from a smaller bottle top or folded paper. Tape it to the rocket's top.
5. Test-fit the cork in the bottle neck. It should be tight but removable.
6. Plan the water amount: mark the bottle at 1/3 full.
7. **Checkpoint:** Before proceeding to build day, check that:
   - Fins are securely attached and evenly spaced.
   - The cork fits tightly.
   - The nose cone is firmly taped.
   - The rocket stands upright on its fins.

### Common Mistakes
- **Fins too small:** Rocket wobbles in flight. Fix: make fins larger or add more.
- **Fins uneven:** Rocket curves to one side. Fix: space fins evenly (120° apart for 3 fins).
- **Cork too loose:** Air escapes before launch. Fix: wrap the cork with tape for a tighter fit.
- **Nose cone too heavy:** Rocket nose-dives. Fix: use lightweight paper, not thick cardboard.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What provides the thrust in a water rocket?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Why do water rockets need fins?
3. Explain why a water rocket works using Newton's Third Law.
4. Predict: would a rocket with 1/4 water or 1/2 water fly higher? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Expelled water** pushed by compressed air.
2. To provide **stability** and keep the rocket flying straight.
3. The rocket pushes water **downward** (action). The water pushes the rocket **upward** (reaction). This is Newton's Third Law.
4. **1/4 water** would likely fly higher — less mass to lift, and the air can expand more. But too little water means less thrust. The optimum is about **1/3**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Explain why the optimal water amount in a water rocket is about one-third of the bottle volume. What trade-offs determine this optimum?

**Worked Answer:**
The water rocket's flight depends on two competing factors:

1. **Thrust:** More water means more mass to expel, which creates more thrust (F = Δ(mv)/Δt). However, the thrust lasts for a shorter time because the air pressure drops faster as more water leaves.

2. **Mass:** More water means a heavier rocket, requiring more force to accelerate (F = ma). A heavy rocket with lots of water may not lift off at all.

**At 1/3 water:**
- There is enough water to provide substantial thrust.
- The remaining 2/3 is air, which can expand and push water out steadily.
- The mass is not excessive — the rocket can accelerate.

**At 1/2 water:**
- More thrust initially, but the air runs out quickly.
- Heavier rocket → lower acceleration.
- Thrust ends before the rocket reaches maximum speed.

**At 1/4 water:**
- Lighter rocket → good acceleration.
- But less thrust overall — the rocket may not reach its potential height.

The optimum (~1/3) balances these effects. Experiments confirm this ratio for typical bottle rockets.

**Q:** A water rocket has a bottle of volume 2 L. If filled with 1/3 water and pumped to 3 atmospheres of pressure, calculate the mass of water expelled and the approximate thrust if the water leaves at 15 m/s in 0.5 s.

**Worked Answer:**
**Water volume:** 1/3 × 2 L = 0.667 L = **0.000667 m³**.
**Water mass:** m = ρV = 1000 kg/m³ × 0.000667 m³ = **0.667 kg**.

**Thrust:**
F = Δ(mv)/Δt = (0.667 kg × 15 m/s) / 0.5 s = 10 / 0.5 = **20 N**.

**Rocket mass:**
Bottle mass ≈ 0.05 kg + water mass 0.667 kg = **0.717 kg**.

**Acceleration:**
a = F/m = 20 / 0.717 ≈ **27.9 m/s²**.

This is about 2.8 g — a strong acceleration that launches the rocket quickly.

## Safety Notes
- Launch outdoors only, away from buildings and people.
- Do not aim at anyone.
- Wear eye protection if available.
- The cork can pop with force — keep face away during pumping.
- Do not over-pump (risk of bottle bursting). Stop at firm resistance.
