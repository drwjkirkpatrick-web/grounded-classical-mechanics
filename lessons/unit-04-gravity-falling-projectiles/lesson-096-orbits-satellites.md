# Lesson 096: Orbits and Satellites: Falling Around the Earth

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-4.2;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that an orbit is a continuous state of free fall.
2. Describe why satellites do not crash into the Earth.
3. Connect satellite motion to horizontal projectile motion.

## Materials
- A ball
- A string (for the swing model)
- A globe or ball to represent Earth
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Orbit** *(mzunguko)* — a curved path of an object around a planet due to gravity.
- **Satellite** *(setalaiti)* — an object that orbits a planet.
- **Free fall** *(kuduruma huru)* — motion under gravity alone.
- **Weightlessness** *(kutokuwa na uzito)* — the sensation of having no weight, experienced in orbit.

## Pre-Test
1. Why doesn't the Moon fall to Earth?
2. Is an astronaut in orbit weightless because there is no gravity?
3. What happens if you throw a ball harder and harder horizontally?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. It is falling, but its sideways motion carries it forward continuously.
2. No — there is plenty of gravity. They are in free fall, so everything falls together.
3. It travels farther before hitting the ground. Fast enough, and it curves around the Earth.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Swing a ball on a string in a circle. Ask, "Is the ball falling?" (Expected: no, it's going in a circle.) Explain that it IS falling toward the centre — but its sideways motion keeps it from hitting.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** Imagine throwing a ball horizontally from a tall mountain.
   - Slow throw: ball curves and hits the ground nearby.
   - Faster throw: ball curves less, lands farther away.
   - Fast enough: ball curves at the same rate as Earth curves away — it never hits!
   - **Key discovery:** An orbit is just falling with enough sideways speed that the ground curves away beneath you.
   - Kenyan connection: Kenya's first satellite, Uhuru-1 (1970), and recent Taifa-1. These satellites orbit Earth at ~500 km altitude, falling continuously but moving sideways at ~7.5 km/s.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Orbit = continuous free fall:** The satellite is constantly pulled toward Earth by gravity. But it is also moving sideways so fast that by the time it would hit, the ground has curved away.
   - **Newton's cannon:** Newton imagined a cannon on a high mountain. Slow shot: hits ground. Fast shot: orbits. Faster still: escapes Earth entirely.
   - **Weightlessness:** Astronauts feel weightless not because gravity is gone, but because they and their spacecraft are falling together. Like being in a falling elevator — everything floats.
   - **Speeds:**
     - Low Earth orbit: ~7.5 km/s (27,000 km/h).
     - Geostationary orbit: ~3 km/s (but much farther out).
     - Escape velocity: ~11 km/s.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains why satellites don't crash. Homework: research one Kenyan satellite and its orbit.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Why doesn't the Moon crash into Earth?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Are astronauts weightless because there is no gravity in space?
3. What speed is needed to orbit Earth (approximately)?
4. Explain why an orbit is called "falling around" the Earth.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The Moon is **falling**, but its sideways motion keeps it from hitting — the Earth curves away beneath it.
2. **No** — gravity is strong in orbit. They are weightless because they are in continuous free fall.
3. About **7.5 km/s** for low Earth orbit.
4. The satellite is constantly falling toward Earth, but moving sideways so fast that it misses the ground and curves around.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Explain weightlessness in orbit as continuous free fall, not absence of gravity. Use the analogy of a falling elevator.

**Worked Answer:**
**The falling elevator analogy:**
Imagine you are in an elevator and the cable snaps. You and the elevator fall together at the same rate. Relative to the elevator, you float — you feel weightless. But gravity is still pulling you down at 10 m/s². You are falling, but the elevator is falling with you.

**In orbit:**
The spacecraft and everything inside it are falling toward Earth at the same rate. The spacecraft's sideways speed (7.5 km/s) means it keeps missing the Earth. But everything inside falls together, so relative to the spacecraft, objects float. Gravity is not absent — it is what keeps the spacecraft in orbit!

**The difference from deep space:**
Far from Earth (e.g., halfway to the Moon), gravity is weaker but still present. Weightlessness there is also due to free fall — you and your spacecraft are falling toward Earth, just very slowly because gravity is weaker.

True "zero gravity" only exists infinitely far from all masses. Everywhere else, gravity exists — weightlessness is a result of motion, not absence of gravity.

**Q:** Calculate the orbital speed of a satellite at 400 km altitude. Earth's radius = 6400 km, g at surface = 10 m/s². Show that v ≈ 7.7 km/s.

**Worked Answer:**
**Method 1: Using g and orbital radius**
Total orbital radius r = 6400 + 400 = **6800 km** = 6.8 × 10⁶ m.

Gravity at altitude: g' = g × (R_earth/r)² = 10 × (6400/6800)² = 10 × (0.941)² = 10 × 0.885 = **8.85 m/s²**.

For circular orbit: centripetal acceleration = gravity.
v²/r = g' → v = √(g'r) = √(8.85 × 6.8 × 10⁶) = √(6.02 × 10⁷) = **7.76 × 10³ m/s** = **7.76 km/s**.

**Method 2: Using orbital mechanics**
v = √(GM/r). At Earth's surface: GM = gR² = 10 × (6.4 × 10⁶)² = 4.096 × 10¹⁴.
v = √(4.096 × 10¹⁴ / 6.8 × 10⁶) = √(6.02 × 10⁷) = **7.76 km/s**.

Both methods agree. This is why satellites need powerful rockets to reach orbit — 7.76 km/s is about 25 times faster than a speeding bullet.
