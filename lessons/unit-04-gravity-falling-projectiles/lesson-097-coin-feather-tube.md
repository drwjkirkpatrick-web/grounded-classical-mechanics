# Lesson 097: The Coin and Feather Problem

**Unit:** 4 — Gravity, Falling Bodies & Projectiles | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State that in a vacuum, all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass.
2. Explain why feathers fall slower than coins in air.
3. Construct a logical argument using evidence from parachute experiments.

## Materials
- A clear plastic bottle with cap
- A coin and a small feather
- A vacuum pump (if available) or a balloon and pump
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Vacuum** *(tupu)* — a space with no air or other matter.
- **Evidence** *(ushahidi)* — observations or data used to support a conclusion.
- **Logical argument** *(hoja ya mantiki)* — a reasoned chain of statements leading to a conclusion.
- **Demonstration** *(onyesho)* — an experiment that illustrates a principle.

## Pre-Test
1. In a vacuum, does a coin or a feather fall faster?
2. Why does a feather fall slower than a coin in air?
3. How could you prove that mass does not affect falling speed?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. They fall at the same rate — both accelerate at g.
2. Air resistance affects the feather more because of its large surface area and light mass.
3. Remove air (vacuum) or compare objects of the same shape but different mass.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "If I drop a coin and a feather, which lands first?" (Expected: coin.) "What if there was no air?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners attempt the coin-and-feather experiment.
   - **In air:** Drop coin and feather simultaneously. Coin wins.
   - **In a bottle (if pump available):** Place coin and feather in a clear bottle. Pump out air. Invert the bottle. They fall together.
   - **Without pump:** Use evidence from Lessons 080 and 085.
     - Crumpled paper vs flat paper: same mass, different fall rate → shape (air resistance) matters.
     - Bottles with different water levels: same shape, different mass → same fall rate → mass does not matter.
   - **Key discovery:** The evidence from multiple experiments points to one conclusion: in the absence of air, all objects fall at the same rate.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **The argument:**
     - Evidence 1: Same shape, different mass → same fall rate (Lesson 080).
     - Evidence 2: Same mass, different shape → different fall rate (Lesson 085).
     - Conclusion: Mass does not determine fall rate; air resistance does.
     - Therefore: In vacuum, coin and feather fall together.
   - **Apollo 15 demonstration:** Astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon in 1971. They landed together — confirming Galileo's prediction.
   - **The power of evidence:** Even without a vacuum pump, we can reason from related experiments to a general conclusion.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states the conclusion supported by evidence. Homework: write the logical argument in your own words.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* In a vacuum, do a coin and a feather fall at the same rate?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Why does a feather fall slower than a coin in air?
3. What evidence from Lesson 080 supports the idea that mass does not matter?
4. What evidence from Lesson 085 supports the idea that shape matters?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Yes** — both accelerate at g.
2. **Air resistance** — the feather has large surface area and low mass.
3. Bottles of the **same shape but different mass** fell at the same rate.
4. A **flat sheet** fell slower than a **crumpled ball** of the same paper.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Write the argument that settles the coin-and-feather case even if a vacuum pump is unavailable. What evidence from this unit closes the case?

**Worked Answer:**
**Premise 1:** If mass determines falling speed, then heavier objects always fall faster.
**Premise 2:** If shape determines falling speed, then objects of different shapes fall at different rates even if mass is the same.

**Evidence:**
- Lesson 080: Bottles of same shape, different mass → fall together. This **falsifies** Premise 1.
- Lesson 085: Same paper, different shape (flat vs crumpled) → different fall rates. This **supports** Premise 2.

**Conclusion:**
Mass does not determine falling speed. Shape (and thus air resistance) does. Therefore, in the absence of air resistance, a coin and a feather — having the same shape factor (none, in vacuum) — would fall at the same rate.

**Additional evidence:**
- Lesson 081: All objects in free fall accelerate at g = 10 m/s², with no mass term in the equation.
- The Moon demonstration (Apollo 15): An actual vacuum test confirming the prediction.

The case is closed by **multiple lines of converging evidence** — not just one experiment, but a pattern across many.

**Q:** On the Moon, an astronaut drops a hammer and a feather from 1.5 m. Calculate the time to fall and the speed at impact. Explain why the demonstration was done on the Moon rather than Earth.

**Worked Answer:**
**Time to fall:**
d = ½gt² → 1.5 = ½ × 1.6 × t² → t² = 1.5/0.8 = 1.875 → t = **1.37 s**.

**Speed at impact:**
v = gt = 1.6 × 1.37 = **2.19 m/s**.

**Why on the Moon:**
- The Moon has **no atmosphere** — no air resistance.
- On Earth, the feather would flutter and land much later than the hammer.
- On the Moon, both fall together, making the point dramatically and unambiguously.
- The demonstration required a **vacuum**, and the Moon provides one naturally.

This was not just a stunt — it was a powerful confirmation of Galileo's reasoning, made possible by reaching another world.

## Safety Notes
- If using a vacuum pump, ensure the bottle is strong and sealed.
- Do not over-pump — risk of bottle implosion.
- Standard classroom safety otherwise.
