# Lesson 099: What 'Work' Means in Physics

**Unit:** 5 — Work, Energy & Power | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define work in physics as force multiplied by distance in the direction of the force.
2. Distinguish between everyday "work" and physics "work."
3. Calculate work done when a force moves an object in the force's direction.

## Materials
- A debe or bucket
- A spring balance
- A tape measure
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Work** *(kazi)* — in physics, force multiplied by distance moved in the force's direction; measured in joules.
- **Force** *(nguvu)* — a push or pull measured in newtons.
- **Distance** *(umbali)* — how far an object moves.
- **Joule** *(joule)* — the unit of work and energy; 1 J = 1 N × 1 m.

## Pre-Test
1. You carry a debe of water across the yard. Are you doing physics work on the debe?
2. You hold a heavy book above your head for 5 minutes. Are you doing physics work on the book?
3. What two quantities do you multiply to calculate work?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Only if you lift it — horizontal carrying does no physics work against gravity.
2. No — there is no movement.
3. Force and distance (in the force's direction).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "Who has done work today?" (Expected: chores, studying.) Explain that physics has a specific meaning for "work."

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners test three scenarios.
   - **Scenario 1:** Lift a debe 1 m upward. Measure force with spring balance. Calculate: W = F × d.
   - **Scenario 2:** Hold the debe at 1 m height for 10 seconds. Measure force. Calculate work. (Answer: zero — no movement.)
   - **Scenario 3:** Carry the debe horizontally across 5 m. Calculate work against gravity. (Answer: zero — force is vertical, movement is horizontal.)
   - **Key discovery:** Physics work requires BOTH force AND movement in the force's direction.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Formula:** W = F × d where W = work (J), F = force (N), d = distance in force's direction (m).
   - **Cases where work is zero:**
     - No force applied (object moves by itself).
     - No movement (pushing a wall).
     - Force perpendicular to motion (carrying horizontally).
   - **Everyday vs physics:** Holding a book is tiring (muscles contract) but does no physics work. Pushing a wall is exhausting but does no physics work.
   - Kenyan context: A mkokoteni puller does work pulling horizontally; a porter holding luggage on their head does no physics work (though they tire).

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner gives one example of physics work and one of no physics work. Homework: list five activities and classify as physics work or not.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* You hold a heavy debe above your head for 1 minute. How much physics work do you do on the debe?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* What two quantities are multiplied to find work?
3. A learner pushes a matatu that does not move. How much work is done?
4. A farmer lifts a 200 N jerrican 1.5 m onto a stand. Calculate the work done.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Zero** — there is no movement.
2. **Force and distance** (in the force's direction).
3. **Zero** — no distance moved.
4. W = 200 N × 1.5 m = **300 J**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** You hold a 200 N load for 5 minutes. How much physics work is done on the load, and why do your muscles tire anyway?

**Worked Answer:**
**Physics work:** W = F × d = 200 N × 0 m = **0 J**.

There is no movement, so no physics work is done on the load. The load does not move, so its energy does not change.

**Why muscles tire:**
Muscles tire because they are continuously contracting and relaxing at a microscopic level to maintain tension. Muscle fibres use chemical energy (ATP) to generate force, even without movement. This energy is converted to heat, not mechanical work. The human body is inefficient at holding static positions — energy is consumed even when no physics work is done.

This is why a labourer holding a load on their head gets tired even though the physics work on the load is zero. The work is done internally by the muscles, not on the external load.

**Q:** A satellite orbits Earth in a circular path under gravity. Explain why gravity does zero work on the satellite during one complete orbit.

**Worked Answer:**
Work = F × d × cos θ where θ is the angle between force and displacement.

In circular orbit:
- Gravity points **toward Earth's centre** (radial direction).
- The satellite's displacement at every instant is **tangent to the circle** (perpendicular to the radius).
- Therefore, the angle between gravity and displacement is **90°**.
- cos(90°) = **0**.

So W = F × d × 0 = **0 J**.

Gravity continuously changes the satellite's **direction** (centripetal acceleration) but does not change its **speed**. Since kinetic energy depends on speed, and speed is constant, the satellite's kinetic energy does not change. By the work-energy theorem (W = ΔKE), zero work means zero change in kinetic energy.

This is a profound result: gravity is the only force acting, yet it does no work! The satellite's motion is maintained by the geometry of the orbit, not by energy input from gravity.
