# Lesson 102: Energy: The Ability to Do Work

**Unit:** 5 — Work, Energy & Power | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define energy as the ability to do work.
2. Identify at least six forms of energy in the local environment.
3. Trace energy sources back to the Sun where possible.

## Materials
- Notebook
- Torch (flashlight)
- Rubber band
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Energy** *(nishati)* — the capacity to do work.
- **Chemical energy** *(nishati ya kemikali)* — energy stored in chemical bonds (food, fuel).
- **Kinetic energy** *(nishati ya harakati)* — energy of motion.
- **Potential energy** *(nishati ya uwezekano)* — stored energy due to position or condition.
- **Heat energy** *(nishati ya joto)* — energy due to temperature.
- **Light energy** *(nishati ya mwanga)* — energy carried by electromagnetic waves.
- **Sound energy** *(nishati ya sauti)* — energy carried by vibrations in a medium.

## Pre-Test
1. What is energy in physics terms?
2. Name three forms of energy you can find in the classroom.
3. Where does the energy in firewood come from originally?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The ability to do work.
2. Any three of: chemical (food), kinetic (moving fan), potential (raised book), heat (sunlight), light (bulb), sound (voices).
3. The Sun — captured by trees through photosynthesis.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands and list on board.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Turn off lights, then turn on a torch. Ask, "Where did the light energy come from?" Trace back: torch → battery → chemical energy → factory → coal/oil → ancient plants → Sun.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners hunt for energy forms around the school compound.
   - Find and record examples of:
     - Chemical: food in the kitchen, firewood, charcoal.
     - Kinetic: moving pupils, spinning bicycle wheel, flowing water.
     - Potential: water tank on a stand, book on a shelf, stretched rubber band.
     - Heat: cooking jiko, sun-warmed ground, body warmth.
     - Light: sun, torch, classroom windows.
     - Sound: talking, drumming, footsteps.
     - Electrical: phone charging (if available), battery-powered radio.
   - **Key discovery:** Energy is everywhere and comes in many forms. Most trace back to the Sun.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Energy = ability to do work.** If something has energy, it can exert a force over a distance.
   - **Forms:**
     - **Chemical:** Food gives your body energy; petrol powers a generator.
     - **Kinetic:** A moving boda has energy; faster = more energy.
     - **Potential:** Raised water in a tank can do work when released.
     - **Elastic:** A stretched rubber band stores energy.
     - **Heat:** Hot water can cook; hot air rises (kinetic).
     - **Light:** Sunlight grows crops; solar panels make electricity.
     - **Sound:** Vibrations carry small amounts of energy.
   - **Sun as source:**
     - Solar panels → electricity.
     - Plants → food and firewood.
     - Evaporation → rain → hydro power.
     - Wind → caused by solar heating.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner names one energy form and its source. Homework: draw an energy map of your home showing five forms.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Define energy in physics terms.
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* Where does the energy in food come from originally?
3. Name three forms of energy found in a kitchen.
4. A raised water tank has what kind of energy?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The **ability to do work**.
2. The **Sun** — via photosynthesis in plants.
3. Any three of: chemical (gas/charcoal), heat (jiko), kinetic (boiling water), potential (water in tank), light (bulb/sun).
4. **Gravitational potential energy**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Classify ten energy stores around your school compound and trace each back to the Sun where possible. Explain any that cannot be traced to the Sun.

**Worked Answer:**
**Ten examples:**

1. **Food in the kitchen** → Chemical → Sun (photosynthesis).
2. **Firewood pile** → Chemical → Sun (photosynthesis).
3. **Moving pupil** → Kinetic → Chemical (food) → Sun.
4. **Water tank on stand** → Gravitational potential → Sun (evaporation → rain).
5. **Bicycle wheel spinning** → Kinetic → Chemical (food) → Sun.
6. **Charcoal jiko burning** → Heat + light → Chemical → Sun (photosynthesis).
7. **Phone battery** → Electrical (chemical) → Solar panel or grid → Sun (if solar) or geothermal/nuclear.
8. **Wind moving leaves** → Kinetic → Sun (differential heating).
9. **Running stream** → Kinetic → Sun (evaporation → rain → flow).
10. **Radioactive rock (if any)** → Nuclear → **NOT from Sun** — from formation of Earth.

**Not traceable to Sun:**
- **Nuclear energy:** From atomic nuclei; created in stars (including our Sun) but also from supernovae and primordial elements.
- **Geothermal energy:** From Earth's internal heat (radioactive decay + primordial heat).
- **Tidal energy:** From Moon's gravity (mostly), not Sun.

Most everyday energy on Earth's surface traces to the Sun. Deep Earth and nuclear sources are exceptions.

**Q:** Explain why energy is called "the ability to do work" using the example of a stretched rubber band and a raised hammer.

**Worked Answer:**
**Stretched rubber band:**
The rubber band stores **elastic potential energy**. When released, this energy converts to kinetic energy, which can do work — fling a paper clip, power a toy plane, or close a bag. Without the stretch (energy input), the rubber band cannot do these things.

**Raised hammer:**
The hammer at height has **gravitational potential energy** = mgh. When dropped, this converts to kinetic energy. On impact, the kinetic energy does work — drives a nail into wood, breaks a stone, or flattens metal. A hammer held at ground level cannot do this work.

**General principle:**
Energy is stored capability. Work is energy in action. The amount of work a system can do equals its stored energy (ignoring losses). This is why energy and work share the same unit — the joule.
