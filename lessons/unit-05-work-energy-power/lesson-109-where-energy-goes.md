# Lesson 109: Where Does the Energy 'Go'? Friction and Heat

**Unit:** 5 — Work, Energy & Power | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that "lost" energy becomes heat due to friction.
2. Identify sources of friction in everyday Kenyan devices.
3. Describe how to reduce unwanted friction.

## Materials
- A bicycle wheel or any wheel
- Cooking oil or grease
- Sandpaper
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Friction** *(mgongano)* — a force that opposes motion between surfaces.
- **Heat** *(joto)* — energy transferred due to temperature difference.
- **Lubrication** *(kutoa mafuta)* — adding oil or grease to reduce friction.
- **Wear** *(kuchakaa)* — damage to surfaces caused by friction.

## Pre-Test
1. You rub your hands together fast. What do you feel?
2. Why does a bicycle chain need oil?
3. A matatu brakes hard. What happens to the kinetic energy?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Heat — friction converts kinetic energy to heat.
2. To reduce friction between chain and gears.
3. It becomes heat in the brake pads and discs.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Rub hands together vigorously. Ask, "Where did the heat come from?" Introduce friction as energy converter.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners test friction in three scenarios.
   - **Scenario 1:** Rub hands together → feel heat. Measure temperature rise (by feel or thermometer).
   - **Scenario 2:** Spin a bicycle wheel. It stops. Why? (Friction in bearings and air resistance.)
   - **Scenario 3:** Slide a book across a desk. It stops. Why? (Friction between book and desk.)
   - **Key discovery:** Friction converts kinetic energy to heat. This is where "lost" energy goes.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Friction converts motion to heat:**
     - Brakes: KE → heat in pads.
     - Rubbing hands: KE → heat in skin.
     - Engine: fuel energy → motion + heat (friction).
   - **Why friction is sometimes useful:**
     - Walking: friction prevents slipping.
     - Brakes: friction stops the vehicle.
     - Writing: friction between pen and paper makes marks.
   - **Why friction is sometimes unwanted:**
     - Engines: friction wastes fuel.
     - Moving parts: friction causes wear.
     - Heat: unwanted temperature rise.
   - **Reducing friction:**
     - Lubrication (oil, grease).
     - Smooth surfaces (polishing).
     - Rolling instead of sliding (ball bearings).
     - Streamlined shapes (reduce air resistance).
   - Kenyan context: Bodas need chain oil; posho mills need bearing grease; mkokoteni wheels need smooth bearings.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner names one useful and one harmful friction example. Homework: find three friction-reducing methods in your home.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Rubbing your hands together produces what?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Why does a bicycle chain need oil?
3. A matatu brakes from 20 m/s to stop. Where does the kinetic energy go?
4. Name two ways to reduce friction in a machine.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Heat** (from friction).
2. To **reduce friction** between moving parts.
3. It becomes **heat** in the brake pads and discs.
4. Any two of: lubrication, smooth surfaces, ball bearings, streamlined shapes.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A 1000 kg matatu travelling at 20 m/s brakes to a stop. Calculate (a) the initial kinetic energy, and (b) the average braking force if the stopping distance is 40 m. Assume all KE becomes heat in the brakes.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) KE = ½mv² = ½ × 1000 × (20)² = 500 × 400 = **200,000 J** (200 kJ).

(b) Work done by brakes = change in KE = −200,000 J.
W = F × d → −200,000 = F × 40 → F = **−5,000 N**.

The braking force is **5,000 N** opposite to motion.

**Temperature rise (optional):**
If brake pads have mass 5 kg and specific heat capacity 500 J/kg°C:
ΔT = Q/mc = 200,000 / (5 × 500) = 200,000 / 2,500 = **80°C**.

This is why brakes get very hot — and why repeated hard braking can overheat them.

**Q:** Explain why no machine can be 100% efficient, and calculate the efficiency of a motor that does 800 J of useful work from 2000 J of electrical input. Where does the remaining energy go?

**Worked Answer:**
**Why no machine is 100% efficient:**
1. **Friction:** Moving parts rub against each other, converting mechanical energy to heat.
2. **Air resistance:** Moving objects push air aside, wasting energy.
3. **Internal losses:** Electrical resistance in wires converts electricity to heat.
4. **Sound:** Vibrations carry away small amounts of energy.

These losses are unavoidable in real systems.

**Efficiency calculation:**
η = (useful work / energy input) × 100% = (800 / 2000) × 100% = **40%**.

**Remaining energy:**
2000 − 800 = **1200 J**.

This becomes:
- Heat in motor windings (~800 J).
- Heat in bearings (~200 J).
- Sound and vibrations (~100 J).
- Heat in air resistance (~100 J).

## Safety Notes
- Be careful with hot surfaces (brakes, engines).
- Handle oils and greases carefully — slippery.
- Standard classroom safety.
