# Lesson 110: Measuring Your Own Power

**Unit:** 5 — Work, Energy & Power | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define power as the rate of doing work.
2. Calculate their own power output.
3. Compare human power to common machine power.

## Materials
- A staircase or raised platform (at least 2 m vertical)
- A stopwatch
- A scale or known body weight
- A notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Power** *(nguvu/nishati)* — the rate at which work is done; work per unit time.
- **Watt** *(wati)* — unit of power; 1 W = 1 J/s.
- **Kilowatt** *(kilowati)* — 1000 watts.
- **Horsepower** *(nguvu ya farasi)* — an old unit ≈ 746 W.

## Pre-Test
1. If two people do the same amount of work but one is faster, who has more power?
2. What units measure power?
3. Estimate: how many watts can a person produce?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The faster one — power = work / time.
2. Watts (W) or kilowatts (kW).
3. Typical human sustained output: 50–100 W. Peak: ~400 W.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "Who is stronger — someone who lifts slowly or someone who lifts fast?" Introduce power as "how fast you do work."

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners measure their power.
   - Each learner climbs stairs (vertical height measured).
   - Measure: body mass (kg), vertical height (m), time to climb (s).
   - Calculate work: W = mgh.
   - Calculate power: P = W/t.
   - Compare within group. Who has highest power?
   - **Key discovery:** Power depends on how fast you do work, not just how much work.
   - Groups compare: a strong person climbing slowly vs a light person climbing fast.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Formula:** P = W/t = (F × d)/t. Also P = F × v (force × velocity).
   - **Units:** 1 watt = 1 joule per second. A 60 W bulb uses 60 J every second.
   - **Human power:**
     - Resting: ~100 W (body heat).
     - Walking: ~150 W.
     - Running up stairs: ~400 W (peak).
     - Sustained work: ~75 W.
   - **Machine comparison:**
     - Human: ~100 W sustained.
     - Boda engine: ~3000 W (3 kW).
     - Electric kettle: ~2000 W.
     - Small posho mill: ~5000 W (5 kW).
   - **Efficiency:** Human body is ~20% efficient. A boda engine is ~25% efficient.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states their calculated power. Homework: estimate the power of a boda engine.

## Hands-On Build: Stair Climb Power Test

### Parts
- Staircase or platform — min 2 m vertical — school
- Stopwatch — 1 — phone
- Scale or known weights — 1 — school
- Notebook — 1 per learner — classroom

### Workflow
1. Measure the vertical height of the stairs (not the slant distance).
2. Measure or estimate body mass.
3. Climb the stairs at a steady pace. Timer records time.
4. Calculate work: W = m × 10 × h.
5. Calculate power: P = W / t.
6. Compare with others.
7. **Checkpoint:** Measure vertical height, not slant. A common error is using stair length instead of height gain.

### Common Mistakes
- **Using slant distance:** Work against gravity uses vertical height only. Fix: measure height with a tape measure or level.
- **Running vs walking:** Running gives higher power but is harder to sustain. Fix: choose a sustainable pace.
- **Forgetting units:** Power in watts (W). Fix: always include units.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Two people do the same work. Who has more power?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* What units measure power?
3. A 50 kg pupil climbs 3 m in 6 s. Calculate their power output.
4. Why might a small, fast climber have more power than a large, slow climber?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The **faster** one (shorter time = higher power).
2. **Watts (W)** or kilowatts (kW).
3. W = 50 × 10 × 3 = 1500 J. P = 1500 / 6 = **250 W**.
4. Power = work/time. A small climber doing less work but in much less time can have higher power. Power is about rate, not total work.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A 60 kg athlete runs up a 10 m hill in 20 s. Calculate (a) work done against gravity, (b) power output, and (c) compare to a 100 W light bulb.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) W = mgh = 60 × 10 × 10 = **6,000 J**.

(b) P = W/t = 6,000 / 20 = **300 W**.

(c) The athlete produces **3× the power** of a 100 W bulb. This is why humans are remarkable machines — we can produce significant power from food alone.

**If the athlete walks the same hill in 60 s:**
P = 6,000 / 60 = **100 W** — same as the bulb.

This shows how dramatically power depends on the time taken to do the same work.

**Q:** A boda-boda engine produces 3 kW. If a human produces 100 W, how many humans would be needed to match one boda engine? Discuss why we use engines despite humans being more "fuel-efficient" in some ways.

**Worked Answer:**
Humans needed = 3000 / 100 = **30 humans**.

**Why we use engines:**
1. **Power density:** Engines produce more power per kilogram. A 30 kg engine replaces 30 humans (total mass ~2 tonnes).
2. **Endurance:** Engines run for hours without rest. Humans need breaks, food, and sleep.
3. **Consistency:** Engine power is steady. Human power varies with fatigue.
4. **Cost:** Petrol is cheaper than feeding 30 humans.

**Why humans are sometimes better:**
1. **Efficiency:** Humans are ~20% efficient at converting food to work; petrol engines are ~25%. Similar.
2. **Flexibility:** Humans adapt to varied tasks. Engines are specialized.
3. **No pollution:** Human waste is biodegradable. Engine emissions cause air pollution.

The choice depends on the task: engines for heavy, continuous work; humans for flexible, skilled work.
