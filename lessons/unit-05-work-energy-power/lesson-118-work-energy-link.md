# Lesson 118: The Work–Energy Link

**Unit:** 5 — Work, Energy & Power | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State the work-energy theorem: net work equals change in kinetic energy.
2. Apply the theorem to simple situations.
3. Explain why work and energy share the same unit.

## Materials
- Ball
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Work-energy theorem** *(kanuni ya kazi-nishati)* — the principle that net work equals change in kinetic energy.
- **Net work** *(kazi jumla)* — the total work done by all forces.
- **Change in kinetic energy** *(mabadiliko ya nishati ya harakati)* — ΔKE = KE_final − KE_initial.
- **Unified unit** *(kiwango kimoja)* — joule used for both work and energy.

## Pre-Test
1. What is the unit of work?
2. What is the unit of energy?
3. If net work on an object is 100 J, what happens to its kinetic energy?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Joule (J).
2. Joule (J).
3. It increases by 100 J (if positive work) or decreases by 100 J (if negative work).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Push a ball across the floor. Ask, "I did work on the ball. What happened to its energy?" (It gained kinetic energy.)

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners calculate work and energy changes.
   - **Scenario 1:** A 2 kg ball is pushed with 10 N for 5 m. Work = 50 J. Assuming it started from rest, KE_final = 50 J → v = √(2×50/2) = **7.07 m/s**.
   - **Scenario 2:** A 1 kg ball is thrown at 10 m/s. KE = 50 J. It slides and stops after 10 m. Average friction force = W/d = 50/10 = **5 N**.
   - **Key discovery:** Work done = change in KE. This connects force and motion to energy.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Work-energy theorem:** W_net = ΔKE = ½mv²_final − ½mv²_initial.
   - **Why same unit:** Work is energy transfer. When you do work, you transfer energy. The amount transferred equals the work done. Hence both measured in joules.
   - **Signs:**
     - Positive work (force in direction of motion): KE increases.
     - Negative work (force opposite motion, e.g., friction): KE decreases.
   - **Example:**
     - A car accelerates: engine does positive work, KE increases.
     - A car brakes: brakes do negative work, KE decreases (becomes heat).
   - Kenyan context: A mkokoteni pusher does work; the cart gains KE. When brakes are applied, KE becomes heat.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner states the work-energy theorem. Homework: calculate the work needed to accelerate a bicycle from rest to 5 m/s.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What is the unit of work?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* Net work on an object of 80 J results in what change in KE?
3. A 0.5 kg stone is thrown at 8 m/s. What is its KE?
4. A car of mass 1000 kg brakes from 20 m/s to 10 m/s. Calculate the work done by the brakes.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Joule (J)**.
2. **ΔKE = 80 J**.
3. KE = ½ × 0.5 × 64 = **16 J**.
4. Initial KE = ½ × 1000 × 400 = 200,000 J. Final KE = ½ × 1000 × 100 = 50,000 J. Work = 50,000 − 200,000 = **−150,000 J** (brakes do negative work).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Derive the work-energy theorem W = ΔKE from Newton's Second Law and the definition of work. Show all steps.

**Worked Answer:**
**Step 1: Newton's Second Law**
F = ma.

**Step 2: Work definition**
W = F × d (assuming constant force in direction of motion).

**Step 3: Substitute F**
W = ma × d.

**Step 4: Use kinematic equation**
v² = u² + 2ad → ad = (v² − u²)/2.

**Step 5: Substitute**
W = m × (v² − u²)/2 = ½mv² − ½mu² = ΔKE.

**Therefore:** W_net = ΔKE.

This derivation assumes constant force and straight-line motion, but the theorem holds for variable forces and curved paths.

**Q:** A 2 kg block slides down a frictionless slope from height 3 m. Calculate its speed at the bottom using (a) conservation of energy, and (b) the work-energy theorem. Show that both methods give the same result.

**Worked Answer:**
**(a) Conservation of energy:**
PE_top = mgh = 2 × 10 × 3 = **60 J**.
At bottom: PE = 0, KE = 60 J.
KE = ½mv² → 60 = ½ × 2 × v² → v² = 60 → v = **7.75 m/s**.

**(b) Work-energy theorem:**
Work done by gravity = F_gravity_parallel × d.
For a slope, the component of gravity along the slope = mg sin θ.
Distance d = h / sin θ.
W = mg sin θ × (h / sin θ) = mgh = **60 J**.
ΔKE = 60 J → same result: v = **7.75 m/s**.

Both methods agree because gravity is a conservative force — the work done depends only on height change, not path.
