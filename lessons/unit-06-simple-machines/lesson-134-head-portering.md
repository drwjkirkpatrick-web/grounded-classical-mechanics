# Lesson 134: Carrying Loads: The Physics of Head Portering

**Unit:** 6 — Simple Machines | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Analyse the physics of carrying loads on the head vs shoulders vs back.
2. Compare stability and energy efficiency of different carrying methods.
3. Design an improved load-carrying device.

## Materials
- Books or bags of varying weights
- A cloth pad or folded cloth
- A long stick or pole
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Head portering** *(kubeba kichwani)* — carrying loads balanced on the head.
- **Centre of gravity** *(kitovu cha uzito)* — the point where the combined weight of body and load acts.
- **Posture** *(mwili)* — the position of the body.
- **Load distribution** *(mgawanyo wa mizigo)* — how weight is spread across the body.

## Pre-Test
1. Why do women in many Kenyan villages carry loads on their heads?
2. What happens to your centre of gravity when you carry something on your head?
3. Is carrying on the head more or less stable than carrying on your back?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Hands remain free; load is balanced over the spine; culturally traditional.
2. It rises, potentially reducing stability.
3. Less stable (higher CG), but the load is directly over the spine, reducing bending strain.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask learners to balance a book on their head. Observe posture and gait.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners test three carrying methods.
   - **Head:** Balance book on head. Walk 5 m. Observe: upright posture, small steps, arms free.
   - **Shoulders:** Carry on one shoulder. Walk 5 m. Observe: leaning, uneven gait, one hand needed.
   - **Arms:** Carry in front. Walk 5 m. Observe: back strain, arms occupied.
   - **Key discovery:** Head carrying keeps the load aligned with the spine but raises CG.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Head carrying physics:**
     - Load is directly above spine → minimal bending moment.
     - Vertebrae compress evenly → less back strain.
     - CG rises → less stable (easier to topple).
     - Neck muscles work to stabilise.
   - **Shoulder carrying:**
     - Load off-centre → bending moment on spine.
     - One side compressed more → potential back injury.
     - CG stays lower → more stable.
   - **Optimal design:**
     - A head pad (cloth ring) distributes pressure.
     - A carrying pole (mkokoteni-style) allows balanced shoulder carrying.
   - **Energy:** Head carrying is efficient for long distances on flat ground. Uphill, a backpack is better.
   - Kenyan context: Head portering is traditional in East Africa. Studies show experienced porters use less oxygen than backpackers carrying the same load.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: Groups share their improved carrying designs. Homework: design a better load carrier.

## Hands-On Build: Load Carrying Comparison

### Parts
- Book or bag — 1 — classroom
- Cloth pad — 1 — cloth
- Long stick — 1 — ground
- Notebook — 1 per learner — classroom

### Workflow
1. Place book on head with cloth pad. Walk 5 m steadily.
2. Record observations: posture, speed, comfort, stability.
3. Carry book on one shoulder. Walk 5 m.
4. Record observations.
5. Carry book with stick across shoulders (like a yoke). Walk 5 m.
6. Compare all three methods.
7. **Checkpoint:** Ensure loads are similar weight. Use the same load for all trials.

### Common Mistakes
- **Different loads:** Comparing heavy on head vs light on shoulder. Fix: use same load.
- **Short distances:** Less noticeable differences. Fix: walk at least 5 m.
- **Ignoring posture:** Only measuring speed. Fix: observe and record posture too.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Why carry loads on the head?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* What happens to CG when carrying on the head?
3. Is head carrying more stable or less stable than shoulder carrying?
4. Suggest one improvement to head carrying.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Hands free, load over spine, traditional.
2. CG **rises**.
3. **Less stable** (higher CG) but more efficient for the spine.
4. Head pad, wider base, walking stick, or distributing load to a yoke.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A 20 kg load is carried on the head. Estimate (a) the additional compressive force on the spine, and (b) the bending moment if the same load is carried 30 cm in front of the body. Compare the strain.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) On head: Force = 20 × 10 = **200 N** straight down through spine.
    No bending moment (aligned with spine).

(b) In front: Force = 200 N still, but displaced 0.3 m forward.
    Bending moment = 200 × 0.3 = **60 Nm**.

**Comparison:**
Head carrying: 200 N compression, 0 Nm bending.
Front carrying: 200 N compression + 60 Nm bending.

The back muscles must generate a counter-moment of 60 Nm to prevent falling forward. This requires significant muscle force and causes fatigue.

**Q:** Research suggests experienced East African porters use 20% less energy than Europeans carrying the same load on their backs. Propose three biomechanical or physiological reasons for this difference.

**Worked Answer:**
1. **Pendulum gait:** Experienced porters let the load swing slightly in rhythm with walking, using the load's momentum. This reduces the work muscles must do to accelerate and decelerate the load.

2. **Muscle efficiency:** Years of head carrying strengthen neck and core muscles specifically. These muscles may be more efficient at stabilising the load than back muscles used for backpack carrying.

3. **Energy storage:** The elastic recoil of leg tendons and the slight bouncing of the load on the head may store and return energy, like a bouncing ball. This "passive spring" effect reduces active muscle work.

4. **Posture:** Head carrying maintains a straight spine. Backpack carrying, even with good posture, involves some forward lean to balance the load behind. This lean requires constant muscle work.

## Safety Notes
- Use light loads only (books, not heavy objects).
- Ensure walking area is clear.
- Those with neck or back problems should observe.
- Standard classroom safety.
