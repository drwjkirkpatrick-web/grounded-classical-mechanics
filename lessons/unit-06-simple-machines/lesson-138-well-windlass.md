# Lesson 138: Build: The Well Windlass

**Unit:** 6 — Simple Machines | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** well-windlass

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a working model of a well windlass.
2. Measure the mechanical advantage of their windlass.
3. Explain why the large wheel makes lifting easier.

## Materials
- Two cans or bottles (different sizes) for wheel and axle
- String or rope
- Pencil or stick for axle
- Load (stones or bag)
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Windlass** *(windlass)* — a device for lifting water using a wheel and axle.
- **Drum** *(ndoo)* — the cylinder around which rope wraps.
- **Handle** *(shikizo)* — the part turned to operate the windlass.
- **Lift** *(kuinua)* — raising a load vertically.

## Pre-Test
1. What simple machine is a windlass?
2. Why is the turning part larger than the lifting part?
3. What happens if the handle is very short?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Wheel and axle.
2. Larger wheel = greater MA = easier lifting.
3. MA is smaller → harder to lift (more force needed).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Ask, "Who has drawn water from a well? Was it hard?" Introduce windlass as a solution.

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners build a model windlass.
   - Large can = wheel/handle. Small can = axle/drum.
   - Attach string to small can. Wrap around axle.
   - Turn large can to wind string and lift load.
   - Measure: wheel radius, axle radius, load force, effort force.
   - **Key discovery:** Large wheel makes lifting easy but requires more turning.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **How it works:**
     - Turning the wheel (large radius) moves a small force through a large distance.
     - The axle (small radius) pulls the rope with a large force through a small distance.
     - MA = wheel radius / axle radius.
   - **Trade-off:**
     - Easy to turn (small effort) but must turn many times.
     - Fast to lift (large axle) but requires more force.
   - **Kenyan context:** Many rural wells still use hand windlasses. The design hasn't changed in centuries because it works.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: Groups demonstrate their windlasses. Most efficient design wins. Homework: calculate how many turns to lift a bucket 10 m.

## Hands-On Build: Model Well Windlass

### Parts
- Large can — 1 — kitchen
- Small can — 1 — kitchen
- String — 1 m — classroom
- Pencil — 1 — classroom
- Stones or bag — 1 — ground
- Tape — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Tape the small can to the pencil (axle).
2. Attach string to small can, wrap around it.
3. Attach load to free end of string.
4. Hold pencil ends, turn large can.
5. Observe load rising.
6. Measure wheel and axle radii.
7. Calculate MA.
8. **Checkpoint:** Before testing, ensure the string winds evenly on the small can. Overlapping string changes the effective radius.

### Common Mistakes
- **Loose axle:** Cans slip. Fix: tape securely.
- **String slipping:** Not attached to drum. Fix: tie knot through hole in can.
- **Overlapping string:** Increases effective radius. Fix: guide string evenly.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What simple machine is a windlass?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Why is the turning part larger?
3. A windlass has wheel radius 0.3 m and axle radius 0.05 m. Calculate MA.
4. What is the trade-off of a large wheel?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Wheel and axle**.
2. Larger wheel = **greater MA** = easier lifting.
3. MA = 0.3 / 0.05 = **6**.
4. Must turn **more times** (greater distance).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A well windlass has wheel radius 0.5 m and axle radius 0.08 m. Calculate (a) MA, (b) the force to lift a 25 kg bucket, (c) the distance the handle moves to raise the bucket 12 m, and (d) the work done by the person.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) MA = 0.5 / 0.08 = **6.25**.

(b) Load = 25 × 10 = 250 N. Effort = 250 / 6.25 = **40 N**.

(c) VR = 6.25. d_handle = 6.25 × 12 = **75 m**.

(d) Work = Effort × d_handle = 40 × 75 = **3000 J**.
   Or: Work = Load × height = 250 × 12 = **3000 J**. ✓

**Q:** Compare the well windlass to a direct hand-over-hand rope pull for lifting water. When would each be preferable?

**Worked Answer:**
**Windlass advantages:**
- Continuous operation (no need to re-grab rope).
- Can rest while holding position.
- Mechanical advantage reduces fatigue.
- Safer (hands away from rope if bucket slips).

**Direct pull advantages:**
- Faster for short lifts.
- No moving parts to break.
- Less setup.

**When to use each:**
- **Windlass:** Deep wells, heavy buckets, frequent use.
- **Direct pull:** Shallow wells, light buckets, occasional use.

In Kenya, most hand-dug wells (10–30 m) use windlasses. Very shallow wells (under 5 m) may use direct pull.
