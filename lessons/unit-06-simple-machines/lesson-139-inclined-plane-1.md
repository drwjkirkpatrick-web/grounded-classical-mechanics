# Lesson 139: The Inclined Plane I: Ramp vs Lift

**Unit:** 6 — Simple Machines | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define an inclined plane as a sloping surface that reduces the force needed to raise a load.
2. Show that the same work is done whether lifting directly or using a ramp.
3. Explain why a gentler slope requires less force but more distance.

## Materials
- Wooden plank or board
- Books or bricks for support
- A load (bag of stones or books)
- Spring balance or rubber band scale
- Tape measure
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Inclined plane** *(safu mlalo)* — a sloping surface that reduces the force needed to raise an object.
- **Ramp** *(rampu)* — an inclined plane used for moving objects up or down.
- **Slope** *(mteremko)* — the steepness of an inclined plane.
- **Direct lift** *(kuinua moja kwa moja)* — raising an object vertically without a machine.

## Pre-Test
1. Is it easier to push a load up a ramp or lift it straight up?
2. Does a ramp reduce the total work needed?
3. What happens to the required force as the ramp gets gentler?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Easier up a ramp (less force, more distance).
2. No — same work (ignoring friction).
3. Force decreases, but distance increases.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: Show a plank propped on books. Ask, "Would you rather push a bag up this ramp or lift it straight up?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners test inclined planes.
   - Set up a plank at 30° angle. Measure height (e.g., 0.5 m) and slope length.
   - Lift the load straight up with a spring balance. Record force.
   - Push the load up the plank. Record force.
   - Calculate: Force × distance for both methods.
   - **Key discovery:** Pushing up the ramp requires less force but over a longer distance. The product (work) is approximately the same.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Principle:**
     - Direct lift: Work = Weight × Height.
     - Ramp: Work = Force × Slope length.
     - Since slope length > height, the force is smaller.
   - **Why it works:**
     - The ramp distributes the vertical lift over a longer horizontal path.
     - Each small segment of the ramp lifts the object a tiny bit.
   - **Formula:**
     - Ideal case: F = (Weight × Height) / Slope length = Weight × sin θ.
     - Gentler slope (smaller θ) → smaller F.
   - **Friction:** In reality, friction adds extra work. Smooth ramps are better.
   - Kenyan context: Loading heavy goods onto lorries using planks. Building access ramps. Switchback roads on hills.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains the ramp trade-off. Homework: find three ramps in your environment.

## Hands-On Build: Ramp Force Comparison

### Parts
- Wooden plank — 1 — classroom
- Books or bricks — 5 — classroom
- Load (bag) — 1 — classroom
- Spring balance — 1 — classroom
- Tape measure — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Measure the load's weight with the spring balance.
2. Set up the plank at an angle. Measure height and slope length.
3. Pull the load up the plank at constant speed. Record force.
4. Calculate: Work_ramp = Force_ramp × slope length.
5. Calculate: Work_lift = Weight × height.
6. Compare the two values.
7. Repeat with a gentler slope.
8. **Checkpoint:** Pull at constant speed. Accelerating or decelerating changes the force reading.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not measuring at constant speed:** Accelerating requires extra force. Fix: pull slowly and steadily.
- **Ignoring friction:** Work_ramp > Work_lift due to friction. Fix: subtract estimated friction or accept the difference.
- **Wrong distance:** Using horizontal distance instead of slope length. Fix: measure along the plank.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Is it easier to push up a ramp or lift straight up?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q3)* What happens to required force as slope gentles?
3. A 50 N load is raised 1 m directly. How much work is done?
4. The same load is pushed up a 2 m ramp. Calculate the ideal force.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Ramp** — less force.
2. Force **decreases**.
3. W = 50 × 1 = **50 J**.
4. F = 50 / 2 = **25 N** (ideal, ignoring friction).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A 100 kg load is pushed up a 5 m ramp that rises 1.5 m. Calculate (a) the ideal pushing force, (b) the work done against gravity, and (c) the actual force if friction is 40 N. Calculate efficiency.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) Ideal force = mg × (h/L) = 100 × 10 × (1.5/5) = 1000 × 0.3 = **300 N**.

(b) Work against gravity = mgh = 100 × 10 × 1.5 = **1500 J**.

(c) Actual force = 300 + 40 = **340 N**.
    Total work = 340 × 5 = **1700 J**.
    Efficiency = (1500 / 1700) × 100% = **88.2%**.

**Q:** A wheelchair ramp should not exceed a 1:12 slope (1 unit rise for 12 units length). Calculate the angle and the force needed to push a 60 kg wheelchair up such a ramp. Why is this slope chosen?

**Worked Answer:**
Slope = 1/12. Angle: tan θ = 1/12 → θ = **4.76°**.

Force = mg × sin θ = 60 × 10 × sin(4.76°) = 600 × 0.083 = **49.8 N**.

(With friction: approximately 70–80 N total.)

**Why 1:12:**
- **Accessibility:** Manual wheelchair users can push themselves up without assistance.
- **Safety:** Steeper slopes risk backward tipping.
- **Comfort:** Gradual slopes are less strenuous for attendants.
- **Regulation:** International standard for public buildings.
