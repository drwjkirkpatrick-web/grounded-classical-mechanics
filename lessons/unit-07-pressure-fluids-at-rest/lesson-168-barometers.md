# Lesson 168: Build: A Bottle Barometer for Weather Watching

**Unit:** 7 — Pressure & Fluids at Rest | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a simple barometer from a bottle and tube.
2. Observe pressure changes over time.
3. Correlate pressure changes with weather patterns.

## Materials
- Glass bottle with narrow neck
- Clear tube (straw or glass)
- Water with food colouring
- Clay or putty
- Ruler
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Barometer** *(kifaa cha kupima shinikizo)* — instrument measuring atmospheric pressure.
- **High pressure** *(shinikizo kubwa)* — associated with clear weather.
- **Low pressure** *(shinikizo dogo)* — associated with clouds and rain.
- **Weather prediction** *(utabiri wa hali ya hewa)* — forecasting based on observations.

## Pre-Test
1. What does a barometer measure?
2. Does high pressure mean rain or sun?
3. Why would water level in a tube change?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Atmospheric pressure.
2. Usually sun (clear weather).
3. Pressure changes push on the bottle, displacing water.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Review safety.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Can we predict rain with a bottle and water?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners build bottle barometers.
   - Fill bottle most of the way with coloured water.
   - Insert tube, sealed with clay.
   - Water rises into tube.
   - Mark initial level.
   - Observe over days (or simulate with pressure changes).
   - **Key discovery:** Rising pressure pushes water down tube. Falling pressure lets water rise.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **How it works:**
     - Bottle is sealed (air inside is trapped).
     - Tube is open to atmosphere.
     - High outside pressure pushes water down tube.
     - Low outside pressure lets trapped air expand, water rises.
   - **Weather correlation:**
     - Rising pressure → clearing skies.
     - Falling pressure → approaching storm.
     - Rapid fall → storm soon.
   - **Limitations:**
     - Temperature affects trapped air.
     - Not as precise as mercury barometers.
     - Good for trends, not absolute values.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: Groups mark predicted weather. Homework: record barometer readings for a week.

## Hands-On Build: Bottle Barometer

### Parts
- Glass bottle — 1 — home
- Clear tube — 15 cm — hardware
- Coloured water — 0.5 L — tap
- Clay — 50 g — art supply
- Ruler — 1 — classroom
- Marker — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Fill bottle ¾ with coloured water.
2. Insert tube through clay seal.
3. Invert tube into water (or insert from top with seal).
4. Water rises into tube.
5. Mark initial level.
6. Seal all gaps with clay.
7. **Checkpoint:** Ensure the bottle seal is airtight. Any leak equalises pressure and ruins the barometer.

### Common Mistakes
- **Leaky seal:** No pressure difference. Fix: use lots of clay, test by gently squeezing bottle.
- **Tube too wide:** Small changes hard to see. Fix: use narrow tube.
- **Temperature changes:** Affects reading. Fix: keep in stable temperature.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What does a barometer measure?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Does high pressure mean rain or sun?
3. What happens to tube water when pressure rises?
4. Why might a bottle barometer be inaccurate?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Atmospheric pressure**.
2. **Sun** (clear weather).
3. Water level **drops** (pushed down by higher outside pressure).
4. **Temperature changes**, leaks, evaporation.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A bottle barometer shows the water level in the tube dropping 2 cm over a day. The tube has 0.5 cm² cross-section and the bottle has 100 cm² cross-section. Calculate the pressure change if the trapped air volume changes from 200 cm³ to 210 cm³ (water displaced from tube into bottle).

**Worked Answer:**
Water displaced = 2 cm × 0.5 cm² = 1 cm³.

This enters the bottle, increasing air volume by 1 cm³.

Using Boyle's Law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
P₁ × 200 = P₂ × 201
P₂ = P₁ × (200/201) = 0.995P₁

ΔP = P₁ − P₂ = P₁(1 − 0.995) = 0.005P₁ = **0.5% decrease**.

Atmospheric pressure ~100,000 Pa:
ΔP ≈ **500 Pa decrease**.

This indicates falling pressure — possible approaching rain.

**Q:** Compare the sensitivity of a bottle barometer (bottle area 100 cm², tube area 0.5 cm²) to a mercury barometer (tube area 1 cm²). Which detects smaller pressure changes, and by what factor?

**Worked Answer:**
**Bottle barometer:**
- Volume change for 1 cm tube movement: 0.5 cm³.
- In 100 cm² bottle: height change = 0.5/100 = 0.005 cm.
- Amplification factor: tube movement / bottle movement = 200×.

A small pressure change causes large tube movement.

**Mercury barometer:**
- Direct measurement: 1 cmHg ≈ 1333 Pa.
- No amplification.

**Sensitivity:**
The bottle barometer is more sensitive to small pressure changes because the large bottle amplifies the effect.

However, mercury barometers are more precise for absolute measurements.
