# Lesson 190: Pumps: Moving Water Against Gravity

**Unit:** 8 — Fluids in Motion, Siphons & Pumps | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain why pumps are needed to move water upward.
2. Identify the main types of pumps used in Kenya.
3. Describe the energy transformation in a pump.

## Materials
- Photos of pumps (hand pump, electric pump, windmill pump)
- Diagrams of pump types
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Pump** *(pampu)* — a device that moves fluid by mechanical action.
- **Suction** *(kuvuta)* — drawing fluid into a pump.
- **Discharge** *(kutoa)* — pushing fluid out of a pump.
- **Head** *(kimo)* — the vertical distance a pump can lift water.

## Pre-Test
1. Why can't a siphon lift water more than 10 m?
2. What does a pump do?
3. Name two types of pumps.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Atmospheric pressure limit.
2. Moves water against gravity.
3. Hand pump, centrifugal pump, force pump, etc.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Water flows downhill. How do we get it uphill?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners analyse pump types.
   - Examine photos of hand pumps, electric pumps, wind pumps.
   - Identify: input energy, output action, maximum lift.
   - Compare: cost, maintenance, reliability.
   - **Key discovery:** Pumps convert mechanical energy to fluid energy, overcoming gravity.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Why pumps are needed:**
     - Gravity moves water down.
     - To move up, need external energy.
     - Siphons limited to ~10 m by atmospheric pressure.
   - **Pump types (overview):**
     - **Lift pumps:** Use suction to raise water. Limited to ~10 m.
     - **Force pumps:** Push water under pressure. Can reach higher.
     - **Centrifugal pumps:** Spin water outward. High volume, moderate lift.
   - **Energy transformations:**
     - Hand pump: human muscle → mechanical → fluid pressure.
     - Electric: electrical → mechanical → fluid pressure.
     - Wind: kinetic (wind) → mechanical → fluid pressure.
   - Kenyan context: Afridev hand pumps in rural areas. Solar pumps for irrigation. Wind pumps in arid regions.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains why pumps beat siphons for deep wells. Homework: find pumps in your community.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** Compare the energy efficiency of a hand pump (10% efficient), a diesel pump (25% efficient), and a solar pump (15% efficient) for lifting 1000 L of water per day from 20 m depth. Calculate the daily energy input for each.

**Worked Answer:**
Work output = mgh = 1000 × 10 × 20 = **200,000 J**.

**Hand pump:** Input = 200,000 / 0.10 = **2,000,000 J** (human energy).

**Diesel pump:** Input = 200,000 / 0.25 = **800,000 J**.
   Diesel energy density ≈ 45 MJ/kg.
   Mass needed = 800,000 / 45,000,000 = **0.018 kg** (18 mL per day).

**Solar pump:** Input = 200,000 / 0.15 = **1,333,333 J**.
   Solar panel ≈ 200 W/m².
   Time needed = 1,333,333 / 200 = **6667 s** = **1.85 hours**.

**Comparison:**
- Diesel most efficient but requires fuel.
- Solar free but needs sun and capital cost.
- Hand pump cheapest but requires labour.

**Q:** Explain why a pump at the bottom of a deep well is more effective than a pump at the top trying to suck water up.

**Worked Answer:**
**Pump at top (suction):**
- Creates partial vacuum above water.
- Atmospheric pressure pushes water up.
- Maximum lift: ~10 m (atmospheric pressure limit).
- Below 10 m: pump cavitates, loses suction.

**Pump at bottom (submersible):**
- Pushes water up directly.
- No atmospheric pressure limit.
- Can lift water hundreds of metres.
- More efficient (no suction losses).
- Less prone to cavitation.

**Conclusion:**
For wells deeper than 10 m, submersible pumps are essential.
