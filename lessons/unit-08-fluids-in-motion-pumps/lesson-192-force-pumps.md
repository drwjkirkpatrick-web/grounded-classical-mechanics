# Lesson 192: Force Pumps: Pushing Water Upward

**Unit:** 8 — Fluids in Motion, Siphons & Pumps | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain how a force pump pushes water under pressure.
2. Distinguish lift pumps from force pumps.
3. Describe the advantage of force pumps for deep wells.

## Materials
- Diagram of force pump
- Photos of deep well pumps
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Force pump** *(pampu ya kushinikiza)* — a pump that pushes water under pressure.
- **Pressure chamber** *(chombo cha shinikizo)* — a chamber that stores pressurised water.
- **Air chamber** *(chombo cha hewa)* — a chamber with trapped air that smooths flow.
- **Delivery pipe** *(bomba la kutoa)* — the pipe carrying water upward under pressure.

## Pre-Test
1. What is the difference between a lift pump and a force pump?
2. Why can a force pump reach deeper than a lift pump?
3. What is the purpose of an air chamber?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Lift pump sucks; force pump pushes.
2. It pushes water under pressure, not limited by atmospheric suction.
3. Smoothes pulsating flow into continuous stream.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "A lift pump can only reach 10 m. How do we get water from a 30 m well?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners compare pump types.
   - Examine diagrams of lift vs force pumps.
   - Trace water path in each.
   - Identify pressure differences.
   - Calculate maximum depth for each.
   - **Key discovery:** Force pumps push water under pressure, overcoming the suction limit.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Force pump operation:**
     - Upstroke: same as lift pump — suction draws water into cylinder.
     - Downstroke: valve closes, piston pushes water upward under pressure.
     - Water forced into delivery pipe and air chamber.
     - Air chamber compresses, storing energy for continuous flow.
   - **Advantages:**
     - Can reach any depth (limited only by pressure and pipe strength).
     - Continuous flow (no waiting for upstroke).
     - Can deliver to elevated tanks.
   - **Disadvantages:**
     - More complex mechanism.
     - Higher maintenance.
     - More expensive.
   - Kenyan context: Deep borehole pumps. Afridev pumps convert from lift to force mode. Community water systems use force pumps.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains why force pumps beat lift pumps for deep wells. Homework: find the deepest well in your area.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What is the difference between lift and force pumps?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Why can force pumps reach deeper?
3. What does the air chamber do?
4. Name one advantage of a force pump.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Lift sucks**; **force pushes**.
2. **Pressure pushing** overcomes suction limit.
3. **Smoothes flow** (stores energy).
4. Any: **reaches any depth**, **continuous flow**, **delivers to height**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A force pump delivers water to a tank 25 m above the pump. Calculate the minimum pressure the pump must generate. If the pump cylinder has area 50 cm², what force must the operator exert?

**Worked Answer:**
P = ρgh = 1000 × 10 × 25 = **250,000 Pa**.

F = P × A = 250,000 × 0.005 = **1250 N**.

This is heavy for human power — typically use mechanical advantage (long handle) or motor.

**Q:** Explain the energy storage mechanism in an air chamber and why it is essential for hand-operated force pumps.

**Worked Answer:**
**Energy storage:**
- Downstroke: piston pushes water into chamber, compressing air.
- Air compresses, storing elastic potential energy.
- Between strokes: compressed air expands, pushing water continuously.
- No air chamber: water flows only during downstroke — intermittent.

**Why essential:**
- Human can only pump 30–60 strokes per minute.
- Water demand is continuous.
- Air chamber bridges the gap between strokes.
- Provides steady pressure for taps and sprinklers.
- Reduces water hammer (pressure spikes).

**Design:**
- Chamber typically 1/3 air, 2/3 water.
- Air compresses to 2–3× atmospheric pressure.
- Large chamber = smoother flow but slower pressure build-up.
