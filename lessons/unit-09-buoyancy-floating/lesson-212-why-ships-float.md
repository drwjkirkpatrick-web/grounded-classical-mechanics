# Lesson 212: Why Ships Float: Shape Beats Material

**Unit:** 9 — Buoyancy & Floating | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain why hollow shapes float even when made of dense material.
2. Build a floating vessel from dense material.
3. Calculate the carrying capacity of a simple boat.

## Materials
- Clay or modelling dough
- Water container
- Small weights (stones, coins)
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Displacement hull** *(umbo la kukimbia)* — a hull shape that pushes aside water.
- **Carrying capacity** *(uwezo wa kubeba)* — maximum weight a vessel can hold while floating.
- **Freeboard** *(ubora wa kuelea)* — distance from waterline to deck.
- **Hull shape** *(umbo la marikebu)* — the form of a vessel's body.

## Pre-Test
1. Why does a steel ship float when a steel nail sinks?
2. What shape helps something float?
3. What happens if you put too much weight in a boat?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Ship is hollow; average density is less than water.
2. Wide, hollow shape that displaces much water.
3. It sinks deeper; may take on water and capsize.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "I give you clay. Make it float."

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners shape clay boats.
   - Start with solid clay ball — sinks.
   - Hollow it out — floats.
   - Add "cargo" (small weights) until it sinks.
   - Try different shapes: wide vs narrow, deep vs shallow.
   - **Key discovery:** Shape matters more than material. Wide hollow shapes displace more water.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Why shape matters:**
     - Solid steel nail: small volume, high density → sinks.
     - Steel ship: huge hollow volume, low average density → floats.
     - The ship is mostly air!
   - **Carrying capacity:**
     - Maximum weight = weight of water displaced at sinking point.
     - Wider hull = more displacement = more capacity.
     - Deeper hull = more displacement but less stable.
   - **Stability:**
     - Low centre of gravity → stable.
     - Wide base → stable.
     - Narrow tall shape → tips easily.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: Best boat design wins. Homework: draw a ship cross-section.

## Hands-On Build: Clay Boat Challenge

### Parts
- Clay — 200 g — art supplies
- Water container — 1 — classroom
- Weights — 10 — stones/coins
- Notebook — 1 per learner

### Workflow
1. Roll clay into ball — test (sinks).
2. Flatten into dish shape.
3. Test (should float).
4. Add weights one by one.
5. Count before sinking.
6. Try different shapes.
7. **Checkpoint:** Make walls thick enough. Thin walls collapse when loaded.

### Common Mistakes
- **Thin walls:** Collapse. Fix: thicker walls.
- **Too narrow:** Tips easily. Fix: wide base.
- **Water in boat:** Sinks. Fix: ensure no leaks.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Why steel ship floats?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* What shape helps float?
3. What determines carrying capacity?
4. Why is a wide boat more stable?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Hollow**, average density **less than water**.
2. **Wide, hollow** shape.
3. **Volume of water displaced**.
4. **Low centre of gravity**, hard to tip.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A rectangular barge 5 m × 3 m sinks 0.4 m when loaded. Calculate (a) the displaced water volume, (b) the barge's total weight, and (c) the additional load it can carry before sinking 0.6 m.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) V = 5 × 3 × 0.4 = **6 m³**.

(b) Weight = ρVg = 1000 × 6 × 10 = **60,000 N** = **6 tonnes**.

(c) At 0.6 m: V = 5 × 3 × 0.6 = 9 m³.
    Weight capacity = 9 tonnes.
    Additional load = 9 − 6 = **3 tonnes**.

**Q:** Explain the concept of metacentric height and how it determines whether a ship will return to upright after being tilted.

**Worked Answer:**
**Metacentre (M):**
- Point where buoyancy force line intersects centreline when tilted.
- Metacentric height (GM) = distance from centre of gravity (G) to metacentre.

**Stability:**
- GM > 0 (M above G): stable. Tilted ship returns upright.
- GM < 0 (M below G): unstable. Ship capsizes.

**Physics:**
- When tilted, centre of buoyancy shifts toward submerged side.
- Buoyancy and gravity create a restoring couple if M is above G.
- If M below G, couple pushes further from upright.

**Factors:**
- Wide beam → high M → stable.
- Low cargo → low G → stable.
- Top-heavy → high G → unstable.
