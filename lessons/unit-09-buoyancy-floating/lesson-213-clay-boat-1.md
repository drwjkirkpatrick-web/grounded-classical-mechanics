# Lesson 213: Clay Boat Challenge I: Design for Cargo

**Unit:** 9 — Buoyancy & Floating | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** clay-boat

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Design a clay boat for maximum cargo capacity.
2. Apply principles of displacement and stability.
3. Plan and sketch a boat design before building.

## Materials
- Clay — 200 g per group
- Water container
- Weights (stones, coins, washers)
- Ruler
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Design plan** *(mpango wa ubunifu)* — a drawing or description before building.
- **Cargo** *(mizigo)* — load carried by a vessel.
- **Stability** *(imara)* — resistance to tipping over.
- **Displacement** *(kukimbia)* — volume of water pushed aside.

## Pre-Test
1. What shape holds the most weight?
2. Why does a wide boat tip less?
3. How do you plan before building?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Wide, hollow shape with deep walls.
2. Low centre of gravity, wide base.
3. Sketch, list materials, estimate dimensions.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Groups review Lesson 212.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Design a boat that carries the most cargo. You have one piece of clay."

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners design clay boats.
   - Sketch design first (plan).
   - Decide: wide or narrow? Deep or shallow? Thick or thin walls?
   - Build from 200 g clay.
   - Test in water.
   - **Key discovery:** Planning helps. Wide, deep boats with thick walls carry more.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Design considerations:**
     - Volume: more displacement = more cargo.
     - Stability: wide base = stable.
     - Strength: thick walls resist collapse.
     - Freeboard: walls above waterline prevent swamping.
   - **Trade-offs:**
     - Wide = stable but harder to shape.
     - Deep = more volume but tips easier.
     - Thin walls = lighter but weaker.
   - **Prediction:**
     - Calculate expected capacity from planned dimensions.
     - V_displaced = length × width × draft.
     - Capacity = ρ_water × V × g.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: Groups show designs. Tomorrow we test! Homework: refine sketch.

## Hands-On Build: Clay Boat Design

### Parts
- Clay — 200 g — art supplies
- Ruler — 1 — classroom
- Paper — 1 sheet — classroom
- Pencil — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Sketch boat design (top view and side view).
2. Label dimensions.
3. Estimate displacement.
4. Build from clay.
5. Check: walls thick? base wide?
6. **Checkpoint:** Sketch must show dimensions. A design without measurements is just a drawing.

### Common Mistakes
- **No plan:** Build randomly. Fix: sketch first.
- **Too thin:** Walls collapse. Fix: minimum 5 mm thick.
- **No freeboard:** Water enters. Fix: walls above waterline.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What shape holds most weight?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Why wide boat tips less?
3. Calculate: boat 10 cm × 8 cm × 3 cm deep, sinks 2 cm. Displaced volume?
4. Name one trade-off in boat design.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Wide, hollow, deep**.
2. **Low centre of gravity**.
3. V = 10 × 8 × 2 = **160 cm³**.
4. Any: **wide vs shapeable**, **deep vs stable**, **thin vs strong**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A clay boat design is 12 cm × 10 cm × 4 cm overall. Walls are 1 cm thick. Calculate (a) the internal volume, (b) the clay volume, and (c) the maximum cargo before the freeboard reaches zero.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) Internal: 10 × 8 × 3 = **240 cm³**.

(b) Clay = total − hollow = (12×10×4) − 240 = 480 − 240 = **240 cm³**.

(c) Displacement at waterline: 12 × 10 × 4 = 480 cm³.
    Weight of boat (clay ≈ 1.5 g/cm³) = 240 × 1.5 = 360 g.
    Cargo = displacement − boat weight = 480 − 360 = **120 g**.

**Q:** Explain why a catamaran (twin-hull) design is more stable than a single-hull boat of the same total displacement.

**Worked Answer:**
**Catamaran stability:**
- Two hulls widely separated.
- Centre of buoyancy shifts less when tilted.
- Large restoring moment arm.

**Single hull:**
- Centre of buoyancy shifts toward submerged side.
- Smaller moment arm.
- More likely to capsize.

**Physics:**
- Stability depends on distance between centre of gravity and centre of buoyancy.
- Catamaran: G and B widely separated horizontally.
- Tilted: B moves outward, creating strong restoring torque.
- Single hull: B moves less, weaker restoring torque.
