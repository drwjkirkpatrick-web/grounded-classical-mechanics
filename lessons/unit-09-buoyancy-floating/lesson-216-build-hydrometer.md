# Lesson 216: Build: A Pencil Hydrometer

**Unit:** 9 — Buoyancy & Floating | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a working hydrometer from simple materials.
2. Calibrate it using known-density liquids.
3. Use it to measure unknown liquid densities.

## Materials
- Pencil with eraser
- Small nails or weights
- Ruler
- Water and salt
- Tall container
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Homemade hydrometer** *(hidromita ya nyumbani)* — a density-measuring device made from simple materials.
- **Calibration** *(kupiga mizani)* — marking known values on a scale.
- **Reference liquid** *(kioo cha maji)* — a liquid of known density for calibration.
- **Scale marking** *(alama za kipimo)* — lines showing density values.

## Pre-Test
1. What makes a hydrometer float upright?
2. How do you calibrate a hydrometer?
3. Where on the pencil will the waterline be: high or low?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Weight at the bottom (low centre of gravity).
2. Float in known liquids, mark positions.
3. Depends on density — this is the point of calibration.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Can we make a hydrometer from a pencil? Yes!"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners build pencil hydrometers.
   - Push nails into pencil eraser end (add weight).
   - Test in water. Mark waterline.
   - Add salt. Mark new line.
   - Compare: lines show density differences.
   - **Key discovery:** Simple materials make useful instruments. Weight at bottom keeps it upright.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Construction:**
     - Pencil provides long stem for reading.
     - Nails at bottom provide weight and stability.
     - More nails = lower float (more displacement needed).
   - **Calibration:**
     - Waterline in pure water = reference mark.
     - Waterline in salt water = denser mark (higher float position on pencil).
     - More marks = more precise scale.
   - **Use:**
     - Float in unknown liquid.
     - Compare mark position to calibration marks.
     - Estimate density.
   - **Limitations:**
     - Less precise than commercial hydrometer.
     - Needs recalibration.
     - Works best for large density differences.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: Test hydrometers on mystery liquids. Homework: calibrate with sugar water.

## Hands-On Build: Pencil Hydrometer

### Parts
- Pencil — 1 — classroom
- Small nails — 3–5 — hardware
- Water — 500 mL — tap
- Salt — 50 g — kitchen
- Tall container — 1 — classroom
- Marker — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Push nails into eraser end of pencil.
2. Test in water. Floats upright?
3. Mark waterline on pencil.
4. Label "water".
5. Add salt to water. Stir.
6. Float again. Mark new line.
7. Label "salt water".
8. **Checkpoint:** Pencil must float upright. If it tips, add more nails at the bottom.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not enough weight:** Pencil lies sideways. Fix: add more nails.
- **Weight at top:** Pencil flips. Fix: weight at bottom.
- **Marks too close:** Hard to distinguish. Fix: use different salt concentrations.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What makes it float upright?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* How calibrate?
3. A pencil floats higher in salt water. What does this tell you?
4. Name one limitation of a pencil hydrometer.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Weight at bottom**.
2. **Float in known liquids**, mark positions.
3. **Salt water is denser** — less volume needed to displace weight.
4. Any: **less precise**, **needs recalibration**, **large differences needed**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A pencil hydrometer has mass 15 g with centre of gravity 5 cm from the bottom. The pencil is 15 cm long and 0.8 cm diameter. Calculate how many centimetres of pencil remain above water when floating in fresh water.

**Worked Answer:**
Displaced volume = 15 cm³ (since 1 g water = 1 cm³).

Pencil cross-section = π × (0.4)² = 0.503 cm².

Submerged length = 15/0.503 = **29.8 cm**.

But pencil is only 15 cm long! This means the pencil is fully submerged plus some weight needs adjusting.

Let me recalculate: if 15 g displaces 15 cm³, and pencil volume per cm = 0.503 cm³, then submerged length = 15/0.503 = 29.8 cm.

Since pencil is only 15 cm, this won't float with just pencil. Need to add buoyancy or reduce weight.

This is a design problem! The question reveals a practical issue. Let me address it.

**Worked Answer (revised):**
The pencil as described cannot float with 15 g total mass — it would need to be fully submerged and still not displace enough water. 

**Solutions:**
1. Use a lighter pencil (mass < 7.5 g so half is submerged).
2. Add a cork or float at the top.
3. Reduce nail weight.

**Practical design:**
- Pencil mass: 5 g.
- Nails: 5 g.
- Total: 10 g.
- Displaced volume: 10 cm³.
- Submerged length: 10/0.503 = **19.9 cm**.

Still too long for 15 cm pencil. Need cork float!

**Conclusion:** Real hydrometers have bulbs (large volume) at bottom. The pencil alone is insufficient. A cork or wider base is needed.

**Q:** Explain why a commercial hydrometer has a weighted bulb at the bottom and a narrow stem at the top, and how this design maximises sensitivity.

**Worked Answer:**
**Weighted bulb:**
- Large volume provides buoyancy.
- Weight at bottom keeps hydrometer upright (low centre of gravity).
- Fixed mass concentrated at bottom.

**Narrow stem:**
- Small cross-section means small volume changes produce large height changes.
- Easy to read small density differences.
- Sensitivity = Δh/Δρ. Narrow stem = large Δh for small Δρ.

**Sensitivity calculation:**
- Stem area = 0.1 cm².
- For Δρ = 1 kg/m³: ΔV ≈ 0.05 cm³.
- Δh = 0.05/0.1 = 0.5 cm = **5 mm per kg/m³**.

**Wide stem comparison:**
- Area = 1 cm².
- Δh = 0.05/1 = 0.05 cm = 0.5 mm per kg/m³.
- **10 times less sensitive**.
