# Lesson 217: Layers of Liquid: The Egg in Salt Water

**Unit:** 9 — Buoyancy & Floating | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that density determines floating level in liquids.
2. Create liquid density layers.
3. Predict where objects will float in layered liquids.

## Materials
- Water
- Salt
- Oil
- Egg
- Tall transparent container
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Density layering** *(safu za msisimko)* — liquids arranged by density with densest at bottom.
- **Immiscible** *(isiyo changamana)* — liquids that don't mix.
- **Interface** *(kipengele)* — the boundary between two liquid layers.
- **Neutral buoyancy** *(ufunuo wa kati)* — when an object floats at a specific depth.

## Pre-Test
1. Will an egg float in fresh water?
2. What happens if you add salt to water?
3. Where would an egg float: oil, water, or salt water?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. No (egg density ≈ 1030 kg/m³ > water).
2. Water becomes denser; egg may float.
3. In salt water (densest of the three).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q1 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Drop an egg in water. Now add salt. What happens?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners create density layers.
   - Fresh water + egg → sinks.
   - Add salt gradually → egg rises.
   - Continue adding salt → egg floats.
   - Add oil on top → egg sits between oil and salt water.
   - **Key discovery:** Density controls floating level. Different liquids have different densities.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Density layering:**
     - Densest liquid at bottom.
     - Least dense at top.
     - Objects float at their density level.
   - **Egg densities:**
     - Fresh water: 1000 kg/m³ → egg sinks.
     - Salt water: 1020–1050 kg/m³ → egg may float.
     - Saturated salt water: ~1200 kg/m³ → egg floats.
   - **Applications:**
     - Ocean currents: warm/fresh water on top, cold/salty below.
     - Oil spills: oil floats on water.
     - Separating mixtures by density.
   - Kenyan context: Lake Turkana (salty) vs Lake Victoria (fresh). Dead Sea (Israel/Jordan) — extreme buoyancy.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains why the Dead Sea is easy to float in. Homework: make a three-layer liquid.

## Hands-On Build: Liquid Density Layers

### Parts
- Water — 300 mL — tap
- Salt — 100 g — kitchen
- Oil — 100 mL — kitchen
- Egg — 1 — kitchen
- Tall container — 1 — classroom
- Spoon — 1 — kitchen

### Workflow
1. Fill container with fresh water.
2. Drop egg. Observe (sinks).
3. Remove egg.
4. Add salt, stir.
5. Drop egg. Observe (may float).
6. Add oil carefully.
7. Drop egg. Observe (sits at interface).
8. **Checkpoint:** Add salt gradually. Too much salt at once creates uneven density.

### Common Mistakes
- **Too much salt at once:** Egg shoots up. Fix: add gradually.
- **Stirring oil in:** Layers mix. Fix: pour oil gently.
- **Broken egg:** Messy. Fix: place gently.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Egg in fresh water?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* What happens with salt?
3. Where does oil sit: top or bottom?
4. Why does the egg float higher in salt water?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Sinks**.
2. Water becomes **denser**.
3. **Top** (less dense than water).
4. **Salt water is denser** — egg needs less displacement to float.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** An egg has mass 60 g and volume 58 cm³. Calculate (a) its density, (b) the minimum salt water density needed for it to float, and (c) the salt concentration by mass if saturated salt water is 1200 kg/m³.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) ρ = 60/58 = **1.034 g/cm³** = **1034 kg/m³**.

(b) Must float: ρ_liquid > ρ_egg → **> 1034 kg/m³**.

(c) In 1 L (1000 g) of saturated solution:
    Total mass = 1200 g.
    Salt mass = 1200 − 1000 = **200 g**.
    Concentration = 200/1200 = **16.7% by mass**.

**Q:** Explain why the oceans have distinct layers (thermocline and halocline) and how these layers affect marine life and climate.

**Worked Answer:**
**Thermocline:**
- Warm water (less dense) on top.
- Cold water (denser) below.
- Boundary: temperature changes rapidly.

**Halocline:**
- Salty water (denser) below.
- Fresh water (less dense) on top.
- Boundary: salinity changes rapidly.

**Combined effects:**
- Warm, salty water can be denser than cold, fresh water.
- Complex layering in estuaries and polar regions.

**Marine life:**
- Nutrients trapped below thermocline.
- Upwelling brings nutrients up.
- Different species at different depths.

**Climate:**
- Ocean currents redistribute heat.
- Thermohaline circulation: global conveyor belt.
- Disruption affects weather patterns.
