# Lesson 218: Submarines and Swim Bladders

**Unit:** 9 — Buoyancy & Floating | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain how submarines and fish control buoyancy.
2. Build a simple submarine model.
3. Compare natural and engineered buoyancy control.

## Materials
- Plastic bottle
- Coins or weights
- Syringe or tube
- Water
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Ballast tank** *(tangazo la uzito)* — a tank that fills with water to make a vessel sink.
- **Swim bladder** *(pumbu la kuogelea)* — an organ in fish that controls buoyancy.
- **Neutral buoyancy** *(ufunuo wa kati)* — floating at constant depth.
- **Trim** *(urekebisho)* — adjusting balance for level floating.

## Pre-Test
1. How does a submarine dive?
2. How does a fish stay at one depth?
3. What do submarines and fish have in common?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Fills ballast tanks with water.
2. Adjusts swim bladder size.
3. Both control buoyancy by changing average density.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q3 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Submarines sink AND float. Fish stay at one depth. How?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners build submarine models.
   - Bottle with some air inside.
   - Add weights to just barely float.
   - Squeeze bottle — sinks.
   - Release — rises.
   - **Key discovery:** Compressing air changes volume → changes buoyancy.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Submarines:**
     - Surface: ballast tanks full of air (light).
     - Dive: fill tanks with water (heavy).
     - Neutral: balance water and air.
     - Rise: blow water out with compressed air.
   - **Fish:**
     - Swim bladder: gas-filled organ.
     - Expand bladder → more volume → float up.
     - Contract bladder → less volume → sink.
     - No energy needed to maintain depth.
   - **Comparison:**
     - Both change volume to control buoyancy.
     - Submarine uses mechanical pumps.
     - Fish uses biological gas exchange.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains submarine vs fish buoyancy. Homework: research deep-sea fish without swim bladders.

## Hands-On Build: Bottle Submarine

### Parts
- Plastic bottle — 1 — home
- Coins — 3–5 — classroom
- Water — 1 L — tap
- Container — 1 — classroom

### Workflow
1. Partially fill bottle with water (some air inside).
2. Add coins until it just barely floats.
3. Place in container.
4. Squeeze bottle (compresses air).
5. Observe: it sinks.
6. Release: it rises.
7. **Checkpoint:** Fine-tune weight until neutral buoyancy. Too heavy sinks permanently; too light floats permanently.

### Common Mistakes
- **Too heavy:** Sinks immediately. Fix: remove coins.
- **Too light:** Won't sink. Fix: add coins.
- **No air space:** Can't compress. Fix: leave air gap.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* How submarine dive?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* How fish stay at depth?
3. What happens when you squeeze a floating bottle?
4. Name one similarity between submarines and fish.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Fills ballast tanks** with water.
2. **Adjusts swim bladder** size.
3. **Sinks** (compressed air reduces volume).
4. Both **control buoyancy** by changing volume/density.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A submarine displaces 2000 m³ of seawater (ρ = 1025 kg/m³) when surfaced. Its mass is 1500 tonnes. Calculate (a) the additional water mass needed in ballast tanks for neutral buoyancy, and (b) the ballast tank volume required.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) Displaced mass when surfaced = 1025 × 2000 = **2,050,000 kg** = 2050 tonnes.
    Already 1500 tonnes → can add 550 tonnes before sinking.
    But for neutral buoyancy at same displacement: need total mass = 2050 tonnes.
    Additional water = 2050 − 1500 = **550 tonnes**.

(b) Volume = m/ρ = 550,000 / 1025 = **537 m³**.

**Q:** Explain why deep-sea fish often lack swim bladders, and how they achieve neutral buoyancy without them.

**Worked Answer:**
**Why no swim bladder:**
- Extreme pressure at depth (1000 m = 100 atm).
- Swim bladder gas would compress to tiny volume.
- Gas exchange with blood becomes impossible.
- Bladder wall would need to be impossibly strong.

**Alternative strategies:**
1. **Watery tissues:** Body 90%+ water. Water is incompressible, matches surrounding density.
2. **Light skeletons:** Cartilage instead of bone (less dense).
3. **High lipid content:** Oils less dense than water, provide buoyancy without gas.
4. **Active swimming:** Constant motion to stay aloft (like sharks).

**Comparison:**
- Swim bladder: energy-efficient, depth-limited.
- Watery body: works at any depth, but less manoeuvrable.
