# Lesson 222: Hot Air Rises: Buoyancy in Gases

**Unit:** 9 — Buoyancy & Floating | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE;UP-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that buoyancy applies to gases as well as liquids.
2. Describe why hot air rises.
3. Identify examples of gas buoyancy in everyday life.

## Materials
- Candle or heat source
- Paper strips or tissue
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Gas buoyancy** *(ufunuo wa gesi)* — upward force on an object in a gas.
- **Convection** *(mchakato wa joto)* — heat transfer by rising and sinking fluid.
- **Hot air balloon** *(puto la hewa ya moto)* — a balloon lifted by heated air.
- **Atmospheric buoyancy** *(ufunuo wa anga)* — buoyant force from surrounding air.

## Pre-Test
1. Does buoyancy work in air?
2. Why does hot air rise?
3. Name something that floats in air.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Yes.
2. Hot air is less dense than cool air.
3. Hot air balloon, helium balloon, smoke.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Hold paper over a candle. What happens? Why?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners test hot air.
   - Hold paper strip above candle. It rises.
   - Compare: strip at room temperature doesn't rise.
   - Discuss: warm air vs cool air around candle.
   - **Key discovery:** Hot air rises because it's less dense. This is buoyancy in gases.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Gas buoyancy:**
     - Same principle as liquids: less dense rises, denser sinks.
     - Hot air: molecules move faster, spread apart → less dense.
     - Cool air: molecules closer → denser.
   - **Examples:**
     - Hot air balloons: heat air inside → rises.
     - Smoke: hot gas rises from fire.
     - Weather: warm air rises, creates clouds.
     - Gliders: ride rising warm air.
   - **Convection:**
     - Warm air rises, cools, sinks.
     - Creates circulation patterns.
     - Drives weather and climate.
   - Kenyan context: Hot air balloons over Masai Mara. Smoke from jikos rises. Convectional rains from Lake Victoria.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains why a hot air balloon works. Homework: find examples of convection at home.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A hot air balloon has volume 3000 m³. The air inside is heated to 80°C while outside air is 20°C. Air density at 20°C is 1.2 kg/m³ and at 80°C is 1.0 kg/m³. Calculate (a) the weight of air displaced, (b) the weight of hot air inside, and (c) the lifting capacity.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) Displaced weight = ρ_outside × V × g = 1.2 × 3000 × 10 = **36,000 N**.

(b) Hot air weight = ρ_inside × V × g = 1.0 × 3000 × 10 = **30,000 N**.

(c) Lifting capacity = 36,000 − 30,000 = **6,000 N**.
    Mass that can be lifted = 6,000/10 = **600 kg**.
    (Minus balloon mass, basket, fuel, people.)

**Q:** Explain why a hot air balloon cannot reach space, and calculate the maximum altitude it could theoretically reach if outside air density decreases by 10% per 1000 m.

**Worked Answer:**
**Why not space:**
- Space has no air → no buoyancy.
- Balloon needs surrounding air to displace.
- At high altitude: air too thin to provide lift.
- Maximum altitude where buoyancy still exceeds total weight.

**Calculation:**
Assume at sea level: ρ = 1.2 kg/m³.
After n km: ρ = 1.2 × (0.9)ⁿ.

Need ρ_outside > ρ_inside (heated to 0.8 kg/m³ at all altitudes).

1.2 × (0.9)ⁿ > 0.8
(0.9)ⁿ > 0.667
n × ln(0.9) > ln(0.667)
n > ln(0.667)/ln(0.9) = −0.405/−0.105 = **3.86 km**.

Maximum altitude ≈ **3800 m**.

(Actual limit lower due to balloon fabric strength, fuel limitations.)
