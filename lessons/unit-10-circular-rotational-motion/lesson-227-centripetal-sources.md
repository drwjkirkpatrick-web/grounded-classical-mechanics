# Lesson 227: Sources of Centripetal Force

**Unit:** 10 — Circular & Rotational Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.5;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** No | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Identify different sources of centripetal force in everyday situations.
2. Explain which force provides the inward pull in each case.
3. Predict what happens when the centripetal force is insufficient.

## Materials
- Photos or diagrams of circular motion situations
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Tension** *(utando)* — pulling force through a string or rope.
- **Friction** *(msuguano)* — force between surfaces that resists sliding.
- **Gravity** *(mvuto)* — attractive force between masses.
- **Normal force** *(nguvu ya kawaida)* — perpendicular force from a surface.

## Pre-Test
1. What force keeps a satellite in orbit?
2. What force lets a car turn a corner?
3. What happens if friction is too low on a curved road?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Gravity.
2. Friction between tyres and road.
3. Car slides outward (off the road).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "What do a satellite, a car turning, and a spinning top have in common?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners identify centripetal sources.
   - List situations: satellite, car turning, bucket overhead, electron in atom, boda turning, planet orbiting.
   - For each: what provides the inward force?
   - Discuss: what happens if force is removed?
   - **Key discovery:** Gravity, friction, tension, and normal forces can all act as centripetal forces. The source depends on the situation.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Tension:**
     - String, rope, chain pulling inward.
     - Examples: spinning stone, bucket overhead, pendulum.
   - **Friction:**
     - Between tyres and road.
     - Between shoes and floor (spinning on ice).
     - Examples: car turning, person on merry-go-round.
   - **Gravity:**
     - Pulls satellite toward Earth.
     - Pulls planet toward Sun.
     - Examples: orbits, free-fall trajectories.
   - **Normal force:**
     - Wall of rotating cylinder pushes inward.
     - Banked road pushes partly inward.
     - Examples: rotor ride, banked turns.
   - **When force is insufficient:**
     - F_centripetal < mv²/r → object follows straighter path.
     - Car skids, satellite escapes, string breaks.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains three sources of centripetal force. Homework: find two more examples.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* Force keeping satellite in orbit?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Force for car turning?
3. What force keeps a bucket of water moving overhead?
4. Name the centripetal source for a planet orbiting the Sun.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Gravity**.
2. **Friction** (between tyres and road).
3. **Tension** in the arm/string.
4. **Gravity** (from the Sun).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A car of mass 1200 kg takes a corner of radius 50 m at 72 km/h. Calculate (a) the centripetal force required, and (b) the minimum coefficient of friction needed.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) v = 72 km/h = 20 m/s.
    F = mv²/r = 1200 × 20² / 50 = 1200 × 400 / 50 = **9600 N**.

(b) Friction provides centripetal force: μN = μmg = F.
    μ = F/(mg) = 9600/(1200 × 10) = 9600/12000 = **0.8**.

**Q:** Explain why a satellite in low Earth orbit experiences apparent weightlessness, even though gravity is still acting on it with nearly the same strength as at the surface.

**Worked Answer:**
**Weightlessness explanation:**
- Satellite and astronaut are both in free fall toward Earth.
- Gravity provides exactly the centripetal force needed for orbit.
- Both accelerate at same rate.
- No normal force from floor pushes up on astronaut.
- Without normal force, no sensation of weight.

**Why not zero gravity:**
- g at 400 km altitude ≈ 8.7 m/s² (vs 9.8 at surface).
- Only ~11% reduction — still strong gravity.
- "Microgravity" is more accurate than "zero gravity".

**Comparison:**
- Standing on Earth: floor pushes up (N = mg), you feel weight.
- In orbit: no floor, no normal force, no weight sensation.
- In free-fall elevator: same effect briefly.
