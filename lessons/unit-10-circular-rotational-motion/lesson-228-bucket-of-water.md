# Lesson 228: The Bucket of Water Overhead

**Unit:** 10 — Circular & Rotational Motion | **Duration:** 45 minutes | **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.5;JS-FE | **Hands-On:** Yes | **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that water stays in an upside-down bucket when swung fast enough.
2. Explain that centripetal force keeps the water moving in a circle.
3. Calculate the minimum speed needed to keep water in the bucket.

## Materials
- Bucket or cup with handle
- Water
- Strong arm or rope
- Towel
- Notebook for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **Vertical circle** *(duara wima)* — circular motion in a vertical plane.
- **Critical speed** *(kasi muhimu)* — the minimum speed to maintain circular motion.
- **Overhead swing** *(mzingo wa juu)* — swinging a bucket in a vertical circle.
- **Weightlessness** *(kutokuwa na uzito)* — condition where normal force is zero.

## Pre-Test
1. What happens if you turn a cup of water upside down?
2. Can you swing water in a bucket overhead without spilling?
3. What keeps the water in the bucket at the top?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Water falls out (gravity pulls it down).
2. Yes, if swung fast enough.
3. The bucket pushes inward, providing centripetal force.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **(5 min)** Settle and administer the Pre-Test. Collect Q2 by show of hands.

2. **(5 min)** Engage: "Can you hold water upside down without a lid?"

3. **(15 min)** Explore:
   - **Activity:** In groups of 4, learners try the bucket swing.
   - Fill bucket partway with water.
   - Swing in vertical circle.
   - Start slow — water spills at top.
   - Increase speed — water stays.
   - Try different amounts of water.
   - **Key discovery:** Fast enough swing keeps water in. The bucket pushes water inward, countering gravity.

4. **(8 min)** Explain:
   - **Why water stays:**
     - At top of swing: bucket pushes water downward (toward centre).
     - This inward force + gravity = centripetal force.
     - If fast enough: inward force ≥ gravity → water pressed into bucket.
     - If too slow: gravity > inward force → water falls.
   - **Critical speed:**
     - Minimum speed at top: v = √(rg).
     - r = radius, g = 10 N/kg.
     - For r = 1 m: v = √10 ≈ 3.16 m/s.
   - **Feeling weightless:**
     - At exactly critical speed: bucket barely pushes water.
     - Water feels momentarily weightless.
     - Same principle as astronauts in orbit.

5. **(10 min)** Post-Test (see below). Learners work individually, then mark in pairs.

6. **(2 min)** Wrap: One learner explains critical speed. Homework: calculate critical speed for your arm length.

## Hands-On Build: Bucket Swing

### Parts
- Bucket with handle — 1 — home
- Water — 1 L — tap
- Towel — 1 — classroom
- Space to swing — outdoors or high ceiling

### Workflow
1. Fill bucket 1/3 with water.
2. Hold handle firmly.
3. Swing arm in vertical circle.
4. Start slow. Observe spillage.
5. Increase speed until water stays.
6. Try at different radii.
7. **Checkpoint:** Arm must be fully extended at top. Bent arm reduces radius and increases required speed.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not fast enough:** Water spills. Fix: swing faster.
- **Bent arm:** Changes radius unpredictably. Fix: keep arm straight.
- **Too full:** Heavy and dangerous. Fix: 1/3 full maximum.

## Post-Test
1. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q1)* What happens upside down?
2. *(Mirrors Pre-Test Q2)* Can you swing without spilling?
3. What keeps water in at the top?
4. Calculate critical speed for r = 0.8 m.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. **Falls out** (slowly) if not moving.
2. **Yes**, if **fast enough**.
3. **Bucket pushing inward** (centripetal force).
4. v = √(rg) = √(0.8 × 10) = √8 = **2.83 m/s**.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

**Q:** A bucket of water is swung in a vertical circle of radius 1.2 m. Calculate (a) the minimum speed at the top to prevent spilling, (b) the tension in the arm at the bottom if speed is 6 m/s and bucket + water mass is 2 kg.

**Worked Answer:**
(a) v_top = √(rg) = √(1.2 × 10) = √12 = **3.46 m/s**.

(b) At bottom: T − mg = mv²/r (tension up, weight down, net inward).
    T = m(g + v²/r) = 2(10 + 36/1.2) = 2(10 + 30) = 2 × 40 = **80 N**.

**Q:** Explain why a roller coaster loop must be designed with a radius large enough that the cart doesn't fall off at the top, and calculate the minimum radius for a cart entering the loop at 15 m/s.

**Worked Answer:**
**Why radius matters:**
- At top of loop: cart must have enough speed for centripetal force.
- If too slow: cart falls off track (gravity > required centripetal).
- Minimum condition: normal force = 0, gravity alone provides centripetal.

**Calculation:**
Energy conservation: ½mv²_bottom = ½mv²_top + mg(2r).

At minimum: v_top = √(rg).
So: ½v²_bottom = ½rg + 2gr = 2.5rg.

r = v²_bottom / (5g) = 225 / 50 = **4.5 m**.

**Design implication:**
- Loop must be at least 4.5 m radius for 15 m/s entry speed.
- Larger radius = safer but less thrilling.
- Real coasters use clothoid loops (teardrop shape) for comfort.
