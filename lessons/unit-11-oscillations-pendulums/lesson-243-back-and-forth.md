# Lesson 243: Back and Forth: The World Oscillates

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Identify oscillating motion in everyday Kenyan objects and situations.
2. Define cycle, period, frequency, and amplitude in their own words.
3. Distinguish oscillating motion from one-way (linear) motion using a simple test.
4. Relate the idea of "back and forth" to energy shifting between two forms.

## Materials
- A pendulum made in class: string tied to a stand or door frame with a stone bob
- A ruler or stick to deflect and release the pendulum
- A sufuria (cooking pot) and a spoon — for a quick ringing demo
- A rubber strip cut from an inner tube, clamped to a desk with a stone hanging from it
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **oscillation** (mtetemo) — motion that repeats back and forth about a centre point
- **cycle** (mzunguko) — one complete back-and-forth trip, returning to the start
- **period** (kipindi) — time for one cycle, measured in seconds
- **frequency** (mara kwa sekunde) — number of cycles per second, in hertz (Hz)
- **amplitude** (ukiwa) — how far the object swings from the centre (size of the swing)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A child on a swing goes forward, up, back, down, and returns to where she started. How many complete cycles is that?
2. Name two things in your home or village that move back and forth repeatedly.
3. If a swing takes 2 seconds to go all the way forward and all the way back, what is its period?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. One complete cycle. Forward-up-back-down-return = one full back-and-forth.
2. Accept any two: pendulum clock, swing, guitar string, shaking sieve, washing on a line in wind, water sloshing in a bucket, a bouncing ball (accept debate).
3. 2 seconds. Period = time for one complete cycle.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before the demo so learners commit to a prediction.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — stand the pendulum at the front. Pull the stone to one side and release. Ask the class to count silently: how many swings before it stops. Then strike a sufuria with a spoon — the sound fades. Ask: *what do the swing and the ringing pot have in common?* Board the word **oscillation**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in pairs, learners list every oscillating thing they can find in a Kenyan home, market, or farm: swing, pendulum clock, washing line in wind, guitar string, sieve shaken, water sloshing in a jerrican, boda-boda mirror vibrating after a bump, windscreen wiper, tree branch in wind, a bouncing ball (debatable — why?). For each, they identify the centre point and what makes it return. Then introduce the four key terms by labelling the pendulum: one full swing = **cycle**; time for one = **period**; cycles per second = **frequency**; how far from centre = **amplitude**. Learners sketch the pendulum and label each term on their sketch.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Every oscillation has: a **centre** (equilibrium), a **restoring push** that brings it back, and energy sloshing between two forms — for the pendulum, potential energy at the top of each swing, kinetic energy at the bottom. Confront the misconception that oscillation means "going round and round" — a wheel turns, a pendulum oscillates. The test: does it reverse direction? If yes, it oscillates.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *oscillation is nature's rhythm*. Homework: find one oscillating object at home, sketch it, label the centre and the amplitude, and write down how long one cycle takes (use a phone stopwatch or count seconds).

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Define oscillation in one sentence. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Name two things in your home that oscillate. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A pendulum makes 20 cycles in 25 seconds. What is its period? What is its frequency?
4. A wheel spins round and round. Is that oscillation? Explain why or why not.
5. Challenge: a guitar string vibrates at 200 Hz. What is its period? How many cycles does it complete in 3 seconds?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Motion that repeats back and forth about a centre point.
2. Accept any two valid examples (swing, pendulum, string, water sloshing, etc.).
3. Period = 25 s ÷ 20 = 1.25 s. Frequency = 1 ÷ 1.25 = 0.8 Hz.
4. No. A wheel rotates in one direction continuously; it never reverses. Oscillation requires reversing direction.
5. Period = 1 ÷ 200 = 0.005 s (5 milliseconds). In 3 s: 200 × 3 = 600 cycles.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A pendulum makes 20 cycles in 25 s. Compute the period and frequency with correct units.
   **Worked Answer:** Period T = 25 s / 20 = 1.25 s. Frequency f = 1/T = 1/1.25 = 0.80 Hz. Units: T in seconds (s), f in hertz (Hz) = s⁻¹.
2. **Q:** The pendulum in Q1 has an amplitude of 8 cm. A second pendulum has the same period but amplitude 4 cm. What is the same and what is different about their motion?
   **Worked Answer:** Same period (1.25 s) and frequency (0.80 Hz) — these depend on the system, not the swing size (for small angles). Different amplitude: the first swings twice as far from centre. Both return to centre each half-cycle.
3. **Q:** A learner says "the pendulum oscillates because the air pushes it back." Design a simple test or argument to challenge this claim.
   **Worked Answer:** Push the pendulum sideways and release — it returns toward centre even though air resistance acts *against* the motion (slowing it, not restoring it). The restoring force is gravity: the component of weight along the arc pulls the bob back toward equilibrium. Air actually damps the oscillation, it does not drive it.

## Safety Notes
- Suspend the pendulum securely — a falling stone bob can injure feet. Tie the string to a fixed point, not held by hand.
- Keep the swing amplitude small for the demo — large swings can hit objects or people.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have met force, weight, and energy (Units 2 and 5). This lesson opens Unit 11 by naming what learners have already seen.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on identifying oscillations and labelling cycle/period. Ages 14–17 should derive frequency from period and reason about why the restoring force exists.
- Local resource tips: any heavy object on string — a stone, a bunch of keys, a debe lid with a hole. A door frame or a clamp stand works as the pivot.
- This lesson is conceptual; the hands-on experiments begin in Lesson 244. Keep the pendulum demo short — it is a hook, not the main event.
