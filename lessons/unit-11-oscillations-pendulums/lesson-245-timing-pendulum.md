# Lesson 245: Timing a Pendulum Properly

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Time a pendulum correctly using the "start at the bottom" technique.
2. Explain why timing 10 swings and dividing is more accurate than timing one swing.
3. Calculate period from multi-swing measurements with correct units.
4. Estimate and express measurement uncertainty in a period value.

## Materials
- Pendulum from Lesson 244 (string ~50 cm, bob, stand)
- Stopwatch (phone stopwatch is fine) or a watch with a second hand
- Notebook and pencil
- A tally counter or a sheet of tally marks

## Vocabulary
- **period (T)** (kipindi) — time for one complete cycle, in seconds
- **timing** (kupima muda) — the act of measuring a time interval
- **uncertainty** (hitilafu) — the range within which the true value likely lies
- **repeatability** (kurudia) — getting a similar result each time you measure

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Your pendulum swings back and forth once in about 1 second. How would you measure this — start the stopwatch when the bob is at the far left, or when it passes through the bottom?
2. If you time one swing and get 1.2 s, then time it again and get 0.9 s, what does this tell you?
3. A friend times 10 swings and gets 15 s. What is the period?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Start at the bottom (the equilibrium point, fastest moment). This is the sharpest, most repeatable trigger point — the bob is moving fastest, so the moment is easy to catch.
2. Timing a single swing is unreliable — human reaction time is a large fraction of 1 s. The variation shows the measurement is uncertain.
3. Period = 15 s ÷ 10 = 1.5 s.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — ask a learner to time one swing of the pendulum with a phone stopwatch. Do it three times. The results will differ by 0.2–0.5 s. Ask: *why do the answers change?* Board: human reaction time (~0.2 s) is a big fraction of a 1-second swing.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs practise the correct technique (see Hands-On Build). The key skill: start the stopwatch as the bob passes the bottom (fastest point), count 10 complete cycles, and stop at the bottom of the 10th. Each pair does three trials, records each, and divides by 10. They compare their three results and estimate the spread. Then swap roles — the timer becomes the counter and vice versa — and do three more trials.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Why 10 swings? The reaction-time error (~0.2 s) is the same whether you time 1 swing or 10. Over 10 swings it becomes 0.2 s out of ~15 s — a 1% error instead of 20%. Dividing by 10 gives the period with a 10× smaller uncertainty. Why start at the bottom? The bob is fastest there, so the crossing is sharp and repeatable; at the extremes it pauses and the exact "moment of stop" is subjective. Introduce uncertainty: if three trials give 1.98, 2.02, 2.00 s, the period is 2.00 ± 0.02 s.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *measure many, divide — never trust a single swing*. Homework: time the pendulum at home using 20 swings and report the period with uncertainty.

## Hands-On Build: Pendulum Timing Technique
*Practise the correct timing method on the pendulum built in Lesson 244.*

### Parts
- Pendulum from Lesson 244 — string ~50 cm, bob, stand
- Stopwatch (phone) — one per pair
- Tally sheet — paper and pencil

### Workflow
1. Set the pendulum swinging gently (small amplitude, ~10 cm).
2. **Checkpoint:** the amplitude should stay roughly constant during the 10 swings — if it dies too fast, re-start with a gentle push.
3. Stand beside the pendulum, eye level with the bob's lowest point. Place a ruler or pencil as a reference mark at the bottom.
4. Say "zero" as the bob passes the reference going right, and start the stopwatch on the *next* pass through the bottom. **Checkpoint:** you are starting at the bottom, not at the extreme.
5. Count each complete cycle: 1, 2, 3 … 10. Stop the stopwatch as the bob passes the bottom at count 10.
6. Record the total time. Divide by 10 for the period. Repeat three times.
7. Report: period = average ± spread (e.g., 2.00 ± 0.02 s).

### Common Mistakes
- **Starting at the extreme** — the pause makes the start ambiguous; always start at the bottom.
- **Counting "one" on the start** — start the watch on "zero" (the first crossing), count "one" on the next crossing in the same direction.
- **Large amplitude** — the pendulum slows noticeably over 10 swings; keep it small so the period stays constant.
- **Stopping at the wrong pass** — stop when the bob crosses the bottom in the same direction it started, not on the return.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Where should you start the stopwatch — at the extreme or at the bottom? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why is timing 10 swings and dividing by 10 better than timing a single swing? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Three trials of 10 swings give: 19.8 s, 20.2 s, 20.0 s. Calculate the period with uncertainty.
4. A learner times 5 swings in 7.5 s, then 20 swings in 30 s. Both give the same period. Which is likely more accurate, and why?
5. Challenge: your reaction time is 0.2 s. What is the percentage error in timing (a) one swing of 1.0 s and (b) 10 swings totalling 10 s (then dividing)?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. At the bottom. The bob is fastest there, so the crossing moment is sharp and repeatable. At the extreme the bob pauses, making the start/stop ambiguous.
2. The reaction-time error (~0.2 s) is fixed regardless of how many swings you time. Over 10 swings it is 0.2 s out of ~10–20 s (1–2%) instead of 0.2 s out of ~1 s (20%).
3. Period for each: 1.98, 2.02, 2.00 s. Average = 2.00 s. Spread = ±0.02 s. Period = 2.00 ± 0.02 s.
4. The 20-swing measurement. The same reaction-time error is spread over more cycles, giving a smaller percentage uncertainty.
5. (a) 0.2/1.0 × 100 = 20%. (b) 0.2/10 × 100 = 2% (and dividing by 10 keeps the period at 2% uncertainty).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Ten swings give times of 19.8, 20.2, and 20.0 s. Give the period with uncertainty and explain the 10-swing gain in precision.
   **Worked Answer:** Total times: 19.8, 20.2, 20.0 s → average 20.0 s, spread ±0.2 s. Period = 20.0/10 = 2.00 s, uncertainty ±0.2/10 = ±0.02 s → T = 2.00 ± 0.02 s. The 10-swing method spreads the ~0.2 s reaction-time error over 10 cycles, so the per-cycle uncertainty shrinks by a factor of 10. A single swing of ~2.0 s would carry the full ±0.2 s (10% error) versus ±0.02 s (1% error) here.
2. **Q:** A learner starts the stopwatch at the left extreme instead of the bottom. Explain why this introduces a systematic error and estimate its size.
   **Worked Answer:** At the extreme, the bob momentarily stops — the "moment" of zero speed lasts several tens of milliseconds, so different observers (or the same observer on repeated trials) pick different start times. This is a systematic error because it consistently biases the result in the same direction (likely late, since the eye waits for the "stop"). Estimated size: 0.05–0.1 s per start/stop — smaller than the reaction time but biased, not random.
3. **Q:** Design an experiment to determine whether your reaction time differs between starting and stopping the stopwatch.
   **Worked Answer:** Have a partner clap at random intervals. Start the watch at one clap, stop at the next. Repeat 20 times. Compare the mean to the known interval (use a phone timer to generate the claps at fixed 1.0 s gaps). If the mean is consistently >1.0 s, your reaction time is adding a positive bias. Repeat the experiment with you starting *and* stopping vs. a partner doing one of the two, to isolate start vs. stop error.

## Safety Notes
- Secure the pendulum stand — a falling bob can injure.
- Learners should not lean into the swing path while timing.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 244 established the pendulum's anatomy and the PE↔KE picture. This lesson builds the measurement skill needed for every subsequent investigation in the unit.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the 10-swing technique and simple averaging. Ages 14–17 should compute uncertainty, percentage error, and distinguish random from systematic error.
- Local resource tips: phone stopwatches are sufficient. If none available, count seconds aloud ("one-Mombasa, two-Mombasa…") — less precise but teaches the same principle.
- This lesson's technique is the backbone of Lessons 246–248 (the three "what affects the period?" experiments). Practise it well.
