# Lesson 247: What Affects the Period? Test: Amplitude

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Set up a fair test varying only the release angle (amplitude) while keeping length and mass fixed.
2. Measure and compare periods at small and large amplitudes.
3. Identify the small-angle regime where period is nearly independent of amplitude.
4. Quantify where the small-angle approximation breaks down (≈30°).

## Materials
- Pendulum from Lesson 244 (string ~50 cm, bob, stand)
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Protractor card — paper with a protractor traced or printed on it, mounted behind the pendulum
- Notebook and pencil
- A reference mark at the bottom (ruler or pencil)

## Vocabulary
- **amplitude** (ukiwa) — how far the bob swings from centre, measured as angle or distance
- **small-angle approximation** — the rule that period is constant when amplitude is small (under ~15°)
- **fair test** (jaribio la haki) — changing only one variable at a time
- **breakdown** (kuvunjika) — the point where the approximation fails and the period changes

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you pull a pendulum back just a little (5°) versus a lot (45°), do you expect the periods to be the same or different?
2. In this experiment, what must you keep the same and what do you change?
3. Why do we use a protractor card in this lesson?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Most predict the larger swing takes longer (it has farther to go). The surprise: for small angles they are nearly the same; only at large angles does the period increase noticeably.
2. Keep the same: string length, bob mass, pivot. Change: the release angle (amplitude).
3. To measure and set the release angle precisely so the test is fair.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Note the prediction split.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate two swings side by side: a gentle 5° swing and a big 45° swing. Ask the class to watch and guess: which takes longer per cycle? Board predictions. (Most will say the big one is slower — and for large angles they are right, but by a surprisingly small amount.)
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs set up the amplitude test (see Hands-On Build). Each pair measures the period at three angles: 10°, 30°, and 60°, using the 10-swing method with three trials each. Results go on a class table: angle | period. If results are noisy, pool the class data on the board.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. At 10° and 30° the periods are nearly the same (within uncertainty) — this is the **small-angle regime**. At 60° the period is noticeably longer (5–10% increase). The small-angle approximation says: for angles under about 15°, the period is practically independent of amplitude. It breaks down beyond ~30°. Confront the misconception that "bigger swing always means bigger period" — it is true but only weakly for small angles, and strongly for large angles. This is why pendulum clocks use small swings.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *small swings keep time*. Homework: measure your pendulum's period at 10° and at 45° at home and report the difference (if any).

## Hands-On Build: Amplitude Fair Test
*Test whether release angle changes the period, keeping length and mass fixed.*

### Parts
- Pendulum from Lesson 244 — string ~50 cm, bob, stand
- Protractor card — mounted behind the pendulum at the pivot, so the angle can be read
- Stopwatch — phone
- Reference mark — a ruler or pencil at the bottom

### Workflow
1. Mount the protractor card behind the pendulum so 0° is at the bottom (string vertical). **Checkpoint:** when hanging at rest, the string lines up with 0°.
2. Pull the bob to 10° and release (no push). Time 10 swings, three trials. Record period.
3. Pull to 30° and release. Time 10 swings, three trials. **Checkpoint:** the protractor must stay fixed; do not move it between trials.
4. Pull to 60° and release. Time 10 swings, three trials.
5. Record all three periods on the class table. Compare 10° vs 30° vs 60°.
6. If time allows, try 5° as well — it should match 10°.

### Common Mistakes
- **Pushing instead of releasing** — a push adds energy and changes the amplitude mid-swing. Just open the hand.
- **Protractor not aligned** — if 0° is not at vertical, all angles are off. Check the resting position first.
- **Large amplitude dying too fast** — at 60°, the pendulum slows noticeably over 10 swings. Accept the first few cycles; or time 5 swings instead.
- **String stretching** — a heavy bob at 60° may stretch the string slightly, changing the length. Use a non-stretchy string.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. For small angles (under 15°), does the period change when you increase the amplitude? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What must you keep the same in this experiment? What do you change? (mirrors pre-test)
3. At 10° the period is 1.42 s. At 60° it is 1.50 s. What does this tell you about the small-angle approximation?
4. Why do pendulum clocks use small swings (a few degrees) rather than large ones?
5. Challenge: measure the period at 10°, 30°, and 60°. At which angle does the period start to increase noticeably?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. No — for small angles, the period is nearly independent of amplitude. This is the small-angle approximation.
2. Keep the same: string length, bob mass, pivot, protractor position. Change: the release angle (amplitude).
3. At 10° the period is 1.42 s; at 60° it is 1.50 s — about 5.6% longer. The small-angle approximation holds well at 10° but breaks down by 60°.
4. Small swings keep the period constant (within the small-angle regime). Large swings would make the clock run at different rates depending on amplitude, which changes as the clock winds down or temperature shifts.
5. The period starts to increase noticeably above ~30°. Below 30° the increase is small (under 2%); above 30° it grows rapidly.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Measure the period at 10°, 30°, and 60°. Quantify the breakdown and explain the small-angle approximation.
   **Worked Answer:** Typical results (T₀ = period at small angle): 10° → T ≈ T₀; 30° → T ≈ 1.02 T₀ (2% increase); 60° → T ≈ 1.07 T₀ (7% increase). The small-angle approximation says sin θ ≈ θ (in radians) for small θ. At 10° (0.17 rad), sin θ = 0.174 — error is 2%. At 30° (0.52 rad), sin θ = 0.500 — error is 4%. At 60° (1.05 rad), sin θ = 0.866 — error is 17%. The period depends on sin θ, not θ directly, so the approximation fails as the angle grows.
2. **Q:** The exact formula for a pendulum period at large angles involves a series: T = T₀(1 + θ²/16 + …). Using this, predict the percentage increase at 30° and 60°. Compare with your measurements.
   **Worked Answer:** At 30° (θ = 0.524 rad): θ²/16 = 0.275/16 = 0.017 → 1.7% increase → T = 1.017 T₀. At 60° (θ = 1.047 rad): θ²/16 = 1.096/16 = 0.069 → 6.9% increase → T = 1.069 T₀. These match the typical measured values within experimental uncertainty.
3. **Q:** A pendulum clock is running slow because its swing has grown to 40° from wear. Calculate the fractional time loss per day and suggest a fix.
   **Worked Answer:** At 40° (θ = 0.698 rad): θ²/16 = 0.488/16 = 0.0305 → 3.05% slow. In 24 h, the clock loses 0.0305 × 86400 s ≈ 2635 s ≈ 44 min/day. Fix: reduce the swing amplitude back to under 10° by adjusting the escapement, or shorten the pendulum slightly to compensate (but the amplitude will keep drifting). The correct fix is to restore small-amplitude operation.

## Safety Notes
- Large swings (60°) can hit desks or learners. Clear the swing area.
- A heavy bob released at 60° has more energy — ensure the stand is stable and the string is strong.
- Do not stand in the swing path.
