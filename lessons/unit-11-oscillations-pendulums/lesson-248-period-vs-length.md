# Lesson 248: What Affects the Period? Test: Length — The One That Matters

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Set up a fair test varying only the string length while keeping mass and amplitude fixed.
2. Measure periods at several lengths from 20 to 100 cm and record a class dataset.
3. Discover that longer pendulums have longer periods and that T grows with √L.
4. Use the √L relationship to predict a period at a new length.

## Materials
- Pendulum kit: string (~1.2 m), bob (stone or keys), stand
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Tape measure or metre stick
- Protractor card (to keep amplitude fixed at ~10°)
- Notebook and pencil
- Class data table on the board (length | period, one row per pair)

## Vocabulary
- **length (L)** (urefu) — distance from pivot to centre of bob, in metres or cm
- **square root** (sqrt) — the number that multiplied by itself gives the original
- **proportional** (sambamba) — when one quantity changes in step with another
- **class dataset** (data ya darasa) — results pooled from all pairs for better coverage

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you make a pendulum string longer, will the period increase, decrease, or stay the same?
2. What three things must you keep the same when testing the effect of length?
3. A 100 cm pendulum has a period of 2.0 s. Predict the period at 25 cm (hint: it depends on √L).

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Increase — longer pendulums swing slower (longer period).
2. Bob mass, release angle (amplitude), and pivot position. Only the length should change.
3. 25 cm is 1/4 of 100 cm. √(1/4) = 1/2. So the period halves: 2.0 × 0.5 = 1.0 s.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show two pendulums side by side: one at 100 cm, one at 25 cm. Release both at the same time and same angle. The short one races ahead. Ask: *how many short swings for one long swing?* (Answer: about 2). Board the question: is the relationship linear (double length = double period) or something else?
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs are assigned lengths: 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100 cm. Each pair measures the period at their assigned length using the 10-swing method (three trials), then writes their result on the class table on the board. The class now has a 10-row dataset: length | period. Each pair copies the full table.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The period clearly increases with length, but not linearly. Doubling the length (50 → 100 cm) gives about 1.4× the period, not 2×. That factor is √2 ≈ 1.41. The relationship is T ∝ √L — period is proportional to the square root of length. This is the key result. Confront the misconception that "double the length = double the time" — show the data: it is √2, not 2. This dataset feeds Lesson 249, where we plot T² against L and get a straight line.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *length is the one that matters*. Homework: using T ∝ √L, predict the period at 36 cm if the period at 100 cm is 2.0 s. (Answer: √(36/100) = 0.6 → 2.0 × 0.6 = 1.2 s.)

## Hands-On Build: Length Fair Test
*Test whether string length changes the period, keeping mass and amplitude fixed.*

### Parts
- String — ~1.2 m, adjustable
- Bob — same stone or keys for all pairs
- Stand — same pivot
- Tape measure or metre stick — to set length from pivot to centre of bob
- Stopwatch — phone
- Protractor card — to keep release angle at ~10° for all trials

### Workflow
1. Set your assigned length (e.g., 50 cm) by measuring from the pivot to the centre of the bob. **Checkpoint:** measure to the bob's centre, not to the top of the bob.
2. Pull to 10° using the protractor card and release.
3. Time 10 swings, three trials. Record each total and divide by 10 for the period.
4. Average the three periods. Write your result on the class table: length | period.
5. Copy the full class table into your notebook for Lesson 249.
6. If time allows, your teacher may assign you a second length to measure.

### Common Mistakes
- **Measuring to the top of the bob** — always measure from pivot to the centre (middle) of the bob. The bob's own size is part of the length.
- **Changing the bob** — keep the same bob for all lengths. Changing mass (Lesson 246 showed it doesn't matter, but keeping it fixed is good fair-test discipline).
- **Large amplitude** — keep at 10° so the small-angle approximation holds (Lesson 247).
- **String not vertical at rest** — check the resting position is straight down before measuring length.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Does increasing the string length increase or decrease the period? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What must you keep fixed in this experiment? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A 100 cm pendulum has T = 2.0 s. Predict T at 25 cm using T ∝ √L. (mirrors pre-test)
4. When the length doubles from 50 cm to 100 cm, the period increases by a factor of √2 ≈ 1.41, not 2. Explain what this tells you about the relationship.
5. Challenge: predict the period at 64 cm if T at 100 cm is 2.0 s. Show your working.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Increase — longer pendulums have longer periods.
2. Bob mass and release angle (amplitude). Only length changes.
3. √(25/100) = √0.25 = 0.5. T = 2.0 × 0.5 = 1.0 s.
4. The relationship is not linear (T ∝ L). It is T ∝ √L — the period grows with the square root of length, not with length itself. Doubling L multiplies T by √2, not 2.
5. √(64/100) = √0.64 = 0.8. T = 2.0 × 0.8 = 1.6 s.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Predict the period at 25 cm given T = 1.0 s at 100 cm, using T ∝ √L. Verify with the class data.
   **Worked Answer:** T ∝ √L. Ratio: √(25/100) = √0.25 = 0.5. T(25 cm) = 1.0 × 0.5 = 0.50 s. (Note: if T at 100 cm is 2.0 s instead, then T at 25 cm = 2.0 × 0.5 = 1.0 s.) Check against the class table — should be within ±0.02 s.
2. **Q:** The formula is T = 2π√(L/g). Rearrange to solve for L given T = 1.0 s and g = 9.8 m/s².
   **Worked Answer:** T = 2π√(L/g) → √(L/g) = T/(2π) → L/g = [T/(2π)]² → L = g[T/(2π)]². With T = 1.0 s, g = 9.8: L = 9.8 × [1.0/(6.283)]² = 9.8 × [0.1592]² = 9.8 × 0.02535 = 0.248 m ≈ 25 cm. So a 1-second pendulum needs about 25 cm.
3. **Q:** Your class data gives T = 1.50 s at L = 56 cm and T = 2.00 s at L = 100 cm. Check whether these are consistent with T ∝ √L.
   **Worked Answer:** Ratio of periods: 2.00/1.50 = 1.333. Ratio of √L: √(100/56) = √1.786 = 1.336. These match (1.333 ≈ 1.336), confirming T ∝ √L within experimental error.

## Safety Notes
- Long pendulums (100 cm) can swing into desks or people — clear the area.
- Ensure the stand is stable at all lengths; a long pendulum exerts more torque on the pivot.
- Use a non-stretchy string; elastic string changes length under load.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 246–247 showed mass and amplitude (at small angles) do not affect the period. This lesson reveals that length does — it is the variable that matters.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the fair test and the qualitative result (longer = slower). Ages 14–17 should use T ∝ √L for predictions and verify against the class dataset.
- Local resource tips: a tape measure or metre stick is essential. If unavailable, mark lengths on the string with a pen or tape in advance.
