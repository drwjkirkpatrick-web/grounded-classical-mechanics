# Lesson 250: Measuring g Precisely: Pendulum vs Drop

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Compare the pendulum method and the drop method for measuring g side by side.
2. Identify sources of error in each method and rank them by size.
3. Judge which method is more accurate and explain why using error analysis.
4. Write a simple error budget for both methods.

## Materials
- Pendulum kit: string (~1 m), bob, stand, stopwatch
- Drop kit: a stone or ball, a tape measure, a stopwatch (phone)
- Class results from Lesson 249 (pendulum g values)
- Notebook and pencil
- Calculator (phone)

## Vocabulary
- **error budget** (bajeti ya hitilafu) — a list of each error source and its estimated size
- **systematic error** (hitilafu ya mfumo) — an error that biases results in one direction
- **random error** (hitilafu ya bahati) — an error that varies unpredictably, reduced by averaging
- **repeatability** (kurudia) — how close repeated measurements are to each other

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Name the two methods we have used to measure g in this course.
2. Which method do you think is more accurate, and why?
3. In the drop method, a ball falls 1.0 m. How long does the fall take (use g = 10 m/s²)?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The drop method (Unit 4) and the pendulum method (Lesson 249).
2. The pendulum method — because you time many swings (total ~15–20 s) so reaction-time error is a small percentage. The drop is over in ~0.45 s, so reaction time is a large fraction.
3. t = √(2h/g) = √(2 × 1.0/10) = √0.2 = 0.447 s ≈ 0.45 s.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — recap the two g values from the class: pendulum g (from Lesson 249) and drop g (from Unit 4, if recorded; otherwise estimate from memory). Write both on the board. Ask: *why are they different? Which one do we trust more?* Board the question.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs run both methods side by side (see Hands-On Build). For the pendulum: one length (1.0 m), 10-swing timing, three trials. For the drop: drop a stone from 1.0 m, time the fall, three trials. Compute g from each: pendulum g = 4π²L/T²; drop g = 2h/t². Record both on the class table.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The pendulum g should be closer to 9.8 with less scatter. The drop g will have more scatter (typically ±1–2 m/s²) because the fall time is ~0.45 s and reaction time is ~0.2 s — a 40% error. The pendulum's total time is ~20 s for 10 swings at 1 m, so reaction time is ~1%. Build the error budget on the board: for each method, list the error sources and their approximate sizes. Confront the misconception that "both methods measure g, so they are equally good" — the precision depends on the ratio of error to measured quantity.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *precision is about the ratio of error to signal*. Homework: write a one-paragraph recommendation: which method should be the school standard for measuring g, and why.

## Hands-On Build: Pendulum vs Drop Comparison
*Run both methods side by side and compare the precision.*

### Parts
- Pendulum kit: string (1.0 m), bob, stand, stopwatch
- Drop kit: a stone or ball, tape measure (or metre stick), stopwatch
- A wall or post to mark 1.0 m for the drop

### Workflow
1. **Pendulum:** set L = 1.0 m (pivot to centre of bob). Pull to 10°, release. Time 10 swings, three trials. Average → T. **Checkpoint:** the three trials should agree within ±0.2 s.
2. Compute g_pendulum = 4π²L/T² = 39.48 × 1.0 / T².
3. **Drop:** mark 1.0 m on a wall. Hold the stone at the mark. Drop (release without pushing) while a partner starts the stopwatch on release and stops on impact. Three trials. **Checkpoint:** release with an open hand — do not push down.
4. Compute g_drop = 2h/t² = 2 × 1.0 / t².
5. Record both g values on the class table. Compare scatter (range of values across trials and across the class).

### Common Mistakes
- **Pushing the stone down** — adds initial speed; the stone arrives too fast, inflating g. Open the hand cleanly.
- **Starting the stopwatch late** — in the drop method, the reaction time on start and stop is the dominant error. Accept it; that is the point of the comparison.
- **Measuring L to the top of the bob** — measure to the centre; the bob's size adds to the effective length.
- **Different lengths for pendulum and drop** — use 1.0 m for both to make the comparison fair.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Name the two methods for measuring g. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Which method is more precise, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Your drop times for 1.0 m are: 0.42, 0.48, 0.45 s. Compute g for each and the range.
4. Your pendulum 10-swing time is 20.0 s at L = 1.0 m. Compute g.
5. Challenge: write an error budget for both methods, listing at least two error sources each and estimating their size.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The drop method (measuring fall time from a known height) and the pendulum method (timing swings at a known length).
2. The pendulum method — the total measured time (10 swings ≈ 20 s) is much longer than the reaction-time error (~0.2 s), giving ~1% uncertainty. The drop time (~0.45 s) is comparable to reaction time, giving ~40% uncertainty.
3. g = 2h/t². For t = 0.42: g = 2/0.1764 = 11.3 m/s². For t = 0.48: g = 2/0.2304 = 8.7 m/s². For t = 0.45: g = 2/0.2025 = 9.9 m/s². Range: 8.7 to 11.3 — a spread of 2.6 m/s².
4. T = 20.0/10 = 2.0 s. g = 4π² × 1.0/2.0² = 39.48/4.0 = 9.87 m/s².
5. Drop method: (a) reaction time ~0.2 s out of 0.45 s ≈ 40%; (b) height measurement error ~1 cm out of 1 m ≈ 1%. Pendulum method: (a) reaction time ~0.2 s out of 20 s ≈ 1%; (b) length measurement error ~5 mm out of 1 m ≈ 0.5%; (c) amplitude effect at 10° ~0.5%.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Write an error budget for both methods; recommend the school-standard procedure.
   **Worked Answer:** Drop method: reaction time ±0.2 s / 0.45 s = ±44%; height ±0.01 m / 1.0 m = ±1%. Total: dominated by reaction time → ~44% uncertainty. Pendulum method: reaction time ±0.2 s / 20 s = ±1%; length ±0.005 m / 1.0 m = ±0.5%; amplitude (10°) correction ~+0.8%; string stiffness ~0.3%. Total: ~±1.5%. Recommendation: use the pendulum method as the school standard; aim for small amplitude (<10°), 20-swing timing, and precise length measurement.
2. **Q:** The drop method gives g = 8.7, 9.9, 11.3 m/s² (three trials). The pendulum gives 9.8, 9.9, 9.8 m/s². Compare precision and accuracy, and explain the difference.
   **Worked Answer:** Pendulum: mean 9.83, spread ±0.05 — precise and accurate. Drop: mean 9.97, spread ±1.3 — less precise (wide scatter) but the mean is roughly accurate (close to 9.8). The drop method is less precise because the reaction time dominates the short fall. Averaging many drop trials can improve the mean accuracy, but the pendulum is inherently more repeatable.
3. **Q:** Design an improved drop method that reduces the reaction-time problem. What apparatus would you need?
   **Worked Answer:** Use electronic timing: place a light gate at the release point and another at the floor. The falling object breaks the first beam (start) and the second (stop), timing the fall automatically with millisecond precision. Alternatively, use video: record the drop with a phone at 30 fps or 60 fps, then count frames between release and impact (precision ~1/30 s). Both methods reduce timing error from ~0.2 s to <0.01 s, making the drop method competitive with the pendulum.

## Safety Notes
- Drop the stone onto a soft surface (grass, a mat, or a cushion) to avoid damaging the floor or bouncing dangerously.
- Do not stand under the drop area.
- Ensure the pendulum stand is stable.

- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on running both methods and seeing the scatter. Ages 14–17 should build error budgets and discuss precision vs accuracy.
