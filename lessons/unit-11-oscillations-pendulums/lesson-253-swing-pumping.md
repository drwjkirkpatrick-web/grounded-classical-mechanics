# Lesson 253: How to Pump a Swing Higher

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe the standing-squatting technique for pumping a playground swing.
2. Explain how timing the squat pumps energy into the oscillation.
3. Identify that pumping changes the effective length twice per cycle (parametric pumping).
4. Relate the added energy to the work done by the rider against gravity.

## Materials
- A playground swing (or a rope hung from a sturdy branch for the demo)
- A stopwatch (phone) — optional, for timing the swing period
- Notebook and pencil
- A safe outdoor area

## Vocabulary
- **pumping** (kusukuma swingi) — the technique of adding energy to a swing by moving your body
- **parametric pumping** (parametric) — changing the effective length of the pendulum at the right moments to add energy
- **effective length** (urefu wa kazi) — the distance from pivot to the rider's centre of mass, which changes when they squat or stand
- **timing** (muda sahihi) — doing the squat or stand at the right moment in the cycle

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. When you swing, do you go higher if you just sit still or if you move your body?
2. At what point in the swing should you stand up (raise your centre of mass) to go higher?
3. Where does the extra energy come from when you pump a swing?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Moving your body — pumping adds energy; sitting still, the swing decays (damping).
2. Near the bottom (the fastest point). Standing up at the bottom raises your centre of mass when the speed is highest, adding the most energy.
3. From your muscles — you do work against gravity when you stand up (raising your centre of mass). That chemical energy (food → muscle) becomes mechanical energy of the swing.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. This lesson is best done outdoors or where a swing is accessible.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate (or describe) the standing-squatting technique on a swing. The rider stands on the seat, squats at the extremes, and rises at the bottom. Ask: *why does this make the swing go higher?* Board the word **pumping**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs observe and try the technique (see Hands-On Build). One partner swings while the other watches the timing: when do they rise? When do they squat? The observer notes the amplitude growth over 10 cycles. Then they swap. If no swing is available, pairs do a "desk pendulum" version: a bob on a string where they shorten the string at the bottom and lengthen at the extremes (parametric pumping demo). They measure the amplitude before and after 10 pumped cycles.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Pumping a swing is **parametric pumping** — changing the effective pendulum length (by raising or lowering the centre of mass) at the right time. The rider squats at the extremes (low energy, centre of mass low) and stands at the bottom (high speed, centre of mass high). Standing at the bottom raises the centre of mass against gravity at the moment of maximum kinetic energy — this does work (W = mgΔh) that adds to the swing's energy. Squatting at the extremes lowers the centre of mass when the speed is near zero, so the energy cost is minimal. The net effect: energy is added twice per cycle (once per stand-at-bottom), and the amplitude grows. Confront the misconception that "pumping works because you push the air" — the energy comes from the rider's muscles doing work against gravity, not from air resistance. The timing is what makes it efficient: rising at the fast point (bottom) maximises the energy input; squatting at the slow point (extreme) minimises the energy cost.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *pump at the bottom, squat at the top*. Homework: try the technique on a real swing and describe what happens to the amplitude over 10 cycles.

## Hands-On Build: Pumping a Swing
*Practise the standing-squatting technique and observe amplitude growth.*

### Parts
- A playground swing, or a rope hung from a sturdy branch
- A stopwatch (phone) — optional
- A safe outdoor area with clear space around the swing

### Workflow
1. Sit or stand on the swing. Start with a small amplitude (gentle push). **Checkpoint:** the swing moves freely without hitting anything.
2. At the bottom of each swing (the fastest point), stand up or extend your legs to raise your centre of mass. At the extremes (the slowest points), squat or bend your knees to lower your centre of mass. **Checkpoint:** the rhythm is stand-at-bottom, squat-at-top, twice per full cycle.
3. After 10 pumped cycles, note the amplitude — how high do you swing compared to the start?
4. Your partner watches and calls out the timing: "stand" at the bottom, "squat" at the top. **Checkpoint:** if the timing is right, the amplitude should grow steadily over 10 cycles.
5. Try mistimed pumping (stand at the top instead of the bottom). Observe: the amplitude should not grow — it may even shrink.
6. If no swing is available, do the desk version: a bob on a string. Shorten the string by 2 cm at the bottom (pull the string up) and release it (lengthen by 2 cm) at the extremes. The amplitude grows.

### Common Mistakes
- **Standing at the wrong time** — if you stand at the extremes (where speed is zero) instead of the bottom, you do work against gravity but don't add kinetic energy. The effect is minimal.
- **Squatting at the bottom** — lowering your centre of mass at the fastest point removes energy. Always squat at the extremes.
- **Too much motion** — violent pumping can destabilise the swing. Smooth, rhythmic motion is more efficient.
- **Not starting with a small amplitude** — pumping from a dead start is very difficult. Start with a gentle push.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Do you go higher by sitting still or by moving your body? (mirrors pre-test)
2. At what point should you stand up to add the most energy? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Where does the energy come from? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Explain why squatting at the extremes (not the bottom) is more efficient.
5. Challenge: if you pump mistimed (stand at the top), what happens? Why?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Moving your body — pumping adds energy; sitting still, the swing decays due to damping (air resistance, friction).
2. At the bottom — the fastest point. Standing raises the centre of mass against gravity when kinetic energy is maximum, adding the most mechanical energy to the swing.
3. From the rider's muscles. Chemical energy from food is converted by muscles to mechanical work (W = mgΔh) against gravity. That work becomes the swing's kinetic and potential energy.
4. At the extremes, the speed is near zero. Lowering the centre of mass there costs minimal energy because there is no kinetic energy to lose. At the bottom, the speed is maximum, so lowering the centre of mass would remove energy (the centripetal force is highest, and squatting reduces the effective radius, which would reduce the speed). By squatting at the extremes, the energy cost is minimised.
5. The amplitude does not grow (and may shrink). Standing at the top raises the centre of mass where the speed is zero — the work done (mgΔh) adds potential energy, but there is no kinetic energy to amplify. The timing mismatch means some pumps add energy and others remove it, so the net effect is near zero or negative.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Explain pumping as changing the effective length twice per cycle. Where does the added energy come from?
   **Worked Answer:** The rider's centre of mass acts as the effective bob. Standing at the bottom raises the centre of mass, shortening the effective pendulum length (L_eff decreases). Shortening L at the bottom increases the speed (conservation of angular momentum: L_eff × v = constant, so shorter L → faster v). The rider does work (mgΔh) to raise the centre of mass against gravity. This work adds to the swing's kinetic energy. At the extremes, squatting lowers the centre of mass (lengthening L_eff) when v ≈ 0, so the energy cost is minimal (no kinetic energy to lose). The net energy gain per cycle is 2mgΔh (one stand per half-cycle, two per full cycle), where Δh is the height change of the centre of mass.
2. **Q:** A rider raises their centre of mass by 5 cm at the bottom. How much energy is added per cycle? (rider mass = 40 kg, g = 9.8)
   **Worked Answer:** Energy added per stand = mgΔh = 40 × 9.8 × 0.05 = 19.6 J. Two stands per cycle (one per bottom crossing): 2 × 19.6 = 39.2 J per cycle. This is a significant energy input — a few cycles of pumping can build the amplitude from near-zero to full swing.
3. **Q:** If the swing has natural frequency f = 0.5 Hz and the rider pumps at f = 0.5 Hz (once per cycle), why doesn't this look like the resonance of Lesson 254?
   **Worked Answer:** It is a form of resonance, but the mechanism is different. In Lesson 254's resonance, an external driving force at the natural frequency adds energy (direct forcing). Here, the rider changes a *parameter* of the system (the effective length) at twice the natural frequency (standing at each bottom crossing = 2 per cycle = 2f = 1 Hz). This is **parametric resonance** — driving a parameter rather than applying a force. The energy source is the rider's internal work, not an external push. Parametric pumping is more efficient than direct forcing for swings because the rider can time the parameter change to maximise energy input at the high-speed point.

## Safety Notes
- Playground swings must be inspected for structural integrity before use.
- Learners must hold on securely; falling from a swing at height is dangerous.
- Clear the area around the swing — do not walk in front of or behind a swinging rider.
- The desk-pendulum version is a safe alternative if no swing is available.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 243–250 covered pendulum period, energy, and measurement. This lesson applies oscillation physics to a playground experience every learner knows.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the technique and the idea that timing matters. Ages 14–17 should explain parametric pumping, compute the energy input, and distinguish it from direct resonance.
- Local resource tips: a playground swing or a rope on a tree branch. The desk version (shortening and lengthening the string at the right moments) works indoors if no swing is available.
- This lesson bridges everyday experience and physics. Every learner has pumped a swing — now they know why it works. The connection to parametric resonance is a powerful insight that reappears in engineering (bridge cables, electrical circuits).
