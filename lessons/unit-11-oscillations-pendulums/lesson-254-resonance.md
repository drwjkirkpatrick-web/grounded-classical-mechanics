# Lesson 254: Resonance: Small Pushes, Big Swings

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate resonance with two coupled pendulums on a shared string.
2. Explain that maximum energy transfer occurs when natural frequencies match.
3. Describe real-world resonance stories: shaky bridges, wine glasses, boda-boda bouncing on corrugations.
4. Identify damping as the cure for unwanted resonance.

## Materials
- Two pendulums on a shared horizontal string (tied between two stands or chairs)
- Pendulum bobs: two stones or keys, on strings of different lengths (~50 cm and ~80 cm)
- A third matching pendulum (same length as one of the pair) — the "driver"
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **resonance** (mvuto wa sauti) — large-amplitude response when driving frequency matches natural frequency
- **natural frequency** (mara kwa sekunde asili) — the frequency at which a system oscillates on its own
- **coupled pendulums** (pendulum zinazoshikamana) — two pendulums connected through a shared support that transfers energy
- **detuning** (kutenganisha) — making the frequencies different so resonance does not occur
- **damping** (upungufu wa nguvu) — energy loss that limits the resonant amplitude

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you push a swing at the wrong rhythm, what happens to the amplitude?
2. If you push at exactly the right rhythm, what happens?
3. Name one real structure that was damaged or destroyed by resonance.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The amplitude stays small — mistimed pushes cancel out (some add energy, some remove it).
2. The amplitude grows — each push adds energy in phase with the motion. This is resonance.
3. Accept any: Angers Bridge (France, 1850, soldiers marching), Tacoma Narrows Bridge (USA, 1940, wind), or a boda-boda shaking on corrugated road.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — tell the story of the Angers Bridge: in 1850, a battalion of French soldiers marched across a suspension bridge in step. The bridge resonated, oscillated wildly, and collapsed, killing over 200 soldiers. Ask: *why did marching in step destroy a bridge that could carry the same soldiers one by one?* Board: **resonance** — small pushes, big swings, when the rhythm matches.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs set up coupled pendulums on a shared string (see Hands-On Build). They hang two pendulums of different lengths from the same horizontal string. They first time each pendulum's natural period. Then they drive one (the driver) and watch the other (the responder). When the driver's frequency matches the responder's natural frequency, the responder's amplitude grows. When they detune (different lengths), the responder stays small. Pairs record: driver frequency | responder amplitude (small/medium/large).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Resonance: when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency, energy transfers efficiently and amplitude builds up. In coupled pendulums, the shared string carries small pushes from the driver to the responder. When their natural frequencies match, the pushes arrive at the right time each cycle — energy accumulates. When they don't match (detuned), the pushes arrive out of phase and cancel. Real-world stories: (a) bridges — wind or marching soldiers at the bridge's natural frequency; (b) wine glasses — a singer at the glass's natural frequency can shatter it (the amplitude grows until the glass cracks); (c) boda-boda on corrugated road — the bumps at a certain speed match the bike's natural frequency, causing violent shaking. The cure for unwanted resonance: **damping** — adding energy loss (shock absorbers, stiffening, detuning by changing the natural frequency). Confront the misconception that "resonance is always destructive" — it is also used constructively: musical instruments (amplifying sound), radios (tuning to a station), MRI machines.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *resonance is rhythm matching — useful or dangerous*. Homework: find one resonance story from Kenya or elsewhere and describe it in two sentences.

## Hands-On Build: Coupled Pendulum Resonance
*Two pendulums on a shared string to demonstrate energy transfer by resonance.*

### Parts
- A horizontal string tied between two stands, chairs, or clamped sticks (the shared support)
- Two pendulum bobs on strings — one ~50 cm, one ~80 cm
- A third bob on a string matching one of the pair (e.g., 50 cm) — the driver
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Tape or clips to attach the pendulums to the shared string

### Workflow
1. Tie the horizontal string between two stands, ~1 m apart. It should be taut but slightly flexible — it must transfer small movements. **Checkpoint:** pluck the string; it should vibrate slightly.
2. Hang two pendulums from the shared string, 20 cm apart. One at 50 cm, one at 80 cm. **Checkpoint:** both hang freely without touching.
3. Time each pendulum's period (10 swings). Compute f = 1/T for each. **Checkpoint:** the 50 cm pendulum has a higher frequency than the 80 cm one.
4. Leave both hanging. Pull the 50 cm pendulum (the driver) to 10° and release. Watch the 80 cm pendulum (the responder). **Checkpoint:** the responder should barely move — the frequencies don't match (detuned).
5. Now hang a third pendulum at 50 cm (matching the first). Pull the driver and release. Watch the matching responder. **Checkpoint:** the matching responder's amplitude should grow over 30–60 seconds — resonance!
6. Record: driver frequency | responder length | responder amplitude (small/medium/large).

### Common Mistakes
- **Support too rigid** — a rigid metal bar won't transfer energy. Use a string or a flexible stick.
- **Pendulums too far apart** — keep them 15–20 cm apart on the support for strong coupling.
- **Not watching long enough** — resonance builds slowly. Watch for at least 30–60 seconds.
- **Pendulums touching** — if they swing into each other, the contact transfers energy directly, not through resonance. Space them apart.
- **Different lengths but similar frequencies** — if the lengths are too close, both pendulums respond partially. Use clearly different lengths (50 cm vs 80 cm) for the detuned case.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What happens to amplitude when you push at the wrong frequency? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What happens when you push at the right frequency? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name one real structure damaged by resonance. (mirrors pre-test)
4. Two pendulums (50 cm and 80 cm) are coupled. You drive the 50 cm one. Does the 80 cm one resonate? Why or why not?
5. Challenge: a boda-boda shakes violently at one particular speed on a corrugated road but is smooth at other speeds. Explain using resonance. How could the rider stop the shaking?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The amplitude stays small. Mistimed pushes add and remove energy alternately, so the net energy input is near zero.
2. The amplitude grows — each push adds energy in phase with the motion. This is resonance.
3. Accept any: Angers Bridge (1850), Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940), or any other valid example.
4. No — the 80 cm pendulum has a different natural frequency from the 50 cm driver. The frequencies don't match, so energy transfer is inefficient and the responder stays small (detuned).
5. At that speed, the bump frequency (speed ÷ bump spacing) matches the bike's natural frequency → resonance → violent shaking. At other speeds, the frequency doesn't match → no resonance. The rider can stop the shaking by changing speed (detuning — moving away from the resonant frequency), tightening the suspension (shifting the natural frequency), or adding damping (shock absorbers that absorb energy).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Two coupled pendulums: explain energy transfer when lengths match, and detuning when they don't.
   **Worked Answer:** When lengths match, the driver's frequency equals the responder's natural frequency. Each cycle, the shared support transfers a small push in phase with the responder's motion — energy accumulates and amplitude grows (resonance). The energy passes back and forth: first the driver gives energy to the responder, then the responder returns it (the driver's amplitude decreases as the responder's grows). This is the "beat" pattern. When lengths don't match (detuned), the push arrives at a different phase each cycle — sometimes helping, sometimes fighting the motion. The net energy transfer averages to near zero, and the responder stays small. The larger the detuning, the less energy transfers.
2. **Q:** A wine glass has a natural frequency of 800 Hz. A singer hits 800 Hz at 120 dB. Estimate the amplitude growth and explain why the glass cracks.
   **Worked Answer:** At resonance, the sound pressure drives the glass at its natural frequency. The amplitude grows until the energy input (from sound) equals the energy lost (damping from internal friction and air). At 120 dB (high intensity), the energy input is large. The glass strain (deformation) at the rim grows to the point where the tensile stress exceeds the glass's breaking strength (~50 MPa for ordinary glass). The amplitude needed is small (micrometres) because the stress concentrates at imperfections. The glass cracks when the stress exceeds the failure threshold — a few cycles of high-amplitude oscillation are enough.
3. **Q:** A bridge has a natural frequency of 0.8 Hz. Soldiers march at 1.6 steps/s. Will resonance occur? What if they march at 0.8 Hz?
   **Worked Answer:** At 1.6 Hz: no resonance — the driving frequency (1.6 Hz) does not match the natural frequency (0.8 Hz). At 0.8 Hz: yes, resonance — the marching frequency matches the bridge's natural frequency. Each step adds energy in phase with the bridge's oscillation, and the amplitude builds. This is why soldiers are ordered to "break step" when crossing bridges — random stepping ensures no single frequency dominates, preventing resonance.

## Safety Notes
- Do not over-amplify coupled pendulums — the responder can swing surprisingly wide. Stop before it hits anything.
- Secure the shared support; a taut string between two stands is sufficient.
- Do not attempt the wine-glass demo in class — it requires high-intensity sound and glass shards are dangerous.
- Keep the swing area clear.

- Prior knowledge: Lessons 243–250 covered pendulum period, length dependence, and measurement. Lesson 253 covered pumping (adding energy by body movement). This lesson introduces resonance — adding energy by an external periodic force at the natural frequency.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the demo and the idea that matching frequencies cause big swings. Ages 14–17 should explain energy transfer, detuning, and real engineering applications.
