# Lesson 255: Damping: The Shock Absorber's Job

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Observe and measure how oscillation amplitude decays over time due to damping.
2. Test a mass on a rubber strip with and without a card damper and compare decay rates.
3. Explain the role of a shock absorber on a boda-boda or bicycle.
4. Relate damping to energy: each cycle loses energy to friction, reducing amplitude.

## Materials
- A rubber strip (~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long) cut from an inner tube
- A mass: a stone or metal object (~100 g)
- A stand or hook to hang the rubber strip
- A card damper: a piece of cardboard (~10×15 cm) taped to the mass to increase air resistance
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Ruler — to measure amplitude
- Notebook and pencil
- A bicycle or boda-boda for observation (if available)

## Vocabulary
- **damping** (upungufu wa nguvu) — energy loss from an oscillating system, causing amplitude to shrink
- **shock absorber** (kipooza) — a device that damps oscillations in vehicle suspension
- **air resistance** (upinzani wa hewa) — friction with air that removes energy each swing
- **amplitude decay** (upungufu wa ukiwa) — the shrinking of the swing size over time
- **cycles to settle** (mizunguko hadi kufifia) — how many oscillations before the system effectively stops

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you set a mass bouncing on a rubber strip and leave it alone, what happens to the amplitude?
2. Where does the oscillation energy go?
3. Why does a boda-boda have shock absorbers on the rear wheel?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The amplitude decreases — the bounce gets smaller and eventually stops. This is damping.
2. Energy is converted to heat — by air resistance (friction with air) and internal friction in the rubber. The energy is not destroyed; it is dissipated as thermal energy.
3. To damp the oscillation after a bump. Without shock absorbers, the bike would bounce many times after each bump — uncomfortable and dangerous (wheel loses contact with the road). The shock absorber kills the bounce in 1–2 cycles.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate: hang a mass on a rubber strip, pull down, release. It bounces many times before stopping. Ask: *how many bounces? Where does the energy go?* Then ask: *what if we add a card damper — a piece of cardboard that catches the air?* Board: **damping** — the shock absorber's job.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs run the damping comparison (see Hands-On Build). They set the mass bouncing on the rubber strip and count the number of cycles to settle (amplitude below ~1 cm) — both without and with the card damper. They also measure the amplitude every 5 cycles for 20 cycles to see the decay pattern. Results go on a class table: condition | cycles to settle | amplitude at cycle 5/10/15/20.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Damping is the gradual loss of oscillation energy to friction (air resistance, internal friction in the rubber, pivot friction). The amplitude decreases by roughly the same fraction each cycle (exponential decay). More damping = fewer cycles to settle. A shock absorber on a boda-boda or bicycle works the same way: it adds damping (usually a piston in oil) so that after a bump, the suspension compresses and then returns to equilibrium in 1–2 cycles, instead of bouncing many times. Confront the misconception that "shock absorbers absorb shocks" — they actually *damp the oscillation after* the shock. The spring absorbs the shock (stores energy); the damper dissipates it (converts to heat). A car with springs but no dampers would bounce endlessly; a car with dampers but no springs would be harsh. Both are needed. Also address the bounce-test comparison: a freshly serviced suspension settles in ~1.5 cycles; a worn one takes ~4 cycles — the worn damper has less damping, so the energy lasts longer.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *damping turns motion into heat — that's the shock absorber's job*. Homework: push down the corner of a bicycle or boda-boda and count how many bounces it takes to settle. Write down the number.

## Hands-On Build: Damping Comparison
*Compare oscillation decay with and without a card damper on a rubber-strip oscillator.*

### Parts
- Rubber strip — ~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long, from an inner tube
- Mass — a stone or metal object, ~100 g
- Stand or hook — to hang the rubber strip
- Card damper — a piece of cardboard ~10×15 cm
- Tape — to attach the card to the mass
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Ruler — to measure amplitude (cm)

### Workflow
1. Hang the rubber strip from the stand. Attach the mass. Let it settle to equilibrium. **Checkpoint:** the mass hangs still; note the equilibrium position.
2. Pull the mass down 5 cm and release. Count the number of cycles (up-and-down = 1 cycle) until the amplitude is below 1 cm. **Checkpoint:** count out loud — "1, 2, 3 …" until the motion is barely visible.
3. Record: cycles to settle (no damper).
4. Now tape the card damper to the mass so it catches air on the up-and-down motion. **Checkpoint:** the card should be horizontal (flat) so it catches air as the mass moves.
5. Pull down 5 cm and release. Count cycles to settle. **Checkpoint:** the card should cause the oscillation to die much faster — typically 2–4 cycles instead of 10–20.
6. Repeat both conditions and record amplitude every 5 cycles (pull 5 cm, measure amplitude after cycle 5, 10, 15, 20). Compare the decay with and without the damper.

### Common Mistakes
- **Card not catching air** — if the card is vertical (edge-on to the motion), it doesn't add air resistance. Tape it flat (horizontal).
- **Rubber strip too stiff or too soft** — adjust the width. A 2 cm strip with a 100 g mass should stretch 3–8 cm at equilibrium.
- **Overstretching** — pulling more than 8 cm can permanently deform the rubber. Pull 5 cm.
- **Sideways motion** — if the mass swings sideways as a pendulum, the count is contaminated. Keep the motion vertical; guide the mass straight down.
- **Counting subjectively** — define "settled" as amplitude < 1 cm (barely visible). Use the ruler to check.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What happens to the amplitude of a bouncing mass over time? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Where does the energy go? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why does a boda-boda have shock absorbers? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your mass bounces 15 cycles without the card damper and 3 cycles with it. What does the card do?
5. Challenge: a boda-boda corner settles in 1.5 cycles after shock service, but 4 cycles before. Explain what changed energetically.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The amplitude decreases steadily — the bounce gets smaller and eventually stops. This is damping.
2. Energy is converted to heat by air resistance and internal friction in the rubber (or the shock absorber's oil). Total energy is conserved; mechanical energy decreases.
3. To damp the oscillation after a bump. Without dampers, the bike bounces many times — uncomfortable and dangerous (wheel loses contact with the road). The damper kills the bounce in 1–2 cycles.
4. The card adds air resistance, increasing the damping. More energy is lost per cycle, so the oscillation dies faster (3 cycles vs 15). The card acts like a shock absorber.
5. The shock service restored the damper's ability to dissipate energy. Before: the worn damper removed little energy per cycle (4 cycles to settle, more energy remaining each cycle). After: the serviced damper removes more energy per cycle (1.5 cycles to settle, energy dissipated quickly). The damping coefficient increased — the same initial energy is dissipated in fewer cycles.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A bounce-test corner settles in 1.5 vs 4 cycles after shock service. Explain what changed energetically.
   **Worked Answer:** The number of cycles to settle is inversely related to the damping coefficient. Before service: 4 cycles — the worn damper removes ~25% of the remaining energy per cycle (E_n = E₀ × 0.75^n; after 4 cycles: 0.75⁴ = 0.316, so ~68% of energy dissipated). After service: 1.5 cycles — the serviced damper removes ~50% per cycle (0.5^1.5 = 0.354, so ~65% dissipated in 1.5 cycles). The key difference: the serviced damper has a higher damping coefficient (twice the energy removal rate), so the oscillation dies in half the cycles. The spring is unchanged; only the damping changed. The shock absorber was rebuilt or replaced — its oil viscosity or piston seal was restored.
2. **Q:** The amplitude halves every 3 cycles. Calculate the fraction of energy lost per cycle.
   **Worked Answer:** Amplitude halves every 3 cycles: A₃ = A₀/2. Since E ∝ A² (for SHM, E = ½kA²): E₃ = E₀/4. So in 3 cycles, 75% of energy is lost, 25% remains. Per cycle: E_n = E₀ × r³, where r is the fraction remaining per cycle. r³ = 0.25 → r = 0.25^(1/3) = 0.630. So 37% of energy is lost per cycle (63% remains).
3. **Q:** Design a damper for a jua kali water tank on a tower that sways in the wind. What form should the damping take?
   **Worked Answer:** The water tank oscillates as an inverted pendulum (the tower is the "string", the tank is the "bob"). Wind provides the driving force. To damp: (1) add a tuned mass damper — a heavy block on a spring inside the tank, tuned to the tower's natural frequency, that absorbs energy (used in tall buildings). (2) add hydraulic dampers between the tower and the ground — pistons in oil that resist the sway. (3) increase structural damping by adding flexible joints or rubber pads that dissipate energy through internal friction. (4) detune by stiffening the tower (raising the natural frequency above the wind frequency). The simplest jua kali approach: cross-bracing (stiffens) plus a water-filled U-tube damper inside the tank (the sloshing water absorbs energy — a tuned liquid damper).

## Safety Notes
- Do not overstretch the rubber strip — it can snap. Keep amplitudes under 5 cm.
- The mass can fall if the strip breaks — use a soft landing surface (grass, a mat).
- Cardboard edges can be sharp — tape the edges if needed.
- If observing a real boda-boda, do not push down hard enough to damage the suspension. Push gently.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 254 introduced resonance (adding energy). This lesson is the counterpart: damping (removing energy). The boda-boda shock absorber is the local context for this concept.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the demo and the idea that damping kills oscillations. Ages 14–17 should quantify decay rates, relate amplitude to energy (E ∝ A²), and design damping solutions.
- Local resource tips: a rubber strip from an inner tube is the most accessible oscillator. A card damper is cardboard from a box. For the boda-boda observation, any parked bike with rear suspension works.
- The bounce-test comparison (1.5 vs 4 cycles) connects directly to the boda-boda context. If learners have access to a bike, the homework task (push the corner and count bounces) is a powerful real-world measurement.
