# Lesson 256: Springs Oscillate Too: Mass on a Rubber Strip

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3; G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a vertical spring oscillator using a rubber strip and measure its period.
2. Vary the hanging mass and discover that heavier masses oscillate slower (unlike the pendulum).
3. Plot T² vs m and recognise the straight-line relationship.
4. Extract the spring constant k from the gradient using T = 2π√(m/k).

## Materials
- A rubber strip (~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long) from an inner tube — the "spring"
- Masses: stones or metal objects of at least three different masses (e.g., ~50 g, ~100 g, ~200 g)
- A stand or hook to hang the rubber strip
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Ruler or tape measure — to measure extension
- Graph paper or squared notebook
- Calculator (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **spring constant (k)** (ugumu wa springi) — the stiffness of the spring, in N/m; higher k = stiffer
- **extension** (kunjua) — how much the spring stretches under a load, in metres
- **Hooke's law** (sheria ya Hooke) — F = kx; force is proportional to extension
- **mass-dependent** (inategemea uzito) — unlike the pendulum, the spring's period depends on mass

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A heavier mass hangs on a spring. Does it oscillate faster or slower than a lighter one?
2. The pendulum's period does NOT depend on mass. Does the spring's period depend on mass?
3. Write the formula for the spring period T.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Slower — T = 2π√(m/k), so heavier mass (larger m) gives longer T.
2. Yes — unlike the pendulum, the spring's period depends on mass. (The pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g), no m. The spring: T = 2π√(m/k), m is in the formula.)
3. T = 2π√(m/k), where m is the mass (kg) and k is the spring constant (N/m).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — recap: the pendulum's period depends on length, not mass (Lesson 246). Today: does a spring's period depend on mass? Hang a rubber strip with a light mass, then a heavy one. The class watches: the heavy one bounces slower. Board the contrast: pendulum (mass-independent) vs spring (mass-dependent).
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the rubber-strip oscillator and measure the period at three masses (see Hands-On Build). For each mass, they: (a) measure the extension at equilibrium (to compute k = mg/x), (b) time 10 oscillations for the period. They record: mass | extension | k | period. Then they compute T² for each mass and plot T² vs m on graph paper. The graph should be a straight line through the origin.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The spring period is T = 2π√(m/k) — it depends on mass (unlike the pendulum). Squaring: T² = 4π²m/k. So T² ∝ m — plotting T² against m gives a straight line through the origin with gradient 4π²/k. From the gradient, k = 4π²/gradient. This is the spring equivalent of the pendulum's T² vs L graph (Lesson 249). Confront the misconception that "the pendulum and spring are the same" — they share the SHM structure (2π√(inertia/restoring)), but the inertia term is different: L for the pendulum (geometric), m for the spring (mass). The pendulum's restoring force is gravity (mg), which contains m, so m cancels. The spring's restoring force is stiffness (kx), which does not contain m, so m stays in the formula.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the spring cares about mass; the pendulum doesn't*. Homework: complete the T² vs m graph and compute k from the gradient.

## Hands-On Build: Rubber-Strip Oscillator and T² vs m Graph
*Build a vertical oscillator, vary the mass, and extract k from the graph.*

### Parts
- Rubber strip — ~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long, from an inner tube
- Three masses — ~50 g, ~100 g, ~200 g (stones or metal objects)
- Stand or hook
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Ruler — to measure extension
- Graph paper or squared notebook
- Calculator (phone)

### Workflow
1. Hang the rubber strip from the stand. Attach the lightest mass. Let it settle. **Checkpoint:** the mass hangs still; the strip is stretched but not overextended.
2. Measure the extension (stretch from natural length to equilibrium). Compute k = mg/x. **Checkpoint:** k should be roughly the same for all masses (it's a property of the strip, not the mass). If k varies a lot, the rubber is non-linear — use small extensions.
3. Pull the mass down 2 cm and release. Time 10 oscillations. Divide by 10 for T. **Checkpoint:** the motion is vertical, not sideways.
4. Record: mass (kg) | extension (m) | k (N/m) | T (s).
5. Swap to the next mass. Repeat for all three masses. **Checkpoint:** heavier masses should give longer periods.
6. Compute T² for each mass. Plot T² (y-axis, s²) vs m (x-axis, kg). **Checkpoint:** the points should form a straight line through the origin.
7. Calculate the gradient = ΔT²/Δm. Compute k = 4π²/gradient = 39.48/gradient. Compare with the k from step 2.

### Common Mistakes
- **Non-linear rubber** — rubber strips are only approximately Hookean for small extensions. Keep extensions under 5 cm. If the strip stretches too much, k decreases (rubber softens), and the T² vs m graph curves.
- **Sideways swinging** — if the mass swings as a pendulum, the period is contaminated. Keep the motion purely vertical.
- **Mass in grams, not kg** — the formula needs kg. 100 g = 0.1 kg. Plotting in grams gives a gradient 1000× too large.
- **Not measuring extension** — without the extension, you can't compute k independently. Measure from the unstretched length to equilibrium.
- **Too few masses** — three points make a line; two is risky. Use at least three masses, ideally four or five.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A heavier mass on a spring: faster or slower? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Does the spring's period depend on mass? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Write T = 2π√(m/k). If you double m, what happens to T? (mirrors pre-test)
4. The T² vs m graph has gradient 40 s²/kg. Calculate k.
5. Challenge: explain why the spring period depends on mass but the pendulum period does not.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Slower — T = 2π√(m/k), so larger m gives longer T.
2. Yes — T = 2π√(m/k) contains m. Unlike the pendulum (T = 2π√(L/g), no m).
3. T increases by √2 ≈ 1.41. Doubling m under the square root multiplies T by √2.
4. k = 4π²/gradient = 39.48/40 = 0.987 N/m ≈ 1.0 N/m.
5. The pendulum's restoring force is gravity (mg sin θ), which contains m. The inertia is also m. In T = 2π√(L/g), the m cancels (g = restoring/inertia = mg/m = g). The spring's restoring force is kx (stiffness × displacement), which does NOT contain m. The inertia is m. So m stays in the formula: T = 2π√(m/k). The spring "cares" about mass because the restoring force doesn't scale with mass.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Plot T² vs m; use the gradient with T = 2π√(m/k) to extract your strip's spring constant k.
   **Worked Answer:** T² = 4π²m/k → T² = (4π²/k) × m. The gradient of T² vs m is 4π²/k. Example data: m = 0.05 kg → T = 0.45 s → T² = 0.20 s²; m = 0.10 kg → T = 0.64 s → T² = 0.41 s²; m = 0.20 kg → T = 0.90 s → T² = 0.81 s². Gradient = (0.81 − 0.20)/(0.20 − 0.05) = 0.61/0.15 = 4.07 s²/kg. k = 4π²/4.07 = 39.48/4.07 = 9.70 N/m. Check against k from extension: if 0.10 kg stretches 0.10 m, k = 0.10 × 9.8/0.10 = 9.8 N/m — good agreement.
2. **Q:** A rubber strip gives k = 10 N/m. Predict T for m = 0.1 kg and m = 0.4 kg. By what factor does T change?
   **Worked Answer:** T₁ = 2π√(0.1/10) = 2π√0.01 = 2π × 0.1 = 0.628 s. T₂ = 2π√(0.4/10) = 2π√0.04 = 2π × 0.2 = 1.257 s. Factor: 1.257/0.628 = 2.0. Doubling m twice (4×) doubles T (since T ∝ √m, and √4 = 2). This is the same √ relationship as the pendulum (T ∝ √L).
3. **Q:** Compare the pendulum (T independent of m) and spring (T ∝ √m). What physical difference causes this?
   **Worked Answer:** In the pendulum, the restoring force (mg sin θ) and the inertia (ma) both contain m. Newton's law: ma = −mg(x/L) → a = −g(x/L). The m cancels — acceleration depends only on g and geometry, not on mass. In the spring, the restoring force (kx) does NOT contain m. Newton's law: ma = −kx → a = −(k/m)x. The m does not cancel — acceleration depends on k/m, so heavier masses accelerate less and oscillate slower. The key: in the pendulum, gravity provides the restoring force, and gravity's force scales with mass (weight = mg). In the spring, the restoring force is elastic (kx), independent of mass. This is why the pendulum is mass-independent and the spring is mass-dependent.

## Safety Notes
- Do not overstretch the rubber strip — it can snap and whip. Keep amplitudes under 3 cm.
- Heavy masses can fall if the strip breaks — use a soft landing surface.
- Do not use masses over 300 g on a thin rubber strip — it may exceed the elastic limit.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 243–250 covered the pendulum in depth, including the T² vs L graph (Lesson 249). This lesson introduces the spring oscillator and repeats the graphical method for a new variable (mass).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the oscillator and seeing that heavier = slower. Ages 14–17 should plot T² vs m, extract k, and explain the mass-dependence contrast with the pendulum.
- Local resource tips: a rubber strip from an inner tube is the most accessible spring. Cut it 2–3 cm wide. Metal springs from pens are usually too stiff; a slinky or flexible wire works better if available.
- The structural comparison between the two oscillator formulas is the key takeaway. Both are 2π√(inertia/restoring), but the inertia and restoring terms differ. This pattern is universal in SHM.
