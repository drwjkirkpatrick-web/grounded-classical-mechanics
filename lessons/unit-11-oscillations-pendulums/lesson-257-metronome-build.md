# Lesson 257: Build: A Stick Metronome

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** metronome

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build an inverted pendulum (a stick rocking on a pivot) with a sliding clay weight.
2. Discover that sliding the weight up (away from the pivot) slows the beat and down speeds it up.
3. Calibrate the metronome to specific beats per minute (BPM) for music.
4. Explain with the physical-pendulum idea why moving the mass up slows the beat.

## Materials
- A ruler or flat stick (~30–50 cm)
- A pivot: a nail, a rounded edge of a desk, or a V-notch cut in a block
- A sliding weight: a lump of clay (plasticine) or a peg clip that grips the stick
- A protractor card (optional — for reference)
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **metronome** (metronomu) — a device that produces a steady beat for music practice
- **inverted pendulum** (pendulum ya kinyume) — a stick balanced point-down on a pivot, rocking side to side
- **beats per minute (BPM)** (mara kwa dakika) — the number of oscillations per minute; standard musical tempo unit
- **physical pendulum** (pendulum halisi) — any rigid object oscillating about a pivot (not a point mass on a string)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A metronome ticks at a steady rate. What controls the speed of the tick?
2. If you slide a weight up a metronome stick (away from the pivot), does the beat get faster or slower?
3. What does BPM mean in music?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The position of the weight on the stick. Moving the weight changes the effective length and the moment of inertia, changing the period.
2. Slower — the weight further from the pivot increases the moment of inertia (resistance to rotation), so the rocking period increases.
3. Beats per minute — the number of steady beats in one minute. A tempo of 60 BPM = one beat per second. 120 BPM = two beats per second.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate a real metronome (or play a metronome app). Ask: *how does it change tempo?* In a mechanical metronome, a weight slides on a stick — up for slow, down for fast. Board the challenge: build one from a stick and clay.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the stick metronome (see Hands-On Build). They balance the stick point-down on the pivot, attach the clay weight, and set it rocking. They try the weight at three positions: near the pivot, middle, and far from the pivot. At each position, they time 10 beats and compute the period T and the BPM. They record: weight position | T (s) | BPM. Then they calibrate: find the position that gives 60 BPM (T = 1 s) and 120 BPM (T = 0.5 s).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The stick metronome is a **physical pendulum** — a rigid body oscillating about a pivot, not a point mass on a string. The period depends on both the mass distribution and the distance from the pivot. Moving the weight up (away from the pivot) increases the **moment of inertia** (the resistance to rotation, I ∝ m × r²). A larger I means the stick is harder to swing, so the period increases (slower beat). Moving the weight down (toward the pivot) decreases I, so the period decreases (faster beat). The physical-pendulum formula is T = 2π√(I/(mgd)), where I is the moment of inertia, m is the mass, g is gravity, and d is the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass. Moving the weight up increases I and d, but I grows as r² while the restoring torque grows as d (linear), so I wins — the period increases. Confront the misconception that "moving the weight up makes it swing faster because it's lighter at the bottom" — it's not about weight distribution; it's about the moment of inertia. The total mass is the same; the distribution changes.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the further the mass, the slower the beat*. Homework: calibrate your metronome to 80 BPM and mark the position on the stick.

## Hands-On Build: Stick Metronome
*Build an inverted pendulum metronome with a sliding clay weight.*

### Parts
- A ruler or flat stick — ~30–50 cm, light enough to rock freely
- A pivot — a nail, the rounded edge of a desk, or a V-notch in a block of wood
- A sliding weight — a lump of clay (plasticine) or a peg clip that grips the stick
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Protractor card (optional)

### Workflow
1. Balance the stick point-down on the pivot. Find the balance point (the centre of mass should be just above the pivot so the stick rocks but doesn't fall). **Checkpoint:** the stick should rock side to side and return — like a metronome. If it falls, the centre of mass is too high; add a small weight at the bottom.
2. Attach the clay weight near the bottom of the stick (close to the pivot). Set it rocking gently (small amplitude, ~5°). **Checkpoint:** the stick rocks smoothly without toppling.
3. Time 10 beats (one beat = one full left-right-left cycle or one half-cycle, depending on convention; use one full cycle for consistency). Divide by 10 for T. Compute BPM = 60/T. **Checkpoint:** the weight near the pivot should give a fast beat.
4. Slide the weight up (away from the pivot) by 5 cm. Re-time 10 beats. Record T and BPM. **Checkpoint:** the beat should be slower.
5. Slide up another 5 cm. Re-time. Record. **Checkpoint:** slower again.
6. Find the position for 60 BPM (T = 1.0 s) and 120 BPM (T = 0.5 s). Mark them on the stick with a pen.

### Common Mistakes
- **Stick topples** — the centre of mass is above the pivot but too far. The stick needs the centre of mass just slightly above the pivot for stable rocking. If it keeps falling, add a small weight at the bottom or use a wider pivot (V-notch).
- **Weight too light** — the clay must be heavy enough to shift the centre of mass noticeably. Use a generous lump (20–50 g).
- **Large amplitude** — the stick may topple at large angles. Keep the rocking small (~5°).
- **Friction at the pivot** — a rough pivot adds damping and the rocking dies quickly. Use a smooth nail or a rounded edge.
- **Counting half-cycles vs full cycles** — decide whether one "beat" is a half-cycle (one direction) or a full cycle. In music, a metronome click is one half-cycle (left or right). Be consistent and note which you use. (For BPM = 60/T where T is the full-cycle period: one click per half-cycle = 2 clicks per cycle → BPM = 120/T.)

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What controls the speed of a metronome? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Sliding the weight up (away from pivot): faster or slower? (mirrors pre-test)
3. What does BPM mean? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your metronome has T = 0.75 s at one position. What is the BPM?
5. Challenge: explain with the physical-pendulum formula why moving the mass up slows the beat.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The position of the sliding weight on the stick. Moving the weight changes the moment of inertia and the centre-of-mass distance, changing the period.
2. Slower — the weight further from the pivot increases the moment of inertia (I ∝ mr²), which dominates over the increase in restoring torque, so the period increases.
3. Beats per minute — the number of steady beats in one minute. 60 BPM = one beat per second.
4. BPM = 60/T = 60/0.75 = 80 BPM. (If counting one click per half-cycle: 120/0.75 = 160 clicks per minute. Clarify your convention.)
5. The physical-pendulum formula is T = 2π√(I/(mgd)). I is the moment of inertia (resistance to rotation), which grows as m×r² (r = distance from pivot). d is the distance from pivot to centre of mass, which grows linearly with r. When the weight moves up, I grows as r² but the restoring torque (mgd) grows only as r. So I/(mgd) grows as r — the period increases. The inertia grows faster than the restoring force, so the beat slows.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Explain with the physical-pendulum formula T = 2π√(I/(mgd)) why moving the mass up slows the beat.
   **Worked Answer:** For a point mass m at distance r from the pivot: I = mr² (moment of inertia about the pivot). The centre of mass is at distance d = r (for a light stick with most mass in the sliding weight). T = 2π√(mr²/(mgr)) = 2π√(r/g). So T ∝ √r — moving the weight further from the pivot (larger r) increases the period. This is the same √ relationship as the simple pendulum (T ∝ √L), but here L is replaced by r (the distance to the centre of mass). The physical explanation: I grows as r² (harder to rotate), while the restoring torque grows only as r (gravity × lever arm). The ratio I/(mgd) = r/g grows, so T increases.
2. **Q:** Your metronome gives T = 0.5 s with the weight at r = 10 cm. Where should you place it for T = 1.0 s?
   **Worked Answer:** T ∝ √r (from the formula above). T₂/T₁ = √(r₂/r₁) → 1.0/0.5 = √(r₂/10) → 2 = √(r₂/10) → 4 = r₂/10 → r₂ = 40 cm. Place the weight at 40 cm from the pivot. (If the stick is only 30 cm long, you can't reach 40 cm — use a longer stick or a lighter weight to change the relationship.)
3. **Q:** A real metronome has the pivot at the bottom and the weight slides on the upper part. Why is this design better than a simple pendulum for a musician?
   **Worked Answer:** (1) Compact — an inverted pendulum packs into a small box; a simple pendulum needs a tall frame. (2) Adjustable — sliding the weight continuously changes the tempo, unlike a pendulum where you must adjust the string length. (3) Visible scale — the stick can be marked with BPM positions. (4) Stable — the inverted pendulum's self-righting tendency (centre of mass below the pivot when the weight is near the bottom) means it returns to vertical; a simple pendulum has no preferred rest direction. (5) The physical-pendulum formula gives a wide tempo range (40–208 BPM) on a short stick by moving the weight a few centimetres.

## Safety Notes
- The stick can topple off the pivot — keep the area clear.
- A nail pivot is sharp at the point — blunt it or cover with tape.
- Do not use a heavy weight that could make the stick fall hard.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 243–250 covered the simple pendulum. This lesson introduces the physical pendulum — a rigid body, not a point mass — through a practical build.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building and calibrating the metronome. Ages 14–17 should derive T ∝ √r from the physical-pendulum formula and predict positions for target BPMs.
- Local resource tips: a ruler or a flat stick from a tree branch. Clay (plasticine) is available in most schools; alternatively, use a peg or a clip that grips the stick. A nail in a block of wood makes a good pivot.
- This is the metronome project build. The calibration marks on the stick are the deliverable. If possible, have learners use their metronome in a music class to practice keeping time.
