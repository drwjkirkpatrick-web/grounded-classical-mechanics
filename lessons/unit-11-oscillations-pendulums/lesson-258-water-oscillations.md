# Lesson 258: Sloshing Water: Basins, Tanks and Bores

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Slosh a basin of water and measure its natural slosh period.
2. Discover that wider basins have longer slosh periods (the water acts like a pendulum whose "length" is the basin width).
3. Explain why tankers and water trucks use internal baffles to damp sloshing.
4. Describe a seiche — a standing wave oscillation in a lake — as the large-scale version of the same physics.

## Materials
- Two basins or wide containers of different widths (e.g., a cooking pot ~20 cm and a washing basin ~40 cm)
- Water
- Divider cards: pieces of cardboard cut to fit inside the basins (to act as baffles)
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Ruler — to measure basin width
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **sloshing** (kujikwaa maji) — water oscillating back and forth in a container
- **baffle** (kizuizi) — an internal wall inside a tank that blocks and damps sloshing
- **seiche** (seiche) — a standing wave in an enclosed body of water (lake, harbour) that sloshes back and forth
- **natural slosh period** (kipindi cha kujikwaa asili) — the time for one complete slosh cycle, determined by basin size

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you slosh water in a basin, does it keep going forever or does it stop?
2. A wider basin: will the water slosh faster or slower than a narrow one?
3. Why might a water truck tip over if it has no internal walls?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. It stops — friction with the basin walls and the basin bottom damps the oscillation (energy → heat).
2. Slower — wider basins have longer slosh periods. The water travels further per cycle, like a longer pendulum.
3. The water sloshes to one side, shifting the weight dramatically. This can unbalance the truck, especially on a turn or a bump — the moving water acts like a giant swinging mass.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate: half-fill a basin with water. Tip it slightly and return. The water sloshes back and forth, then settles. Ask: *what controls how fast it sloshes?* Board the challenge: measure the slosh period of two basins and discover the pattern.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs run the sloshing experiment (see Hands-On Build). They half-fill each basin, give a single tip, and time the slosh period (10 cycles or until it dies). They measure the basin width. Then they insert a baffle (divider card) and repeat — the slosh should die much faster. Results go on a class table: basin | width (cm) | slosh period (s) | cycles to settle (no baffle) | cycles to settle (with baffle).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Sloshing water is an oscillation. The water surface tilts and then restores under gravity — like a pendulum where the "length" is related to the basin width. Wider basins have longer slosh periods (T ∝ √(width), approximately, for shallow water). Tankers and water trucks use **baffles** — internal walls with holes — to break up the slosh. The baffle forces water through small openings, creating turbulence and friction that dissipate energy (damping). Without baffles, a half-full water truck can tip over on a sharp turn — the water sloshes to one side and shifts the centre of mass beyond the wheelbase. A **seiche** is the large-scale version: in a lake, wind or seismic activity pushes water to one end; when the wind stops, the water sloshes back and forth for hours or days. Lake Erie (USA/Canada) has famous seiches up to 2 m. Lake Victoria can also seiche. Confront the misconception that "baffles hold the water still" — they don't hold it; they damp it by forcing it through restricted passages that dissipate kinetic energy as heat.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *water sloshes like a pendulum — baffles are the shock absorbers*. Homework: look at a water truck or fuel tanker and count the baffles visible from outside (hatch covers or weld lines).

## Hands-On Build: Basin Slosh and Baffle Test
*Measure slosh period for basins of different widths and test baffle damping.*

### Parts
- Two basins of different widths (e.g., a pot ~20 cm and a washing basin ~40 cm)
- Water — fill each basin to ~40% depth
- Divider cards (cardboard cut to fit inside the basin, with small holes punched)
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Ruler — to measure basin width
- A table or flat surface

### Workflow
1. Fill the narrow basin to ~40% depth. Place it on a flat table. **Checkpoint:** the water is still before you start.
2. Tip the basin ~2 cm to one side and quickly return it to flat. The water sloshes. **Checkpoint:** the water sloshes smoothly without splashing over the rim.
3. Time the slosh: count 10 complete oscillations (left-high to right-high and back = 1 cycle) and divide by 10 for the period. If the slosh dies before 10 cycles, time as many as you can and divide by that count. **Checkpoint:** record T and the number of cycles to settle (amplitude below ~1 cm).
4. Measure the basin width. Record: width | T | cycles to settle. **Checkpoint:** the water is calm before the next step.
5. Repeat with the wide basin. Record. **Checkpoint:** the wide basin should have a longer slosh period.
6. Insert a baffle card (with holes punched) into the wide basin, dividing it roughly in half. Tip and time the slosh. **Checkpoint:** the baffle should cause the slosh to die much faster — typically 2–3 cycles instead of 10+.

### Common Mistakes
- **Too much water** — if the basin is more than half full, the slosh splashes over the rim. Fill to ~40%.
- **Tipping too hard** — a violent tip creates a splash wave, not a clean slosh. Tip gently (~2 cm).
- **Baffle without holes** — a solid card just splits the basin into two smaller basins (each sloshes independently). A baffle with holes allows water through but dissipates energy via turbulence — that's the real damping mechanism.
- **Measuring the wrong dimension** — the slosh period depends on the width in the direction of the slosh, not the depth or length. Measure the width in the slosh direction.
- **Water not settled before starting** — wait for the surface to be still before tipping.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Does sloshing water go forever or does it stop? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Wider basin: faster or slower slosh? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why might a water truck without baffles tip over? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your wide basin (40 cm) sloshes with T = 0.8 s. Your narrow basin (20 cm) sloshes with T = 0.55 s. By what factor does the period change when you double the width? Is it consistent with T ∝ √(width)?
5. Challenge: explain why a baffle with holes damps sloshing, but a solid wall does not.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. It stops — friction with the basin walls and bottom, and internal turbulence, dissipate the oscillation energy as heat.
2. Slower — wider basins have longer slosh periods. The water mass travels further per cycle.
3. The water sloshes to one side, shifting the centre of mass. On a turn or bump, this can push the truck beyond its balance point, causing a roll-over. Baffles break up the slosh so the weight stays centred.
4. T₁ = 0.8 s (40 cm), T₂ = 0.55 s (20 cm). Factor: 0.8/0.55 = 1.45. If T ∝ √(width): doubling width → √2 = 1.41. The measured 1.45 is close to 1.41 — consistent within experimental error.
5. A solid wall splits the basin into two smaller basins. Each half sloshes independently at its own (shorter) period — the wall doesn't dissipate energy, it just changes the geometry. A baffle with holes lets water through, but the narrow openings force the water to accelerate and decelerate through them, creating turbulence and friction. The kinetic energy of the slosh is dissipated as heat through this turbulent flow. That's true damping — removing energy from the oscillation.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Explain why baffles raise damping and shift frequency; design a baffle layout for a pickup water tank.
   **Worked Answer:** Baffles with holes increase damping by forcing water through narrow passages, creating turbulent jets that dissipate kinetic energy as heat. They also shift the natural frequency by dividing the tank into smaller sub-volumes — each sub-volume has a shorter effective slosh length, raising the natural frequency. For a pickup water tank (e.g., 1.5 m long × 0.8 m wide × 0.5 m deep): install 2–3 baffles across the width (perpendicular to the driving direction), each with holes totalling ~20% of the baffle area. Space them evenly (~50 cm apart). The holes should be 5–10 cm diameter, distributed top and bottom to allow both upper and lower flow. This design damps the dominant longitudinal slosh (the dangerous front-to-back motion on braking/acceleration) while still allowing the tank to drain and fill.
2. **Q:** A shallow basin of width L has slosh period T ≈ 2L/√(gh), where h is the water depth. Compute T for L = 40 cm, h = 8 cm, g = 9.8.
   **Worked Answer:** T ≈ 2L/√(gh) = 2 × 0.40/√(9.8 × 0.08) = 0.80/√0.784 = 0.80/0.885 = 0.904 s. This is the shallow-water seiche formula. For the narrow basin (L = 20 cm): T = 0.40/0.885 = 0.452 s. Ratio: 0.904/0.452 = 2.0 — consistent with T ∝ L (linear, not √L, for shallow water). Note: the shallow-water formula gives T ∝ L, not T ∝ √L. The √L pattern from the pre-test is an approximation; the exact relationship depends on depth-to-width ratio.
3. **Q:** Lake Victoria is ~240 km wide. Using the shallow-water seiche formula, estimate the seiche period for a depth of 40 m.
   **Worked Answer:** T ≈ 2L/√(gh) = 2 × 240000/√(9.8 × 40) = 480000/√392 = 480000/19.8 = 24242 s ≈ 6.7 hours. A Lake Victoria seiche would slosh back and forth with a period of about 6–7 hours. This is why seiches are hard to notice — the water rises and falls slowly over hours, not seconds. Wind-driven seiches on Lake Victoria can produce water-level differences of 10–20 cm between ends.

## Safety Notes
- Do not use glass basins — breakage risk with water movement.
- Keep water away from electronics (phones used for timing should stay dry).
- Clean up spills immediately — wet floors are slippery.
- Do not drink the slosh water (basin may not be food-safe).
