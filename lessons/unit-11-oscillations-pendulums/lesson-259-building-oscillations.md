# Lesson 259: Buildings That Sway

**Unit:** 11 — Oscillations & Pendulums  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** shake-table

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Model buildings with sticks and clay on a simple shake table (a board on rollers or cylindrical pens).
2. Discover that flexible (tall, thin) buildings have a lower natural frequency than stiff (short, wide) ones.
3. Explain why earthquake resonance is dangerous when the ground frequency matches a building's natural frequency.
4. Describe how water tanks on towers act like inverted pendulums and why this matters in the Rift Valley.

## Materials
- Sticks or dowels (~30 cm and ~50 cm, or different widths)
- Clay (plasticine) — to make the "floors" and "roofs" of model buildings
- A shake table: a board on rollers (or on 2–3 cylindrical pens/pencils)
- A ruler — to measure sway amplitude
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **natural frequency** (mara kwa sekunde asili) — the frequency at which a building sways when disturbed
- **shake table** (meza ya kutingisha) — a platform that simulates earthquake ground motion
- **resonance** (mvuto wa sauti) — when ground motion frequency matches the building's natural frequency → large sway → damage
- **inverted pendulum** (pendulum ya kinyume) — a mass on top of a flexible tower (like a water tank); the pivot is at the base

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A tall thin building and a short wide building: which one sways more in an earthquake?
2. What is resonance, and why is it dangerous for buildings?
3. A water tank on a tower: what kind of pendulum does it act like?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The tall thin building sways more — it is more flexible, has a lower natural frequency, and can resonate with low-frequency earthquake waves. Short, stiff buildings resist more but resonate with higher-frequency waves.
2. Resonance is when the driving frequency (ground motion from an earthquake) matches the building's natural frequency. The building's sway amplitude grows each cycle, potentially causing structural failure. It's dangerous because the amplitude can exceed the building's design limits.
3. An inverted pendulum — the tank is the "bob" and the tower is the "string." The pivot is at the base (the ground). When the ground moves, the tank's inertia keeps it in place and the tower bends, like a pendulum rocking on its pivot.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — tell the story: in 1985, Mexico City had a magnitude 8.1 earthquake. Most damage was to buildings between 6 and 20 stories — their natural frequency matched the ground motion frequency (~0.5 Hz). Shorter and taller buildings survived. Ask: *why did the mid-height buildings suffer?* Board: **resonance**. The Kenya context: the Rift Valley is seismically active. Ask learners if they have felt a tremor.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build model buildings and test them on the shake table (see Hands-On Build). Each pair builds two models: (a) a tall thin stick with clay on top (flexible, low frequency) and (b) a short wide stick with clay (stiff, high frequency). They place both on the shake table and shake at different rates: slow, medium, fast. They observe which model resonates (sways the most) at each shaking rate. They also time the natural sway period of each model by pulling the top to one side and releasing. Results go on a class table: model | natural period (s) | resonates at (slow/medium/fast) | sway amplitude (small/medium/large).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Buildings are oscillators. Their natural frequency depends on height and stiffness: tall, flexible buildings have low natural frequencies (long sway periods); short, stiff buildings have high natural frequencies (short sway periods). Earthquake ground motion contains a range of frequencies. When the ground frequency matches a building's natural frequency → **resonance** → the sway amplitude grows → structural damage. The Mexico City example shows this clearly: mid-height buildings (natural frequency ~0.5 Hz) matched the ground motion frequency and resonated. A water tank on a tower is an **inverted pendulum** — the tower is the flexible support, the tank is the mass. If the ground motion frequency matches the tower-tank system's natural frequency, the tank sways dangerously. Confront the misconception that "stiffer is always better" — a very stiff building has a high natural frequency, which may match high-frequency ground motion. The goal is **frequency mismatch**: the building's natural frequency should be different from expected ground frequencies. Engineers also add **damping** (tuned mass dampers, base isolation, flexible joints) to reduce the resonant amplitude. The Taipei 101 tower has a 660-tonne tuned mass damper — a giant pendulum inside the building that absorbs sway energy.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *match the frequency, damage the building — mismatch and damp to survive*. Homework: look at a water tower or tall building near your home and estimate whether it would be a high or low-frequency oscillator.

## Hands-On Build: Shake Table and Model Buildings
*Build two model buildings (flexible vs stiff) and test them on a shake table.*

### Parts
- Sticks or dowels — at least two: one thin and tall (~50 cm), one thick and short (~30 cm)
- Clay (plasticine) — for the building masses (floors/roofs)
- A shake table: a board (~30×40 cm) on 2–3 cylindrical pens or pencils (rollers)
- Ruler — to measure sway
- Stopwatch (phone)
- A flat table

### Workflow
1. Build the flexible model: attach a lump of clay (~30 g) to the top of the thin, tall stick. Embed the stick's base in a lump of clay on the shake-table board. **Checkpoint:** the stick stands vertically and sways when you push the top gently.
2. Build the stiff model: attach a similar clay lump to the top of the thick, short stick. Embed the base in clay on the board. **Checkpoint:** the short stick barely moves when you push the top.
3. Measure the natural sway period of each model: pull the top ~2 cm to one side, release, and time 5–10 sway cycles. Divide for T. **Checkpoint:** the tall model should have a longer period (lower frequency) than the short one.
4. Place the board on the rollers (pens under the board). Shake the board slowly (one push per ~2 s) and watch both models. **Checkpoint:** the tall model should sway more — its low natural frequency is close to the slow shake rate.
5. Shake fast (several pushes per second). Watch both models. **Checkpoint:** the short model should sway more — its high natural frequency is closer to the fast shake rate.
6. Record: model | natural period (s) | resonates at (slow/medium/fast) | sway amplitude (small/medium/large).

### Common Mistakes
- **Board not rolling smoothly** — use 2–3 cylindrical pens spaced evenly under the board. If the board sticks, the shaking is jerky instead of smooth.
- **Model too stiff or too flexible** — adjust the stick thickness or clay mass. The tall model must sway visibly; the short model must resist.
- **Clay base not secure** — if the stick pulls out of its base clay during shaking, embed it deeper or use more clay. The base must be rigid relative to the stick.
- **Shaking too violently** — large-amplitude shaking makes both models sway regardless of frequency. Shake gently and smoothly — you are looking for frequency-matching, not brute force.
- **Not timing the natural period** — without knowing each model's natural period, you can't predict which shake rate will resonate. Always measure the natural period first.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Tall thin vs short wide building: which sways more in an earthquake? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is resonance, and why is it dangerous for buildings? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Water tank on a tower: what kind of pendulum? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your tall model has a natural period of 1.5 s. Your short model has a natural period of 0.3 s. Which will resonate with a slow shake (period ~1.5 s)?
5. Challenge: explain why engineers add damping (tuned mass dampers) to tall buildings. How does this connect to Lesson 255?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The tall thin building sways more — it is more flexible with a lower natural frequency. It can resonate with low-frequency earthquake waves. Short, stiff buildings have high natural frequencies and resist low-frequency shaking, but may resonate with high-frequency waves.
2. Resonance is when the ground motion frequency matches the building's natural frequency. Each cycle of ground motion adds energy in phase, and the sway amplitude grows. This can exceed the building's structural capacity, causing cracks, failure, or collapse. It's dangerous because the amplitude grows cycle after cycle.
3. An inverted pendulum. The tank is the mass (bob), the tower is the flexible support (string), and the ground is the pivot. When the ground moves, the tank's inertia resists motion and the tower bends. The natural frequency depends on the tank mass and tower stiffness.
4. The tall model (T = 1.5 s) will resonate — its natural period (1.5 s) matches the slow shake period (~1.5 s). The short model (T = 0.3 s) is far from the slow shake rate and will barely sway.
5. Damping removes energy from the oscillation, limiting the resonant amplitude. Without damping, resonance could build the sway until the building fails. A tuned mass damper (like Taipei 101's 660-tonne pendulum) absorbs sway energy: when the building sways one way, the damper lags and applies a force opposing the motion. This is the same physics as Lesson 255's shock absorber — the damper converts mechanical energy to heat through friction or fluid resistance, preventing the oscillation from growing. Resonance still occurs (the frequency still matches), but the amplitude is capped because the damper dissipates the energy as fast as the driving force adds it.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Tall flexible vs short stiff model on the shake table: relate survival to natural frequency mismatch.
   **Worked Answer:** Survival depends on the frequency content of the ground motion relative to the building's natural frequency. If the building's natural frequency is far from the dominant ground frequencies, the amplitude stays small (no resonance) — the building survives. If they match, resonance amplifies the sway. In the model: the tall building (low frequency, T ≈ 1.5 s, f ≈ 0.67 Hz) resonates with slow shaking. The short building (high frequency, T ≈ 0.3 s, f ≈ 3.3 Hz) resonates with fast shaking. Neither is "always better" — each is vulnerable to a different frequency range. Real earthquake-resistant design aims for frequency mismatch (tuning the building away from expected ground frequencies) plus damping (to cap the amplitude if resonance does occur). The Mexico City 1985 earthquake had dominant frequencies around 0.5 Hz, matching 6–20 story buildings. Shorter buildings (f > 1 Hz) and taller ones (f < 0.3 Hz) survived.
2. **Q:** A 4-storey building has a natural period of about 0.1 × number_of_storeys seconds (rule of thumb). Compute the natural frequency of a 10-storey building and compare with a 2-storey building.
   **Worked Answer:** 10-storey: T ≈ 0.1 × 10 = 1.0 s → f = 1/T = 1.0 Hz. 2-storey: T ≈ 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 s → f = 1/0.2 = 5.0 Hz. The 10-storey building has a much lower frequency (1 Hz vs 5 Hz). If an earthquake has dominant frequencies around 1 Hz (like Mexico City), the 10-storey building resonates but the 2-storey does not. If the earthquake has dominant frequencies around 5 Hz (shallow, nearby fault), the 2-storey resonates. The rule of thumb T ≈ 0.1N (N = storeys) is widely used in preliminary seismic design.
3. **Q:** A water tank (mass 5000 kg) sits on a tower 10 m tall. Estimate the natural period as an inverted pendulum and assess the risk in the Rift Valley.
   **Worked Answer:** Model as a simple pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g) = 2π√(10/9.8) = 2π × 1.008 = 6.33 s. f = 1/6.33 = 0.158 Hz. This is a very low frequency. Rift Valley earthquake frequencies range from ~0.1 Hz to ~10 Hz depending on the fault and distance. At 0.158 Hz, the tank-tower system could resonate with low-frequency, long-period earthquake waves (especially surface waves, which have periods of several seconds). The risk: if the tank sways too far, the tower legs could buckle. Mitigation: (1) add cross-bracing to stiffen the tower (raises frequency, detuning from surface waves), (2) add a tuned mass damper inside the tank, (3) add damping at the base (rubber bearings). The simplest: cross-bracing, which is common in Kenyan water tower construction.

## Safety Notes
- The shake table is gentle — but models can topple. Keep the area clear.
- Clay sticks can detach and fly — keep the amplitude small.
- Do not shake the board violently — the models are light and small movements are enough to show resonance.
- Pens used as rollers can roll off the table — keep them centred under the board.
