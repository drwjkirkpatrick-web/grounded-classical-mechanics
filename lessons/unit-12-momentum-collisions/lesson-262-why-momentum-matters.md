# Lesson 262: Why Momentum Matters on the Road

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain why an overloaded boda-boda or pickup takes longer to stop than an empty one.
2. Compute the momentum of a loaded vs unloaded vehicle at the same speed.
3. Describe how braking distance grows with both mass and speed (momentum).
4. Relate road safety signage (speed limits, load limits) to momentum physics.

## Materials
- A notebook and pencil
- A whiteboard or chalkboard
- Road safety data (optional): local speed limit signs, or the Kenyan highway code speed limits

## Vocabulary
- **braking distance** (mbali ya kufuli) — the distance a vehicle travels from brake application to stop
- **overloaded** (kupakiwa kupita kiasi) — carrying more mass than the design limit
- **momentum on the road** (mumbo barabarani) — p = mv for vehicles; the quantity that brakes must remove
- **load limit** (kipimo cha mizigo) — the maximum legal or safe mass for a vehicle

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A boda-boda carries one rider. The same boda carries a rider and a passenger and luggage. Which takes longer to stop at the same speed?
2. If you double the speed of a vehicle, what happens to its momentum?
3. Why do highways have lower speed limits for heavy lorries than for cars?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The loaded boda takes longer — more mass means more momentum (p = mv). The brakes must remove more momentum, which takes more distance (or more force, or more time).
2. Momentum doubles. p = mv, so if v → 2v with m unchanged: p → 2mv = 2p.
3. Heavy lorries have more mass, so at the same speed they have more momentum. A lower speed limit reduces their momentum to a level where braking is still effective within a safe distance. If a lorry travelled at car speeds, its braking distance would be impractically long.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show a picture or describe an overloaded pickup: three layers of sacks, people on top, swaying on a rural road. Ask: *why is this dangerous?* Board: momentum. The more mass on the vehicle, the more momentum at a given speed, and the harder it is to stop. Introduce the phrase **"momentum on the road."**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs work through road scenarios (see Hands-On Build — a data analysis, not physical). They compute momentum for: (a) an empty boda (150 kg, 15 m/s), (b) a loaded boda (290 kg, 15 m/s), (c) an empty pickup (1200 kg, 20 m/s), (d) a loaded pickup (3000 kg, 20 m/s). They compare: how much more momentum does the loaded vehicle have? They also explore braking distance: if braking force is fixed, braking distance is proportional to momentum (d ∝ p ∝ mv). They compute the ratio of braking distances. Results go on a class table: vehicle | mass | speed | momentum | braking distance ratio.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Braking distance depends on momentum: a heavier vehicle at the same speed has more momentum, so the brakes (applying a fixed force over a distance) need more distance to remove it. d ∝ mv/F_brake. Doubling the mass doubles the braking distance. Doubling the speed doubles the momentum — but the braking distance actually quadruples (because kinetic energy E = ½mv² and the brakes remove energy, not momentum; the distance is set by E/F). Confront the misconception that "braking distance is proportional to speed" — it is proportional to speed squared (d ∝ v²) because the brakes must dissipate kinetic energy (½mv²), not just momentum. However, the momentum framework explains why overloaded vehicles are harder to stop: more mass → more momentum AND more kinetic energy at the same speed. The load limit signage on roads is a direct application of momentum physics.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *more load, more momentum, more braking distance*. Homework: look at a speed limit sign near your home and write down the limit for cars vs lorries (if different). Explain why in one sentence.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Loaded vs empty boda at the same speed: which takes longer to stop? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If you double the speed, what happens to momentum? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why do lorries have lower speed limits? (mirrors pre-test)
4. An empty pickup (1200 kg) and a loaded pickup (3000 kg) both travel at 20 m/s. How many times more momentum does the loaded one have?
5. Challenge: braking distance depends on kinetic energy (½mv²), not just momentum. If you double the speed, by what factor does the braking distance increase?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The loaded boda — more mass → more momentum (p = mv) → the brakes must remove more momentum → longer braking distance.
2. Momentum doubles. p = mv → 2mv = 2p.
3. Heavy lorries have more mass, so at the same speed they have more momentum and more kinetic energy. Lower speed limits reduce both to levels where braking is effective within a safe distance.
4. p_empty = 1200 × 20 = 24,000 kg·m/s. p_loaded = 3000 × 20 = 60,000 kg·m/s. Ratio: 60,000/24,000 = 2.5. The loaded pickup has 2.5× the momentum.
5. Braking distance d ∝ v² (since KE = ½mv² and the brakes remove energy at a fixed rate of force × distance). Doubling the speed: d → (2v)² = 4v² → 4× the braking distance. This is why speed is the dominant factor in road safety — a small increase in speed causes a large increase in braking distance.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** An empty pickup (1200 kg) at 20 m/s vs a loaded one (3000 kg) at 20 m/s. Compute the momentum ratio and the braking distance ratio (assume constant braking force).
   **Worked Answer:** Momentum ratio: p_loaded/p_empty = (3000 × 20)/(1200 × 20) = 60,000/24,000 = 2.5. Braking distance: d = KE/F_brake = ½mv²/F_brake. d_loaded/d_empty = (3000 × 20²)/(1200 × 20²) = 3000/1200 = 2.5. Both ratios are 2.5 — at the same speed, braking distance is proportional to mass (and thus to momentum). The loaded pickup needs 2.5× the braking distance.
2. **Q:** A boda-boda (290 kg) at 15 m/s vs at 30 m/s (double speed). By what factor does braking distance increase?
   **Worked Answer:** d ∝ v². Doubling speed: d → (2)² = 4× the braking distance. So the boda at 30 m/s needs 4× the braking distance of the same boda at 15 m/s. Momentum also doubles (p = mv → 2p), but braking distance quadruples because it depends on v², not v. This is why speed limits are the single most effective road safety measure.
3. **Q:** A lorry (5000 kg) at 8 m/s and a car (1500 kg) at 27 m/s have the same momentum. Which has the longer braking distance?
   **Worked Answer:** Momentum: lorry p = 5000 × 8 = 40,000 kg·m/s. Car p = 1500 × 27 = 40,500 kg·m/s — approximately equal. Braking distance: d = ½mv²/F. Lorry: d ∝ 5000 × 8² = 5000 × 64 = 320,000. Car: d ∝ 1500 × 27² = 1500 × 729 = 1,093,500. Ratio: 1,093,500/320,000 = 3.4. The car needs 3.4× the braking distance despite having the same momentum! This is because braking distance depends on kinetic energy (½mv²), not momentum. The car's higher speed gives it much more energy per unit momentum. This is a critical insight: equal momentum does NOT mean equal braking distance — speed is the dominant factor.

## Safety Notes
- No physical activity — this is a conceptual and computational lesson.
- The road safety content is itself a safety lesson: reinforce that overloaded vehicles and speeding are dangerous.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 261 introduced p = mv. This lesson applies it to road safety — the most important real-world context for momentum in Kenya.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the idea that more load = harder to stop and compute simple momentum ratios. Ages 14–17 should derive the braking distance formula (d ∝ v²), compare equal-momentum vehicles with different speeds, and explain why speed is the dominant safety factor.
- Local resource tips: Kenyan speed limits (50 km/h in towns, 110 km/h on highways for cars, 80 km/h for lorries) are the local data. The overloaded pickup and boda-boda contexts are everyday observations.
- The connection between momentum (p = mv) and braking distance (d ∝ v²) is subtle: momentum explains why mass matters, but kinetic energy explains why speed matters more. Both are needed for a complete picture. Lesson 263 introduces impulse (F = Δp/Δt), which links momentum to force and time.
