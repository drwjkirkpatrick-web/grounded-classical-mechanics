# Lesson 263: Impulse: The Egg-Catching Secret

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define impulse as J = F × t = Δp (change in momentum).
2. Demonstrate that extending the collision time reduces the force (the egg-catch).
3. Explain why bending knees on landing, gym mats, and sand pits reduce impact force.
4. Compute the force from a given momentum change and collision time.

## Materials
- A raw egg (or a fragile object — a ripe tomato works well)
- A soft cloth or glove for catching
- A hard surface (a desk or the floor) for comparison
- A stopwatch (phone) — optional, for timing the catch
- A ruler — optional, for measuring the "give" distance
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **impulse (J)** (msukumo) — force × time; J = Ft = Δp; the change in momentum
- **collision time (t)** (muda wa mgongano) — the duration of the impact; longer t means less F for the same Δp
- **extending the time** (kuongeza muda) — the technique of "giving" with the impact to reduce force
- **impact force (F)** (nguvu ya mgongano) — the force during the collision; F = Δp/t

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Which hurts more: catching a ball with stiff hands or with hands that give?
2. Why do athletes bend their knees when landing from a jump?
3. Write the impulse formula.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Stiff hands — the collision time is short, so the force is large (F = Δp/t; small t → large F). Hands that give extend the time, reducing the force.
2. To extend the collision time. Bending the knees increases the time over which the momentum changes to zero, reducing the force on the legs and spine. Stiff legs → short time → large force → injury.
3. J = Ft = Δp. Impulse = force × time = change in momentum.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the egg toss: the teacher tosses an egg to a volunteer who catches it with stiff hands (it breaks) vs with hands that give (it survives). Ask: *why does giving save the egg?* Board: **impulse** — J = Ft = Δp. The momentum change is the same, but extending the time reduces the force.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs practise the egg-catch technique (see Hands-On Build). They drop a tomato (or egg) from waist height onto a hard surface (it splats) and onto a soft cloth or cushioned hand (it survives). They estimate the collision time: hard surface ~0.001 s, soft catch ~0.1 s — a factor of 100 difference. They compute the force ratio: F_hard/F_soft = t_soft/t_hard = 0.1/0.001 = 100. The soft catch reduces the force by 100×. They also test: dropping the tomato from different heights (more momentum = more force needed to stop) and catching with more or less "give."
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Impulse: J = Ft = Δp. The change in momentum (Δp) is fixed by the object's mass and speed before impact. The force depends on how quickly you stop it: F = Δp/t. Extending the collision time (t) reduces the force (F). Real-world applications: (a) bending knees on landing — extends the time, reduces the force on joints; (b) gym mats and sand pits — the soft material compresses, extending the stopping time; (c) car airbags — they inflate and deflate slowly (compared to hitting the dashboard), extending the collision time; (d) a crumple zone (Lesson 271) — the car's front end crumples, extending the collision time with the wall. Confront the misconception that "a soft mat reduces the energy" — the energy is the same (½mv²), but the mat extends the time over which it is dissipated, reducing the peak force. The energy still becomes heat; it just happens more gently.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *extend the time, reduce the force*. Homework: name three things in your home or school that extend collision time for safety (mattresses, mats, seatbelts, etc.).

## Hands-On Build: The Egg-Catch Experiment
*Compare the force of a hard stop vs a soft catch by dropping a fragile object.*

### Parts
- A raw egg or a ripe tomato (the "fragile object")
- A soft cloth, glove, or cushion
- A hard surface (desk or floor)
- A ruler — to measure drop height
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Hold the tomato at waist height (~1 m). Drop it onto the hard surface. **Checkpoint:** it splats — the collision time is very short (~0.001 s), so the force is very large.
2. Hold another tomato at the same height. Drop it onto the soft cloth or cushioned hand (give with the catch — let your hand move down with the tomato). **Checkpoint:** it survives — the collision time is longer (~0.1 s), so the force is much smaller.
3. Estimate the collision times: hard surface ~0.001 s (1 ms), soft catch ~0.1 s (100 ms). Compute the force ratio: F_hard/F_soft = t_soft/t_hard = 0.1/0.001 = 100. **Checkpoint:** the soft catch reduces the force by ~100×.
4. Try intermediate "give" — catch with a little give, a lot of give. Observe: more give = more survival. **Checkpoint:** the relationship is linear — doubling the give (collision time) halves the force.
5. Try different drop heights (waist, chest, head). More height → more speed → more momentum → more force needed to stop. **Checkpoint:** even with a soft catch, a very high drop may break the tomato — the momentum is too large for the extended time to fully compensate.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not giving with the catch** — if your hand is stiff, the collision time is short and the tomato breaks. The hand must move down with the tomato.
- **Dropping from too high** — the tomato may break even with a soft catch if the momentum is too large. Start at waist height.
- **Using an unripe tomato** — a hard tomato survives rough handling, giving a false result. Use a ripe, soft tomato.
- **Not cleaning up** — egg or tomato on the floor is slippery. Clean up immediately.
- **Confusing force and energy** — the energy is the same in both cases (same drop height → same KE). The force differs because the time differs. The mat doesn't reduce the energy; it reduces the force by extending the time.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Stiff hands vs giving hands: which hurts more? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why do athletes bend their knees on landing? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Write the impulse formula. (mirrors pre-test)
4. A 0.5 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and stops in 0.01 s. Calculate the force.
5. Challenge: the same ball is caught with a glove that extends the time to 0.2 s. Calculate the new force. By what factor is it reduced?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Stiff hands — short collision time → large force (F = Δp/t). Giving hands extend the time → smaller force.
2. To extend the collision time. Bending the knees increases t, reducing F = Δp/t. This protects the joints and spine from large impact forces.
3. J = Ft = Δp. Impulse = force × time = change in momentum.
4. Δp = mv = 0.5 × 10 = 5 kg·m/s. F = Δp/t = 5/0.01 = 500 N.
5. F = Δp/t = 5/0.2 = 25 N. Reduction factor: 500/25 = 20. The glove reduces the force by 20×.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A 0.5 kg ball at 10 m/s stops in 0.01 s (hard wall) vs 0.2 s (glove). Compute the force in each case and the reduction factor.
   **Worked Answer:** Δp = mv = 0.5 × 10 = 5 kg·m/s. Hard wall: F = 5/0.01 = 500 N. Glove: F = 5/0.2 = 25 N. Reduction factor: 500/25 = 20. The glove extends the collision time by 20× (0.2/0.01 = 20), so the force is reduced by 20×. This is exactly why goalkeepers' gloves are padded — the padding extends the collision time, reducing the sting of a hard shot.
2. **Q:** A 60 kg athlete lands from a jump at 5 m/s. Compare stiff-legged (t = 0.005 s) vs bent-knee (t = 0.3 s) landing forces.
   **Worked Answer:** Δp = mv = 60 × 5 = 300 kg·m/s. Stiff-legged: F = 300/0.005 = 60,000 N. Bent-knee: F = 300/0.3 = 1000 N. Reduction factor: 60,000/1000 = 60. The stiff-legged landing applies 60,000 N — about 100× the athlete's body weight (588 N). This would likely cause fracture. The bent-knee landing applies 1000 N — about 1.7× body weight, which is safe. The factor of 60 in collision time (0.3/0.005 = 60) gives a factor of 60 in force reduction.
3. **Q:** An airbag inflates in 0.03 s and deflates over 0.15 s. A 70 kg driver at 15 m/s hits the airbag. Compare the force with hitting the dashboard (t = 0.002 s).
   **Worked Answer:** Δp = 70 × 15 = 1050 kg·m/s. Dashboard: F = 1050/0.002 = 525,000 N — lethal. Airbag: F = 1050/0.15 = 7000 N — about 10× body weight, survivable. Reduction factor: 525,000/7000 = 75. The airbag extends the collision time by 75× (0.15/0.002 = 75), reducing the force by 75×. Note: the airbag also spreads the force over a larger area (the whole chest and face vs a small contact point on the dashboard), further reducing the pressure (force per area). Both effects — extended time and spread area — save lives.

## Safety Notes
- Use ripe tomatoes, not eggs, if you want to avoid mess. Eggs are more dramatic but harder to clean.
- Do not throw eggs or tomatoes at people — only drop onto surfaces or catch yourself.
- Clean up spills immediately — wet floors are slippery.
- Do not drop from above head height — the splatter area is large.
