# Lesson 264: Follow-Through: Impulse in Sport

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain why a longer contact time (follow-through) increases the impulse delivered to a ball.
2. Demonstrate follow-through in a kick, throw, or strike and feel the difference.
3. Relate follow-through to J = Ft: more time at the same force means more impulse (more momentum change).
4. Analyze sport examples: javelin run-up, football kick follow-through, jembe (hoe) swing.

## Materials
- A football (or a rolled-up cloth ball)
- A stick or ruler (to represent a bat or jembe handle)
- A target (a wall, a goal mark, or a stack of objects to knock over)
- A stopwatch (phone) — optional
- Notebook and pencil
- An open outdoor area

## Vocabulary
- **follow-through** (kuendeleza mwendokazi) — continuing the motion after contact to extend the collision time
- **contact time** (muda wa mgongano) — the duration the foot, hand, or implement is in contact with the ball
- **run-up** (mbio za kuanza) — the approach before a throw or kick that builds speed
- **impulse in sport** (msukumo michezani) — J = Ft; more contact time at the same force means more momentum delivered

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A footballer kicks the ball and stops the leg immediately. Another follows through. Which kick sends the ball further?
2. Why does a javelin thrower take a long run-up?
3. Write the impulse formula and explain how follow-through increases it.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The follow-through kick — the longer contact time means more impulse (J = Ft), so more momentum is delivered to the ball.
2. To build speed before the throw. The run-up gives the thrower (and thus the javelin) more momentum to transfer during the release.
3. J = Ft. Follow-through increases t (contact time) while maintaining F (force), so J increases. More impulse = more momentum change = faster ball.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate (or describe in slow motion) a football kick with and without follow-through. Without: the foot stops at contact — the ball dribbles forward. With: the foot continues through the ball — the ball flies. Ask: *why does follow-through matter?* Board: **J = Ft** — more time, more impulse.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs practise follow-through (see Hands-On Build). Each pair kicks (or throws) a ball with a short jab (no follow-through) and with a full follow-through. They measure the distance the ball travels in each case. They also try a run-up vs a standing throw. Results go on a class table: technique | contact time (short/long) | distance (m).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Follow-through extends the contact time. The force is roughly the same (the leg or arm pushes hard in both cases), but the longer time means more impulse: J = Ft. More impulse = more momentum delivered to the ball = faster ball = greater distance. The run-up builds the thrower's momentum before contact, so there is more momentum to transfer. A jembe (hoe) swing works the same way: a long, smooth swing with follow-through drives the blade deeper than a short chop. Confront the misconception that "follow-through helps because it aims the ball" — aiming is a side benefit, but the physics reason is impulse. The follow-through keeps the force applied for a longer time, increasing the total momentum transferred. Without follow-through, the contact ends too early and the ball leaves with less momentum than it could have.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *follow-through = more time = more impulse*. Homework: watch a football match and describe one player's follow-through in a kick or throw.

## Hands-On Build: Follow-Through Comparison
*Compare ball distance with and without follow-through.*

### Parts
- A football (or a rolled-up cloth ball if no football is available)
- An open outdoor area
- A measuring tape or paced steps (to estimate distance)
- A target (a wall or a goal mark)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Stand at a mark. Kick the ball with a short jab — stop your foot at the moment of contact. **Checkpoint:** the ball travels a short distance.
2. Kick the ball with a full follow-through — let your foot continue through the ball and up after contact. **Checkpoint:** the ball travels much further.
3. Measure (or pace) the distance for each kick. Record: technique | distance. **Checkpoint:** the follow-through kick should go significantly further.
4. Try a standing throw vs a throw with a 3-step run-up. Measure the distance. **Checkpoint:** the run-up throw goes further — the run-up builds the thrower's momentum.
5. Try a jembe swing: a short chop vs a long smooth swing with follow-through. Observe how deep the blade goes (in soft soil or sand). **Checkpoint:** the full swing drives deeper.

### Common Mistakes
- **Kicking too hard with no follow-through** — a hard jab may seem powerful, but the short contact time limits the impulse. A smooth kick with follow-through delivers more.
- **Confusing run-up speed with force** — the run-up builds the thrower's momentum, not the force. The force is applied during contact; the run-up ensures the thrower is already moving fast when contact begins.
- **Not measuring distance** — without a distance comparison, the effect is hard to quantify. Pace the distance or use a measuring tape.
- **Follow-through that pulls the leg across** — the follow-through should continue in the direction of the kick, not across the body. A sideways follow-through wastes the impulse.
- **Not warming up** — kicking hard without warming up can strain muscles. Start gently.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. No follow-through vs follow-through: which sends the ball further? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why does a javelin thrower take a run-up? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Write J = Ft and explain how follow-through increases it. (mirrors pre-test)
4. A footballer applies 200 N for 0.01 s (no follow-through) vs 200 N for 0.05 s (follow-through). Compute the impulse in each case.
5. Challenge: explain why a jembe with a long smooth swing cuts deeper than a short chop, using impulse.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The follow-through kick — longer contact time → more impulse (J = Ft) → more momentum → faster ball → greater distance.
2. To build speed (and thus momentum) before the throw. The run-up gives the thrower more momentum to transfer to the javelin during the release.
3. J = Ft. Follow-through increases t (contact time) while maintaining F (force), so J increases. More impulse = more momentum change = faster ball.
4. No follow-through: J = 200 × 0.01 = 2 N·s. Follow-through: J = 200 × 0.05 = 10 N·s. The follow-through delivers 5× the impulse.
5. A long smooth swing keeps the force applied for a longer time (larger t). J = Ft — more time at the same force means more impulse. More impulse drives the blade deeper into the soil. A short chop has a small t, so even a large force delivers less total impulse. The jembe's effectiveness comes from the sustained force through the swing, not just the peak force at impact.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A footballer applies 200 N for 0.01 s (no follow-through) vs 0.05 s (follow-through). Compute the impulse and the ball's speed change (m = 0.4 kg).
   **Worked Answer:** No follow-through: J = 200 × 0.01 = 2 N·s. Δv = J/m = 2/0.4 = 5 m/s. Follow-through: J = 200 × 0.05 = 10 N·s. Δv = 10/0.4 = 25 m/s. The follow-through kick gives the ball 5× the speed change (25 m/s vs 5 m/s). This is why professional footballers have such powerful shots — their technique maximises contact time, not just force.
2. **Q:** A javelin thrower (mass 80 kg) runs up at 6 m/s and releases the javelin (0.8 kg) at 28 m/s. Estimate the contact force if the release takes 0.1 s.
   **Worked Answer:** The thrower's momentum before release: p_thrower = 80 × 6 = 480 kg·m/s. The javelin's momentum after release: p_javelin = 0.8 × 28 = 22.4 kg·m/s. The impulse on the javelin: J = Δp = 22.4 N·s. The force: F = J/t = 22.4/0.1 = 224 N. Note: the thrower slows down after release (their momentum decreases by 22.4 kg·m/s → new speed = (480 − 22.4)/80 = 5.72 m/s). The run-up is crucial: without it, the thrower starts from rest and the javelin gets much less momentum.
3. **Q:** A cricket batsman "follows through" with a full swing. Explain why the ball travels further than with a defensive block, even if the peak force is the same.
   **Worked Answer:** A defensive block stops the bat at contact — short t, small J = Ft. A full swing continues through the ball — long t, large J = Ft. Even with the same peak force, the full swing delivers more impulse because the force is applied for longer. The ball leaves with more momentum (p = J) and thus more speed, travelling further. Additionally, the full swing keeps the bat moving at high speed through the contact, so the relative speed between bat and ball stays high throughout the contact, maintaining a large force for the entire duration. A defensive block has the bat decelerating at contact, so the force drops to zero quickly.

## Safety Notes
- Kick in an open area, away from windows and other people.
- Do not kick with bare feet — wear shoes.
- Warm up before kicking hard.
- The jembe swing should be in soil or sand, not toward people.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 263 introduced impulse (J = Ft = Δp). This lesson applies it to sport — a context every learner experiences.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the kick comparison and the idea that follow-through = more distance. Ages 14–17 should compute impulses, speed changes, and explain the jembe swing quantitatively.
- Local resource tips: a football is the primary tool. A rolled-up cloth ball works if no football is available. The jembe (hoe) is a familiar agricultural tool — the swing physics is the same as sport follow-through.
- This lesson bridges physics and physical education. If possible, collaborate with the PE teacher to demonstrate follow-through in football, athletics, or cricket.
