# Lesson 266: Collisions That Stick

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Make two clay lumps collide and stick together (a perfectly inelastic collision).
2. Verify that momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is NOT.
3. Calculate the final speed of the combined mass using momentum conservation.
4. Explain where the "lost" kinetic energy went (deformation, heat, sound).

## Materials
- Clay or plasticine (two lumps of different sizes, e.g., 50 g and 100 g)
- A smooth board or tabletop
- A ruler — to measure distances
- A balance or scale (if available) — to measure masses
- Chalk or tape — to mark positions
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **inelastic collision** (mgongano wa kushikamana) — a collision where objects stick together; momentum is conserved but KE is not
- **perfectly inelastic** (kushikamana kabisa) — the maximum KE loss; objects stick and move as one
- **deformation** (kubadilika umbo) — the permanent change of shape that absorbs kinetic energy
- **energy loss** (upotevu wa nishati) — the kinetic energy that is converted to heat, sound, and deformation

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Two clay lumps collide and stick. Is momentum conserved?
2. In the same collision, is kinetic energy conserved?
3. Where does the "lost" kinetic energy go?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Yes — momentum is always conserved in collisions (no external force).
2. No — kinetic energy is NOT conserved in an inelastic collision. Some KE is converted to other forms.
3. The lost KE becomes heat (from deformation of the clay), sound (the thud of impact), and permanent deformation (the clay changes shape). Energy is not destroyed; it is transformed.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate: roll one clay lump into another on the board. They stick and move together. Ask: *how fast do they move? Is it the same as before?* Board: **inelastic collision** — they stick, momentum is conserved, but KE is lost.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs run the clay collision (see Hands-On Build). They roll a clay lump (m₁) into a stationary clay lump (m₂). They stick and move together. They measure the masses and estimate the speeds (by distance travelled and time, or by comparing to the initial roll). They compute: p_before = m₁v₁, p_after = (m₁ + m₂)v_final. They check: p_before ≈ p_after. They also compute KE_before = ½m₁v₁² and KE_after = ½(m₁ + m₂)v_final² and discover that KE_after < KE_before — energy is lost. Results go on a class table: m₁ | v₁ | m₂ | v_final (predicted) | v_final (measured) | KE_before | KE_after | KE lost.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. In a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects stick together and move as one. Momentum is conserved: m₁v₁ = (m₁ + m₂)v_final → v_final = m₁v₁/(m₁ + m₂). Kinetic energy is NOT conserved: KE_before = ½m₁v₁², KE_after = ½(m₁ + m₂)v_final². The difference (KE_lost = KE_before − KE_after) goes into deformation (the clay changes shape permanently), heat (the clay warms slightly), and sound (the thud). Confront the misconception that "energy is destroyed" — energy is never destroyed; it is transformed. The total energy (KE + heat + sound + deformation) is conserved. Only the kinetic energy decreases. Also address: "if momentum is conserved, why isn't energy?" — momentum is a vector (direction matters, and external forces are zero), while KE is a scalar (always positive, and internal forces can convert it to other forms). Momentum conservation comes from Newton's third law (internal forces cancel in pairs). KE conservation requires that the internal forces do no work — but in an inelastic collision, the deformation forces do work (they change shape, which requires energy).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *stick = momentum conserved, energy lost to heat and deformation*. Homework: give one real-world example of an inelastic collision (a car crash, a bird catching a fish, a ball of ugali hitting the floor).

## Hands-On Build: Clay Collision
*Two clay lumps collide, stick, and move together — measure the outcome.*

### Parts
- Two clay lumps (plasticine) — different sizes, e.g., 50 g and 100 g
- A smooth board or tabletop
- A ruler — to measure distances
- A balance or scale (if available) — to measure masses
- Chalk or tape — to mark positions
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Weigh or estimate the masses of the two clay lumps. Record m₁ and m₂. **Checkpoint:** the masses are different — this makes the result more interesting.
2. Place m₂ stationary on the board. Mark its position. **Checkpoint:** it stays still.
3. Roll m₁ toward m₂ along a straight line. Estimate v₁ by measuring the distance and time, or by comparing to a marble rolling at a known speed. **Checkpoint:** the roll is straight and the speed is moderate.
4. The lumps collide and stick. They move together. Mark the final position. **Checkpoint:** the combined lump moves slower than m₁ was moving — energy is lost.
5. Compute v_final from momentum: v_final = m₁v₁/(m₁ + m₂). Compare with the measured v_final (from distance and time). **Checkpoint:** they should agree (within experimental error).
6. Compute KE_before = ½m₁v₁² and KE_after = ½(m₁ + m₂)v_final². Find KE_lost = KE_before − KE_after. **Checkpoint:** KE_after < KE_before — the difference is the energy lost to deformation and heat.

### Common Mistakes
- **Clay too hard** — stiff clay doesn't stick well. Soften it with a little water or warmth.
- **Collision off-centre** — if the lumps glance off each other, they won't stick properly. Align them on the centre line.
- **Not measuring masses** — without masses, you can't compute v_final or KE. Estimate by size (if same density, mass ∝ volume ∝ radius³).
- **Board not smooth** — friction slows the combined lump, making v_final appear lower than the momentum prediction. Use a smooth surface.
- **Rolling too fast** — at high speed, the clay may bounce or slide instead of sticking. Roll gently.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Two clay lumps collide and stick. Is momentum conserved? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Is KE conserved in the same collision? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Where does the lost KE go? (mirrors pre-test)
4. A 0.05 kg clay lump at 2 m/s hits a 0.10 kg stationary lump. They stick. Compute v_final.
5. Challenge: compute KE_before and KE_after for the collision in Q4. How much energy is lost?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Yes — momentum is always conserved in collisions (no external force).
2. No — KE is not conserved. Some is converted to heat, sound, and deformation.
3. Heat (from deformation of the clay), sound (the thud), and permanent deformation (the shape change). Energy is transformed, not destroyed.
4. v_final = m₁v₁/(m₁ + m₂) = 0.05 × 2/(0.05 + 0.10) = 0.10/0.15 = 0.667 m/s. The combined lump moves at 0.67 m/s.
5. KE_before = ½ × 0.05 × 2² = ½ × 0.05 × 4 = 0.10 J. KE_after = ½ × 0.15 × 0.667² = ½ × 0.15 × 0.444 = 0.033 J. KE_lost = 0.10 − 0.033 = 0.067 J. About 67% of the KE is lost — converted to heat and deformation in the clay.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A 0.05 kg lump at 2 m/s sticks to a 0.10 kg stationary lump. Compute v_final, KE_before, KE_after, and the fraction of KE lost.
   **Worked Answer:** v_final = m₁v₁/(m₁ + m₂) = 0.05 × 2/0.15 = 0.667 m/s. KE_before = ½ × 0.05 × 4 = 0.10 J. KE_after = ½ × 0.15 × 0.667² = 0.033 J. KE_lost = 0.10 − 0.033 = 0.067 J. Fraction lost: 0.067/0.10 = 67%. Two-thirds of the kinetic energy is converted to heat and deformation. This is typical of inelastic collisions — a large fraction of KE is lost.
2. **Q:** Derive the general formula for the fraction of KE lost in a perfectly inelastic collision.
   **Worked Answer:** v_final = m₁v₁/(m₁ + m₂). KE_before = ½m₁v₁². KE_after = ½(m₁ + m₂)v_final² = ½(m₁ + m₂)[m₁v₁/(m₁ + m₂)]² = ½m₁²v₁²/(m₁ + m₂). KE_lost = KE_before − KE_after = ½m₁v₁² − ½m₁²v₁²/(m₁ + m₂) = ½m₁v₁²[1 − m₁/(m₁ + m₂)] = ½m₁v₁² × m₂/(m₁ + m₂). Fraction lost: KE_lost/KE_before = m₂/(m₁ + m₂). For equal masses (m₁ = m₂): fraction = ½ = 50%. For m₂ >> m₁ (light hitting heavy): fraction → 1 (almost all KE lost). For m₂ << m₁ (heavy hitting light): fraction → 0 (little KE lost). The more equal the masses, the more energy is lost.
3. **Q:** A 1000 kg car at 15 m/s rear-ends a 2000 kg stationary lorry. They lock bumpers and move together. Compute v_final and the KE lost. Where does the energy go?
   **Worked Answer:** v_final = m₁v₁/(m₁ + m₂) = 1000 × 15/3000 = 5 m/s. KE_before = ½ × 1000 × 225 = 112,500 J. KE_after = ½ × 3000 × 25 = 37,500 J. KE_lost = 112,500 − 37,500 = 75,000 J. Fraction lost: 75,000/112,500 = 67% (= m₂/(m₁ + m₂) = 2000/3000 = 0.667). The energy goes into: crumpling the car's front end (deformation), heat from the metal deformation, sound (the crash), and breaking parts. 75 kJ is enough to raise the temperature of 1 kg of steel by about 160°C (75,000/(450 × 1) ≈ 167°C, where 450 J/kg·°C is the specific heat of steel). The crumple zone is designed to absorb this energy — without it, the energy would go into the passengers (a far worse outcome).

## Safety Notes
- Clay is safe but can stain clothes — use plasticine or wear aprons.
- Do not throw clay lumps — roll them gently.
- Clean the board after the activity — clay residue is slippery.
- Do not eat the clay.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 265 covered elastic collisions (bounce, KE conserved). This lesson covers inelastic collisions (stick, KE lost). The contrast is the key takeaway.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the clay collision and the idea that sticking loses energy. Ages 14–17 should derive v_final, compute KE loss, and derive the general formula for the fraction lost.
- Local resource tips: clay or plasticine is available in most schools. A tabletop is the board. If no scale is available, estimate masses by size (same density → mass ∝ radius³).
- The car-lorry collision in the advanced questions is a real-world application that connects to Lesson 271 (crumple zones) and Lesson 272 (boda safety campaign).
