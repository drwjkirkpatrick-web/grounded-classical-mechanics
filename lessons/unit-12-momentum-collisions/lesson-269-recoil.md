# Lesson 269: Recoil: Stepping Off the Dugout

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate recoil by stepping off a low-friction plank (on rollers or cylinders) and watching the plank slide back.
2. Explain recoil as conservation of momentum: the person's forward momentum equals the plank's backward momentum.
3. Compute the recoil speed given masses and the step-off speed.
4. Relate recoil to real-world examples: canoe push-offs, firefighter hose kickback, gun recoil.

## Materials
- A wooden plank or long board (1–2 m)
- Cylindrical rollers (pens, pencils, or bottle caps placed under the plank to reduce friction)
- A smooth floor
- A measuring tape or ruler — to measure distances
- A stopwatch (phone) — optional
- A balance or scale (if available) — to measure masses
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **recoil** (kurudi nyuma) — the backward motion of a body when it ejects or pushes off mass forward
- **step-off** (kushuka) — the act of stepping forward off a free-moving platform; the platform recoils backward
- **reaction momentum** (mumbo wa kukabiliana) — the backward momentum equal and opposite to the forward momentum
- **kickback** (kuruka nyuma) — the backward push felt when ejecting mass (a hose, a gun, a fire extinguisher)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A person steps forward off a plank on rollers. Which way does the plank move?
2. If the person is heavier than the plank, which moves faster?
3. Write the recoil formula from conservation of momentum.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Backward — the person's forward momentum is balanced by the plank's backward momentum (conservation of momentum, starting from rest).
2. The plank moves faster — m_person × v_person = m_plank × v_plank. If m_person > m_plank, then v_plank > v_person (the lighter object moves faster for the same momentum).
3. From rest: 0 = m_person × v_person + m_plank × v_plank → v_plank = −(m_person × v_person) / m_plank. The negative sign means the plank moves backward.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate: a volunteer stands on a plank on rollers. They step forward off the plank. The plank shoots backward. Ask: *why did the plank go backward?* Board: **recoil** — conservation of momentum. The person goes forward, the plank goes backward.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs take turns stepping off the plank (see Hands-On Build). They measure the distance the plank travels backward and the distance the person travels forward (in the same time). They check: m_person × d_person ≈ m_plank × d_plank (momentum conservation, since d = vt and t is the same). They try: (a) a heavy person vs a light person (the plank recoils faster with a heavy person), (b) stepping gently vs stepping hard (harder step = more forward speed = more recoil). Results go on a class table: m_person | m_plank | d_person | d_plank | m × d (person) | m × d (plank).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Recoil is conservation of momentum in a "push-off" scenario. Starting from rest: 0 = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ → m₁v₁ = −m₂v₂. The person's forward momentum equals the plank's backward momentum. The lighter object moves faster (v_plank/v_person = m_person/m_plank). Real-world examples: (a) stepping off a canoe — the canoe pushes backward (you may fall in if you don't account for it!), (b) a firefighter's hose — the water shoots forward, the hose kicks back (the firefighter must brace), (c) a gun — the bullet goes forward, the gun recoils backward (Lesson 267's cannon problem). Confront the misconception that "the person pushes on the plank, so the plank goes back" — this is true, but the deeper reason is that the push is an internal force. The person and the plank are a system. The internal forces (foot pushing the plank, plank pushing the foot) are equal and opposite (Newton's third law). The total momentum stays zero. The person goes forward, the plank goes backward, and the momenta cancel. Also address: "the plank goes back because the person pushed it" — yes, but the key insight is that the person ALSO gets pushed forward by the plank. Both move because of the action-reaction pair. The heavier person gives the plank more momentum (more mass at the same speed), so the plank recoils faster.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *step forward, plank goes back — equal and opposite momentum*. Homework: explain why a canoe moves backward when you step off it onto a dock.

## Hands-On Build: Step-Off Recoil Plank
*A plank on rollers — step off and watch it recoil.*

### Parts
- A wooden plank or long board (1–2 m, sturdy enough to stand on)
- Cylindrical rollers (pens, pencils, or bottle caps placed under the plank)
- A smooth floor (a hallway or a cleared classroom)
- A measuring tape or ruler — to measure distances
- A balance or scale (if available) — to measure masses
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Place the rollers under the plank on the smooth floor. Test that the plank slides freely. **Checkpoint:** the plank moves easily when pushed — the rollers work.
2. A volunteer stands on the plank (both at rest). Mark the starting position. **Checkpoint:** the plank is stationary; the volunteer is balanced.
3. The volunteer steps forward off the plank (one smooth step). **Checkpoint:** the plank shoots backward. The volunteer lands on the floor ahead.
4. Mark the final positions: where the volunteer landed, where the plank ended up. Measure d_person (forward) and d_plank (backward). **Checkpoint:** the plank travels backward a noticeable distance.
5. Weigh or estimate the masses. Check: m_person × d_person ≈ m_plank × d_plank. **Checkpoint:** the products should be approximately equal (within 20% error from friction).
6. Try different volunteers (different masses) and different step speeds. Record the results. **Checkpoint:** heavier person or faster step = more plank recoil.

### Common Mistakes
- **Plank not on rollers** — without rollers, friction is too high and the plank barely moves. Use pens or pencils as rollers.
- **Stepping too cautiously** — a small, hesitant step doesn't generate enough momentum. Take a smooth, deliberate step.
- **Floor not smooth** — rough or sticky floors prevent the plank from sliding. Use a smooth hallway or polished floor.
- **Plank too heavy** — a very heavy plank barely moves. Use a light board (plywood or a shelf).
- **Not marking positions** — without marks, you can't measure distances. Use tape or chalk.
- **Safety: falling** — the volunteer may stumble when the plank moves. Use a low plank (close to the ground) and have a spotter.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A person steps forward off a plank on rollers. Which way does the plank move? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If the person is heavier, which moves faster? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Write the recoil formula. (mirrors pre-test)
4. A 50 kg person steps off a 5 kg plank at 1 m/s. Find the plank's recoil speed.
5. Challenge: a firefighter holds a hose that ejects 2 kg/s of water at 15 m/s. Find the kickback force on the firefighter.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Backward — the person's forward momentum is balanced by the plank's backward momentum (conservation of momentum).
2. The plank moves faster — m_person × v_person = m_plank × v_plank. If m_person > m_plank, then v_plank > v_person.
3. 0 = m_person × v_person + m_plank × v_plank → v_plank = −(m_person × v_person) / m_plank.
4. v_plank = −(50 × 1) / 5 = −10 m/s. The plank recoils at 10 m/s backward. (It is 10× lighter, so it moves 10× faster.)
5. The hose ejects 2 kg/s at 15 m/s. The momentum flow rate (force) = ṁ × v = 2 × 15 = 30 N. The firefighter feels a 30 N kickback force (about 3 kg of weight pushing back). The firefighter must brace against this continuous force.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A 50 kg person steps off a 5 kg plank at 1 m/s. Find the plank's recoil speed and the total KE. Where does the KE come from?
   **Worked Answer:** v_plank = −(50 × 1)/5 = −10 m/s. KE_person = ½ × 50 × 1 = 25 J. KE_plank = ½ × 5 × 100 = 250 J. Total KE = 275 J. The energy comes from the person's muscles (chemical → mechanical). The plank gets 10× more KE than the person (250 J vs 25 J) because, for equal and opposite momenta, the lighter object has more KE (KE = p²/2m; the plank is 10× lighter, so it gets 10× the KE). The person does work pushing off the plank; most of that work goes into the plank's KE.
2. **Q:** A firefighter's hose ejects 2 kg/s of water at 15 m/s. Find the kickback force. Then compare with a fire extinguisher that ejects 0.5 kg/s at 30 m/s.
   **Worked Answer:** Hose: F = ṁ × v = 2 × 15 = 30 N. Extinguisher: F = 0.5 × 30 = 15 N. The hose has a larger kickback (30 N vs 15 N) because it ejects more mass per second, even though the extinguisher's flow is faster. The force depends on the product ṁ × v (momentum flow rate), not just the speed. Note: the fire extinguisher ejects gas (CO₂), not water — the mass flow is lower but the speed is higher. The net force is still significant (15 N ≈ 1.5 kg of push).
3. **Q:** A 70 kg canoeist steps off a 30 kg canoe onto a dock at 2 m/s. Find the canoe's recoil speed. Why is it dangerous to step off a canoe?
   **Worked Answer:** v_canoe = −(70 × 2)/30 = −4.67 m/s. The canoe recoils at 4.67 m/s backward. This is fast — the canoe shoots away from the dock as the canoeist steps forward. If the canoeist doesn't account for this, they may misjudge the step and fall into the water. The danger is that the canoe moves AWAY from the dock as you step TOWARD the dock — the gap widens. This is why you should step to the centre of the canoe first (to minimise rocking) and then step onto the dock quickly, keeping your weight low. The heavier the canoe (or the slower the step), the less the recoil — but a light canoe at a fast step is a recipe for a swim.

## Safety Notes
- Use a low plank (close to the ground) to minimise fall risk.
- Have a spotter stand beside the volunteer.
- Clear the area of obstacles — the plank may shoot back several metres.
- Do not use a high or narrow plank — it may tip.
- Ensure the floor is smooth but not slippery for walking (the volunteer needs traction to step).

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 267–268 covered conservation of momentum and internal forces. This lesson applies the same principle to recoil — a one-directional "explosion" where one object pushes off another.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the step-off demo and the idea that "person goes forward, plank goes backward." Ages 14–17 should compute recoil speeds, KE distribution, and the hose kickback force.
- Local resource tips: a wooden plank or plywood board is the platform. Pens or pencils are the rollers. A smooth hallway or cleared classroom is the space. The canoe context is familiar to anyone who has been on Lake Victoria or a river.
- The firefighter hose and fire extinguisher examples introduce the concept of momentum flow rate (F = ṁ × v), which is the basis for rocket thrust (Lesson 270).
