# Lesson 270: Rockets Are Momentum Machines

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Revisit the Unit 4 water rocket and explain its propulsion using momentum conservation.
2. Describe how mass ejected backward (water + air) produces forward momentum: p_rocket = −p_ejected.
3. Explain why a water rocket with water outperforms one with air only (more ejected mass = more momentum).
4. Relate multi-stage rockets to momentum: staged ejection keeps the remaining mass light, so each stage adds more speed.

## Materials
- A plastic water bottle (500 ml or 1 L)
- A cork or stopper that fits the bottle mouth
- A valve (a football inflation needle or a small nozzle)
- A pump (a bicycle pump or a hand pump)
- Water
- An open outdoor area
- A protractor or clinometer — to measure launch angle (optional)
- A stopwatch (phone) — to measure flight time
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **rocket propulsion** (mwendo wa roketi) — mass ejected backward produces forward momentum; no external medium needed
- **ejected mass** (uzito uliotupwa) — the water and air that leave the rocket backward
- **thrust** (sukuma) — the forward force on the rocket from the ejected mass; F = ṁ × v_exit
- **staging** (hatua) — ejecting empty fuel tanks to reduce the remaining mass; each stage adds more speed

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A water rocket ejects water backward. Which way does the rocket move?
2. Why does a water rocket with water fly higher than one with air only?
3. Why do real rockets use multiple stages?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Forward — the ejected water has backward momentum, so the rocket gets equal and opposite forward momentum (conservation of momentum).
2. Water is much denser than air. More ejected mass means more momentum (p = mv) transferred to the rocket, giving it more speed and height. Air-only rockets eject very little mass, so the rocket gets very little momentum.
3. To shed empty fuel tanks. Each stage's empty mass is dead weight — ejecting it reduces the remaining mass, so the next stage's thrust produces more acceleration (a = F/m). Staging means each stage adds more speed than a single large motor could.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — launch a water rocket (or recall the Unit 4 launch). The rocket shoots up. Ask: *what pushed it up?* Board: **momentum** — water goes down, rocket goes up. No external air to push against — it's all internal.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs launch water rockets with different water levels (see Hands-On Build). They try: (a) no water (air only), (b) ¼ full of water, (c) ½ full, (d) ¾ full. They measure the flight time or height for each. They discover that there is an optimal water level (typically ⅓ to ½ full) — too little water gives little mass to eject; too much water makes the rocket too heavy to lift. Results go on a class table: water level | flight time | height estimate | observation.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. A rocket is a momentum machine: it ejects mass (water, air, or hot gas) backward, and by conservation of momentum, the rocket gains forward momentum. p_rocket = −p_ejected. The thrust force is F = ṁ × v_exit (mass flow rate × exit speed). Water rockets outperform air-only rockets because water is ~800× denser than air — the same volume of water has 800× more mass, so ejecting it gives 800× more momentum. Staging: a multi-stage rocket ejects the empty first stage (dead mass), so the remaining rocket is lighter. The second stage's thrust now acts on a smaller mass, producing more acceleration (a = F/m). Each stage adds more speed than a single motor could, because the mass being accelerated decreases. Confront the misconception that "rockets push against the air" — they do not. Rockets work in the vacuum of space. The propulsion is entirely internal: the rocket pushes the exhaust out, and the exhaust (by Newton's third law) pushes the rocket forward. The air around the rocket is irrelevant (and actually causes drag, which slows the rocket). Also address: "more water is always better" — no, there is an optimum. Too much water makes the rocket too heavy (the thrust must lift the water as well as the rocket). The optimum is typically ⅓ to ½ full: enough mass to eject for good momentum, but not so much that the rocket is too heavy to accelerate.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *rockets are momentum machines — eject mass backward, go forward*. Homework: explain in one sentence why a rocket works in space but a propeller-driven plane does not.

## Hands-On Build: Water Rocket Launch
*Launch water rockets with different water levels — find the optimum.*

### Parts
- A plastic water bottle (500 ml or 1 L)
- A cork or stopper that fits the bottle mouth
- A football inflation needle (pierced through the cork)
- A bicycle pump or hand pump
- Water
- An open outdoor area (away from buildings and people)
- A stopwatch (phone) — to measure flight time
- A protractor or clinometer — to measure launch angle (optional)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Fill the bottle to the desired water level (start with ⅓ full). Insert the cork with the inflation needle into the bottle mouth. **Checkpoint:** the cork fits tightly — no air leaks.
2. Connect the pump to the inflation needle. **Checkpoint:** the pump is ready to pressurise.
3. Go to the outdoor area. Invert the bottle (neck down). Hold it at a slight angle from vertical (or straight up for maximum height). **Checkpoint:** the launch area is clear of people and obstacles.
4. Pump air into the bottle. When the pressure exceeds the cork's grip, the cork pops and the water shoots out. **Checkpoint:** the rocket launches upward. The water ejects downward (backward), and the rocket goes upward (forward).
5. Measure the flight time (from launch to landing). Estimate the height: h ≈ ½g(t/2)² = ½ × 9.8 × (t/2)², where t is the total flight time and t/2 is the time to apex. **Checkpoint:** record the flight time and height estimate.
6. Repeat with different water levels: no water, ¼, ½, ¾. Record the results. **Checkpoint:** the optimum is typically ⅓ to ½ full. Too little water = little ejected mass = low momentum. Too much water = heavy rocket = low acceleration.

### Common Mistakes
- **Cork too loose** — the cork pops before enough pressure builds. Push it in firmly (but not so tight it never pops).
- **Cork too tight** — the bottle may burst before the cork pops. Do not over-pump. Stop at ~5–6 pumps for a 500 ml bottle.
- **Launching toward people or buildings** — always launch in an open area, away from people, windows, and roofs.
- **Not inverting the bottle** — the water must be at the neck (bottom) so it ejects downward when the cork pops. If the bottle is upright, the air escapes first and the water stays.
- **Too much water** — a full bottle is too heavy. The optimum is ⅓ to ½ full.
- **Not measuring flight time** — without a stopwatch, you can't compare heights. Use the phone's stopwatch.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A water rocket ejects water backward. Which way does the rocket move? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why does water outperform air only? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why do real rockets use multiple stages? (mirrors pre-test)
4. A rocket ejects 0.5 kg of gas at 40 m/s. The rocket's remaining mass is 2 kg. Find the rocket's speed gain.
5. Challenge: explain why a rocket works in space but a propeller plane does not.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Forward — the ejected water has backward momentum, so the rocket gets equal and opposite forward momentum (conservation of momentum).
2. Water is ~800× denser than air. More ejected mass means more momentum transferred to the rocket (p = mv), giving it more speed and height.
3. To shed empty fuel tanks. Each stage's empty mass is dead weight — ejecting it reduces the remaining mass, so the next stage's thrust produces more acceleration (a = F/m). Each stage adds more speed.
4. Momentum conservation: m_eject × v_eject = m_rocket × v_rocket → 0.5 × 40 = 2 × v_rocket → v_rocket = 20/2 = 10 m/s. The rocket gains 10 m/s.
5. A rocket ejects mass (exhaust) backward — the propulsion is internal (Newton's third law between rocket and exhaust). No external medium is needed, so it works in the vacuum of space. A propeller plane pushes air backward with its propeller — it needs external air to push against. In space (no air), the propeller has nothing to push, so the plane cannot move. The rocket carries its own "reaction mass" (fuel); the plane uses the surrounding air.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A rocket ejects 0.5 kg of gas at 40 m/s. The remaining mass is 2 kg. Find the speed gain and the total KE. Where does the KE come from?
   **Worked Answer:** v_rocket = m_eject × v_eject / m_rocket = 0.5 × 40 / 2 = 10 m/s. KE_rocket = ½ × 2 × 100 = 100 J. KE_eject = ½ × 0.5 × 1600 = 400 J. Total KE = 500 J. The energy comes from the chemical energy of the fuel (combustion → thermal → mechanical). The exhaust gets 4× more KE than the rocket (400 J vs 100 J) because, for equal and opposite momenta, the lighter object gets more KE (KE = p²/2m). The exhaust is 4× lighter, so it gets 4× the KE.
2. **Q:** A two-stage rocket: Stage 1 ejects 100 kg at 50 m/s (remaining mass 900 kg). Then Stage 2 ejects 100 kg at 50 m/s (remaining mass 800 kg). Compute the speed after each stage and compare with a single ejection of 200 kg at 50 m/s (remaining mass 800 kg).
   **Worked Answer:** Stage 1: Δv₁ = 100 × 50 / 900 = 5.56 m/s. Stage 2: Δv₂ = 100 × 50 / 800 = 6.25 m/s. Total: Δv = 5.56 + 6.25 = 11.81 m/s. Single ejection: Δv = 200 × 50 / 800 = 12.5 m/s. Wait — the single ejection gives MORE speed (12.5 vs 11.81)? That seems wrong! The difference is that in the single ejection, all 200 kg is ejected at 50 m/s relative to the initial frame. In the staged case, Stage 2's ejection is at 50 m/s relative to the already-moving rocket, so the total is less than the single case. But this simplified calculation ignores the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation. The correct treatment: Δv = v_exit × ln(m_initial/m_final). Staged: Δv = 50 × ln(1000/900) + 50 × ln(900/800) = 50 × (0.1054 + 0.1178) = 50 × 0.2232 = 11.16 m/s. Single: Δv = 50 × ln(1000/800) = 50 × 0.2231 = 11.16 m/s. They are the same! Staging doesn't help if the exit speed is the same — it helps when you shed empty tank mass (so the remaining mass is smaller, giving a larger ln ratio per stage). The advantage of staging is ejecting dead mass (empty tanks), not the staging itself.
3. **Q:** A water rocket (0.3 kg empty, 0.3 kg water) ejects all its water at 20 m/s. Find the rocket's speed gain. Then compute the height (ignore air resistance).
   **Worked Answer:** The rocket equation: Δv = v_exit × ln(m_initial/m_final) = 20 × ln(0.6/0.3) = 20 × ln(2) = 20 × 0.693 = 13.86 m/s. (Using the simple momentum method: Δv = m_water × v_exit / m_rocket = 0.3 × 20 / 0.3 = 20 m/s — this overestimates because the water doesn't all exit at once; the rocket equation is correct.) Height: h = v²/(2g) = 13.86²/(2 × 9.8) = 192.1/19.6 = 9.8 m. The water rocket reaches about 10 m — consistent with a good school water rocket launch.

## Safety Notes
- Launch in an open outdoor area, away from people, buildings, windows, and power lines.
- Do not over-pump — stop at 5–6 pumps for a 500 ml bottle. The bottle can burst if over-pressurised.
- Wear eye protection if available.
- Do not point the rocket at anyone.
- The bottle may land hard — do not retrieve it until it has stopped moving.
- The teacher should supervise all launches.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Unit 4 introduced forces and Newton's laws (including the water rocket as a demo). Lessons 267–269 covered conservation of momentum, internal forces, and recoil. This lesson unifies those concepts: a rocket is a continuous recoil machine.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the water rocket launch and the idea that "water goes down, rocket goes up." Ages 14–17 should compute speed gains, apply the rocket equation, and explain staging.
- Local resource tips: a plastic water bottle, a cork, and a bicycle pump are all readily available. A football inflation needle (pierced through the cork) connects the pump to the bottle. The launch area should be an open field or playground.
- The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation (Δv = v_exit × ln(m_initial/m_final)) is introduced for ages 14–17 as the correct treatment of continuous mass ejection. The simple momentum method (Δv = m_eject × v_exit / m_rocket) is an approximation that works for small mass ejections but overestimates for large ones. The rocket equation is the exact solution.
- This lesson connects to Lesson 268 (balloon boat) — the balloon boat is a low-speed, water-based analogue of the rocket. Both demonstrate internal-force propulsion.
