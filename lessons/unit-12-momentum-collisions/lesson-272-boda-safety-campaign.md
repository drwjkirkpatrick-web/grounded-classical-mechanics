# Lesson 272: Project: Boda Safety Physics Campaign

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** safety-poster

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Turn impulse, braking, and momentum data into clear safety messages for the local boda stage.
2. Design a poster that communicates one physics-based safety argument (e.g., "Double the speed = 4× the braking distance").
3. Peer-test the messages: do other learners understand the physics and the safety point?
4. Present the poster and explain the physics in one minute.

## Materials
- A3 paper or poster board (or large sheets of newsprint)
- Markers, coloured pencils, or paint
- Rulers — for drawing graphs and diagrams
- Reference data (from Lessons 262, 263, 271): braking distance ratios, force comparisons, crumple zone data
- Tape or pins — to display the posters
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **safety campaign** (kampeni ya usalama) — a structured effort to communicate safety messages to a target audience
- **boda stage** (stesheni ya boda) — the local motorcycle taxi stand where riders gather and passengers board
- **physics-based message** (ujumbe wa fizikia) — a safety argument grounded in momentum and impulse data, not just rules
- **peer test** (jaribio la wenzako) — showing the poster to other learners to check if the message is clear

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. At the same speed, how many times more braking distance does a loaded boda (290 kg) need vs an empty one (150 kg)?
2. If you double the speed, by what factor does braking distance increase?
3. A boda passenger hits the ground at 12 m/s without a helmet. Estimate the force on the head (5 kg, collision time 0.001 s).

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. 290/150 = 1.93 ≈ 2× the braking distance. The loaded boda needs about twice the braking distance of the empty one.
2. 4× — braking distance is proportional to v² (since KE = ½mv² and the brakes remove energy). Doubling the speed quadruples the braking distance.
3. Δp = 5 × 12 = 60 kg·m/s. F = Δp/t = 60/0.001 = 60,000 N. Without a helmet, the force on the head is 60,000 N — about 120× the head's weight (49 N). This is lethal. A helmet extends the collision time to ~0.006 s, reducing the force to 10,000 N — still severe but survivable.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the key data from the unit: (a) loaded boda = 2× braking distance, (b) double speed = 4× braking distance, (c) boda passenger force = 33× car passenger force at the same speed, (d) helmet reduces force by 30×. Ask: *if you could put one of these facts on a poster at the boda stage, which would save the most lives?* Board: **Boda Safety Physics Campaign**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs or small groups design a safety poster (see Hands-On Build). Each group picks one physics-based safety message and turns it into a poster. The poster must include: (a) a clear headline (e.g., "Double the Speed = 4× the Stopping Distance"), (b) a simple diagram or graph (e.g., a bar chart of braking distance vs speed), (c) one sentence of physics explanation, (d) a safety action (e.g., "Slow down at the corner"). They use data from the unit's lessons.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — each group presents their poster in one minute. The class peer-tests: does the message make sense? Is the physics correct? Is the safety action clear? The teacher consolidates the key messages: (1) Speed is the dominant factor — braking distance grows with v². (2) Overloading doubles the braking distance. (3) Helmets reduce the force by 30×. (4) A boda crash is 33× more forceful than a car crash at the same speed. Confront the misconception that "safety is just about following rules" — rules are important, but understanding the physics (WHY speed matters, WHY helmets work) makes the rules more convincing. A rider who understands that doubling speed quadruples braking distance is more likely to slow down than one who is just told "don't speed."
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *physics-based safety messages save lives*. Homework: display the poster at home or at a local shop and ask one person if they understand the message.

## Hands-On Build: Boda Safety Poster
*Design a physics-based safety poster for the local boda stage.*

### Parts
- A3 paper or poster board (or large sheets of newsprint)
- Markers, coloured pencils, or paint
- Rulers — for drawing graphs and diagrams
- Reference data (from Lessons 262, 263, 271):
  - Loaded boda (290 kg) vs empty (150 kg): 2× braking distance
  - Double speed: 4× braking distance (d ∝ v²)
  - Boda passenger force vs car passenger force: 33× at the same speed
  - Helmet force reduction: 30×
- Tape or pins — to display the posters
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Choose one physics-based safety message. Options:
   - "Double the Speed = 4× the Stopping Distance" (Lesson 262)
   - "Overloaded Boda = 2× the Stopping Distance" (Lesson 262)
   - "A Helmet Reduces the Force by 30×" (Lesson 271)
   - "A Boda Crash is 33× More Forceful Than a Car Crash" (Lesson 271)
   - "Bend Your Knees When You Land" (Lesson 263)
   **Checkpoint:** the message is specific and physics-based.
2. Design the poster: (a) a clear headline in large letters, (b) a simple diagram or bar chart (e.g., braking distance vs speed), (c) one sentence of physics explanation, (d) a safety action. **Checkpoint:** the poster is clear and readable from 2 m away.
3. Use data from the unit's lessons to support the message. Include numbers (e.g., "290 kg loaded boda at 15 m/s: braking distance = 2× empty boda"). **Checkpoint:** the numbers are correct and cited from the lesson.
4. Present the poster to the class in one minute. Explain the physics and the safety action. **Checkpoint:** the class understands the message.
5. Peer test: other learners give feedback — is the message clear? Is the physics correct? Is the safety action practical? **Checkpoint:** at least 3 peers can explain the message back.
6. Display the posters in the classroom or (with permission) at the school gate or a local shop. **Checkpoint:** the posters are visible to the school community.

### Common Mistakes
- **Too much text** — a poster with paragraphs of text is not read. Keep it to a headline, a diagram, and one sentence.
- **No diagram** — a poster with only text is less effective. Include a simple bar chart or diagram.
- **Wrong numbers** — check the data against the lesson. If the numbers are wrong, the message loses credibility.
- **No safety action** — a poster that says "speed is dangerous" without saying "slow down at the corner" is incomplete. Always include a specific action.
- **Not peer-testing** — without peer feedback, you don't know if the message is clear. Always show it to at least 3 classmates.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Loaded vs empty boda: braking distance ratio? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Double speed: braking distance factor? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Boda passenger without helmet: force estimate? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Name one physics-based safety message from your poster and explain the physics behind it.
5. Challenge: why is a physics-based safety message more effective than just saying "be careful"?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 290/150 ≈ 2× the braking distance. The loaded boda needs about twice the braking distance.
2. 4× — braking distance is proportional to v². Doubling the speed quadruples the braking distance.
3. F = Δp/t = (5 × 12)/0.001 = 60,000 N. Without a helmet, the force is 60,000 N — lethal.
4. (Learner's own answer — check that the physics is correct and the safety action is specific. Example: "A Helmet Reduces the Force by 30× — the foam extends the collision time from 0.001 s to 0.006 s, reducing the force by 30× (F = Δp/t). Safety action: always wear a helmet.")
5. A physics-based message explains WHY, not just WHAT. A rider who understands that "double speed = 4× braking distance" knows the consequence of speeding and is more likely to slow down. "Be careful" gives no reason and is easily ignored. Physics provides the evidence — and evidence is more persuasive than rules alone.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Design a poster with the message "Double the Speed = 4× the Stopping Distance." Include a bar chart of braking distance vs speed (10, 15, 20, 25, 30 m/s) for a 150 kg boda with a braking force of 3000 N.
   **Worked Answer:** Braking distance: d = ½mv²/F = ½ × 150 × v²/3000 = 0.025v². At 10 m/s: d = 2.5 m. At 15: d = 5.6 m. At 20: d = 10 m. At 25: d = 15.6 m. At 30: d = 22.5 m. The bar chart shows a quadratic curve — the bars grow much faster than linear. The poster headline: "Double the Speed (15→30 m/s) = 4× the Stopping Distance (5.6→22.5 m)." The physics: d ∝ v² because the brakes must dissipate KE = ½mv². The safety action: "Observe the speed limit — every extra km/h costs metres of braking distance."
2. **Q:** Design a poster comparing the force on a boda passenger vs a car passenger at 12 m/s. Include the numbers.
   **Worked Answer:** Boda (no crumple zone, t = 0.003 s): F = 60 × 12/0.003 = 240,000 N. Car (crumple zone, t = 0.1 s): F = 60 × 12/0.1 = 7200 N. Ratio: 240,000/7200 = 33. The poster headline: "A Boda Crash is 33× More Forceful Than a Car Crash at the Same Speed." The bar chart: two bars — boda (240 kN) and car (7.2 kN) — the boda bar is 33× taller. The physics: the car's crumple zone extends the collision time by 33× (0.1/0.003 = 33), reducing the force by 33×. The boda has no crumple zone, no seatbelt, and no airbag. The safety action: "Wear a helmet — it's your crumple zone."
3. **Q:** Design a poster with the message "A Helmet is Your Crumple Zone." Include the force reduction data.
   **Worked Answer:** Without helmet (t = 0.001 s): F = 5 × 12/0.001 = 60,000 N. With helmet (t = 0.006 s): F = 5 × 12/0.006 = 10,000 N. Reduction: 60,000/10,000 = 6× (note: in Lesson 271, the reduction was 30× for a 5 m/s impact with 1.5 cm foam compression; at 12 m/s, the foam compresses more but the force is still higher — the reduction depends on the impact speed and foam thickness). The poster headline: "A Helmet is Your Crumple Zone — It Reduces the Force by 6–30×." The diagram: a cross-section of a helmet showing the hard shell (spreads force) and foam liner (extends time). The physics: the foam compresses, extending the collision time (t), reducing the force (F = Δp/t). The safety action: "Always wear a helmet — it's the only crumple zone you have."

## Safety Notes
- The poster content is itself a safety lesson — reinforce the physics behind the safety rules.
- Do not display posters on roads or in dangerous locations — display them in the school, at the gate, or at a local shop (with permission).
- Use non-toxic markers and paints.
- Do not stand on chairs to display posters — use a step stool or ask the teacher.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 262 (braking distance), 263 (impulse), and 271 (crumple zones) provide the data for the posters. This lesson synthesises the unit's safety content into a public communication project.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on designing a clear poster with one message and a simple diagram. Ages 14–17 should compute the data, draw bar charts, and present the physics quantitatively.
- Local resource tips: A3 paper or newsprint, markers or coloured pencils. The boda stage is the local context — every learner has seen a boda-boda and knows the safety risks. The posters can be displayed at the school gate (with permission).
- This is a project lesson (project: safety-poster). The deliverable is a poster that communicates a physics-based safety message. The peer test ensures the message is clear. The presentation builds confidence in explaining physics to a non-technical audience.
- If possible, invite a local boda rider or a road safety officer to view the posters and give feedback. This connects the classroom to the community.
