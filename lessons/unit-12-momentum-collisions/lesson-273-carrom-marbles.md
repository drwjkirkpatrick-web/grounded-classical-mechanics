# Lesson 273: Momentum Games: Carrom and Marbles Tourney

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Set up a carrom break or a marbles course and predict the rebound angles.
2. Observe speed transfer in elastic collisions (the striker hits a carrom piece and stops; the piece shoots forward).
3. Explain the line-of-centres rule: the rebound direction is along the line connecting the centres at contact.
4. Run a scored mini-tournament and tally the results.

## Materials
- A carrom board (or a smooth tabletop with a chalk circle)
- Carrom pieces (or bottle tops, or marbles) — at least 10
- A striker (a heavier piece or a large marble)
- A ruler — to measure distances and angles
- Chalk or tape — to mark positions and the circle
- A scoreboard (a notebook or a whiteboard)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **carrom break** (kuvunja karomu) — the opening shot that scatters the pieces from the centre circle
- **line of centres** (mstari wa vituo) — the line connecting the centres of two colliding objects; the rebound direction
- **speed transfer** (hamisha mwendo) — in an elastic collision, the striker's speed is transferred to the target piece
- **glancing collision** (mgongano mwendokwavu) — an off-centre hit where the objects deflect at angles

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A striker hits a carrom piece head-on. What happens to the striker?
2. A striker hits a piece off-centre. Which way does the piece go?
3. What is the "line of centres"?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The striker stops, and the piece moves forward at the striker's speed (the equal-mass elastic swap — Lesson 265).
2. The piece moves along the line of centres — the line connecting the centres of the striker and the piece at the moment of contact. If the hit is off-centre, the piece goes at an angle.
3. The line connecting the centres of the two colliding objects at the moment of contact. The rebound direction is along this line.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — demonstrate a carrom break: the striker hits the cluster of pieces in the centre, and they scatter. Ask: *can you predict where each piece will go?* Board: **line of centres** — the rebound direction is along the line connecting the centres at contact.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs set up a carrom break or a marbles course (see Hands-On Build). They place pieces in a circle, flick the striker, and predict where each piece will go based on the line of centres. They test: (a) head-on hits (the striker stops, the piece goes forward), (b) glancing hits (the piece goes at an angle, the striker continues at a different angle), (c) multiple collisions (pieces bounce off each other and the walls). They score points for correct predictions and for potting pieces. Results go on a class scoreboard.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. In a head-on elastic collision (equal masses), the striker stops and the target takes the speed (the swap — Lesson 265). In a glancing collision, the target moves along the line of centres, and the striker moves perpendicular to it (for equal masses, the two directions are at 90° to each other — a consequence of momentum and KE conservation). The line of centres rule lets you predict the rebound direction: draw a line from the striker's centre to the target's centre at contact — the target goes along that line. Confront the misconception that "the piece goes in the direction the striker was moving" — only for a head-on hit. For a glancing hit, the piece goes along the line of centres, which is different from the striker's direction. Also address: "the striker always stops" — only for equal-mass head-on collisions. For glancing hits, the striker continues (slower and at an angle). For unequal masses (heavy striker, light piece), the striker continues forward even on a head-on hit.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the line of centres predicts the rebound — aim for the centre of the target*. Homework: play one game of carrom or marbles and describe one collision using the line of centres.

## Hands-On Build: Carrom Break and Marbles Tourney
*Set up a carrom break, predict rebounds, and run a scored tournament.*

### Parts
- A carrom board (or a smooth tabletop with a chalk circle ~30 cm diameter)
- Carrom pieces (or bottle tops, or marbles) — at least 10, same size
- A striker (a heavier piece or a large marble)
- A ruler — to measure distances and angles
- Chalk or tape — to mark positions and the circle
- A scoreboard (a notebook or a whiteboard)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Set up the break: place 5–10 pieces in a circle in the centre of the board. Mark the circle with chalk. **Checkpoint:** the pieces are arranged in a cluster — the break will scatter them.
2. Place the striker at the edge. Flick it toward the cluster. Before flicking, predict where 2–3 pieces will go based on the line of centres. **Checkpoint:** the prediction is made before the shot.
3. Flick the striker. Observe where the pieces go. Score 1 point for each correct prediction. **Checkpoint:** head-on hits stop the striker; glancing hits send pieces at angles.
4. Try a glancing hit: aim the striker at the edge of a piece (off-centre). Predict the angle. **Checkpoint:** the piece goes along the line of centres, not along the striker's original direction.
5. Try a head-on hit: aim the striker directly at the centre of a piece. **Checkpoint:** the striker stops, the piece goes forward at the striker's speed (the swap).
6. Run a mini-tournament: each pair takes 5 shots. Score 1 point per correct prediction + 1 point per piece potted (knocked into a corner pocket or off the board). Tally the scores. **Checkpoint:** the scores are recorded on the scoreboard.

### Common Mistakes
- **Flicking too hard** — pieces fly off the board. Flick gently for controlled collisions.
- **Not predicting before the shot** — the learning is in the prediction. Always predict before flicking.
- **Ignoring the line of centres** — the rebound direction is along the line of centres, not the striker's direction. Draw the line mentally before flicking.
- **Board not smooth** — rough surfaces slow the pieces unevenly. Use a smooth tabletop or a carrom board with powder.
- **Unequal piece sizes** — the swap only works for equal masses. Use same-size pieces.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Striker hits a piece head-on. What happens to the striker? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Striker hits off-centre. Which way does the piece go? (mirrors pre-test)
3. What is the line of centres? (mirrors pre-test)
4. A striker hits a piece at 30° off-centre. Sketch the line of centres and predict the piece's direction.
5. Challenge: for equal masses in a glancing elastic collision, why are the two final directions at 90° to each other?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The striker stops, and the piece moves forward at the striker's speed (the equal-mass elastic swap).
2. The piece moves along the line of centres — the line connecting the centres at contact. The piece goes at an angle, not in the striker's original direction.
3. The line connecting the centres of the two colliding objects at the moment of contact. The rebound direction is along this line.
4. (Sketch — the line of centres is drawn from the striker's centre to the piece's centre at contact. The piece moves along this line, which is at an angle to the striker's original direction. The striker moves off at a different angle, roughly perpendicular to the piece's direction for equal masses.)
5. Conservation of momentum (vector): p₁ = p₁' + p₂' (vector sum). Conservation of KE: p₁²/(2m) = p₁'²/(2m) + p₂'²/(2m) → p₁² = p₁'² + p₂'². These two equations describe a right-angled triangle: p₁ is the hypotenuse, and p₁' and p₂' are the two legs. Therefore, p₁' and p₂' are perpendicular (90° apart). This is a beautiful consequence of both conservation laws — the two pieces always move at 90° to each other after a glancing elastic collision of equal masses.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A striker (0.1 kg) at 4 m/s hits a stationary piece (0.1 kg) at 30° off-centre. The piece moves at 2 m/s along the line of centres. Find the striker's final speed and direction.
   **Worked Answer:** By the 90° rule (equal masses, glancing elastic): the piece and the striker move at 90° to each other. The piece goes along the line of centres at 2 m/s. The striker goes at 90° to that direction. From momentum conservation (vector triangle): p₁² = p₁'² + p₂'² → (0.1 × 4)² = (0.1 × v₁')² + (0.1 × 2)² → 0.16 = 0.01v₁'² + 0.04 → 0.01v₁'² = 0.12 → v₁'² = 12 → v₁' = 3.46 m/s. The striker moves at 3.46 m/s at 90° to the piece's direction. Check: KE_before = ½ × 0.1 × 16 = 0.8 J. KE_after = ½ × 0.1 × 12 + ½ × 0.1 × 4 = 0.6 + 0.2 = 0.8 J ✓.
2. **Q:** In a carrom break, the striker hits two pieces simultaneously. The first piece goes forward at 3 m/s, the second goes at 45° at 2 m/s. Find the striker's final velocity (all masses equal, striker initially at 5 m/s).
   **Worked Answer:** Momentum conservation (vector): p_striker_initial = p_striker_final + p_piece1 + p_piece2. Set up coordinates: striker initially along x. p_initial = 0.1 × 5 = 0.5 kg·m/s (x-direction). p_piece1 = 0.1 × 3 = 0.3 (x-direction, assuming head-on). p_piece2 = 0.1 × 2 at 45° = (0.2cos45°, 0.2sin45°) = (0.141, 0.141). p_striker_final = p_initial − p_piece1 − p_piece2 = (0.5 − 0.3 − 0.141, 0 − 0.141) = (0.059, −0.141). |p_striker_final| = √(0.059² + 0.141²) = √(0.0035 + 0.0199) = √0.0234 = 0.153 kg·m/s. v_striker = 0.153/0.1 = 1.53 m/s. Direction: tan θ = −0.141/0.059 = −2.39 → θ = −67° (below the x-axis). The striker slows to 1.53 m/s and deflects downward. This is a complex collision — the two pieces take most of the momentum.
3. **Q:** A marbles player wants to pot a marble in a corner pocket. The marble is at 30° from the striker's position. Where should the striker aim to hit the marble into the pocket?
   **Worked Answer:** The marble will move along the line of centres at contact. To send the marble toward the pocket, the line of centres must point from the marble to the pocket. So the striker must aim at a point on the marble such that the line from the striker's centre to the marble's centre points toward the pocket. In practice: aim at the "ghost ball" — an imaginary ball touching the target marble on the side opposite the pocket. The striker should aim at the ghost ball position, so that when it arrives, the line of centres points from striker to marble to pocket. This is the fundamental aiming technique in carrom, billiards, and marbles: aim at the ghost ball, not at the target's centre.

## Safety Notes
- Do not flick pieces hard — they can fly off the board and hit someone.
- Keep the area around the board clear.
- Use bottle tops instead of marbles if the floor is hard (marbles can chip or shatter).
- Do not lean on the carrom board — it can tip.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 265 (elastic collisions) and Lesson 267 (conservation of momentum) provide the physics. This lesson applies it to a game context.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the carrom break, the line of centres, and the swap. Ages 14–17 should derive the 90° rule, solve glancing collision problems, and apply the ghost-ball aiming technique.
- Local resource tips: a carrom board is common in many Kenyan schools. If no carrom board is available, a smooth tabletop with a chalk circle works. Bottle tops are a free alternative to carrom pieces. The tournament format keeps learners engaged.
- The 90° rule (equal masses, glancing elastic collision → final directions at 90°) is a beautiful and memorable result. It comes from the Pythagorean relationship between the momentum vectors when both momentum and KE are conserved.
- The ghost-ball aiming technique is a practical application of the line of centres rule. It is used in carrom, billiards, and snooker — the physics is the same.
