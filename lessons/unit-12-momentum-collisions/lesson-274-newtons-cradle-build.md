# Lesson 274: Build: A Bottle-Top Newton's Cradle

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** newtons-cradle

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a Newton's cradle from five bottle tops (or nuts) suspended on V-strings in a frame.
2. Observe the pattern: one ball swings in, one ball swings out; two swing in, two swing out.
3. Explain the pattern using conservation of momentum and kinetic energy (elastic collisions).
4. Tune the cradle for clean transfers by adjusting the string length and contact alignment.

## Materials
- Five bottle tops (or nuts, or small balls) of equal mass
- String or fishing line (thin, strong)
- A wooden frame (two vertical posts and a horizontal top bar) or a cardboard box
- A ruler — to measure string lengths
- Scissors — to cut the string
- Tape or glue — to fix the strings to the frame
- A reference image of a Newton's cradle (if available)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **Newton's cradle** (tedo wa Newton) — a row of equal-mass balls on strings that transfer momentum through elastic collisions
- **momentum transfer** (hamisha mumbo) — the ball that swings in passes its momentum through the row to the ball on the other end
- **clean transfer** (hamisha safi) — one ball in, one ball out; the sign of a well-tuned cradle
- **V-string** (kamba kwa V) — two strings per ball forming a V, to keep the ball swinging in a straight line

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. In a Newton's cradle, one ball swings in and hits the row. How many balls swing out?
2. What two quantities are conserved in each collision?
3. If two balls swing in, how many swing out?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. One ball swings out on the other end — the momentum is transferred through the row.
2. Momentum (p = mv) and kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) — the collisions are elastic.
3. Two balls swing out — the pattern preserves both momentum and KE. Two balls in have 2mv momentum and mv² KE (per ball); two balls out have the same. One ball out would have the right momentum (2mv) but too much KE (½m(2v)² = 2mv² ≠ 2 × ½mv² = mv²). So two must swing out to conserve both.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show a Newton's cradle (or a video). Pull one ball, release — one ball swings out the other end. Pull two — two swing out. Ask: *why does the pattern preserve the count?* Board: **momentum + KE conservation**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the bottle-top Newton's cradle (see Hands-On Build). They suspend five bottle tops on V-strings from the frame, spaced so they just touch. They pull one ball and release — one should swing out. They tune the strings (equal length, aligned contact) until the transfer is clean. They test: one in → one out, two in → two out. Results go on a class table: test | expected | observed | clean?
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The Newton's cradle works because each collision is (nearly) elastic: momentum and KE are conserved. One ball in at speed v: it has p = mv and KE = ½mv². If one ball swings out at v, both p and KE are conserved. If two balls swung out at v/2 each: p = 2 × m(v/2) = mv ✓, but KE = 2 × ½m(v/2)² = ¼mv² ≠ ½mv² ✗. So two balls out at v/2 doesn't work. If two balls swing in at v each: p = 2mv, KE = 2 × ½mv² = mv². Two balls out at v each: p = 2mv ✓, KE = mv² ✓. So two in → two out. Confront the misconception that "the middle balls carry the momentum" — they do momentarily, but the momentum passes through so fast that the middle balls barely move. The transfer is a sequence of pair-wise elastic collisions: ball 1 hits ball 2 (swap), ball 2 hits ball 3 (swap), and so on until ball 5 flies out. The middle balls are intermediaries — they pass the momentum along without retaining it. Also address: "why doesn't one ball in cause two balls out at half speed?" — because KE would not be conserved (½mv² ≠ 2 × ½m(v/2)² = ¼mv²). Both conservation laws must be satisfied, and the only solution for equal masses is "same number in, same number out at the same speed."
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *equal masses + elastic = same number in, same number out*. Homework: explain why one ball in cannot produce two balls out at half speed (use KE conservation).

## Hands-On Build: Bottle-Top Newton's Cradle
*Build a Newton's cradle from bottle tops on V-strings in a frame.*

### Parts
- Five bottle tops (or nuts, or small balls) of equal mass
- String or fishing line (thin, strong)
- A wooden frame (two vertical posts ~30 cm tall and a horizontal top bar ~40 cm long) or a sturdy cardboard box
- A ruler — to measure string lengths
- Scissors — to cut the string
- Tape or glue — to fix the strings to the frame
- A reference image of a Newton's cradle (if available)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Build the frame: two vertical posts and a horizontal top bar (or use a cardboard box with the top cut open). **Checkpoint:** the frame is sturdy and the top bar is level.
2. For each bottle top, tie two strings forming a V: one string to the left, one to the right, both attached to the top bar. The V keeps the ball swinging in a straight line (a single string would let it swing sideways). **Checkpoint:** the V-strings are the same length for all five balls — they must swing in the same arc.
3. Space the bottle tops so they just touch when hanging vertically. **Checkpoint:** the gaps are minimal — if they are too far apart, the collisions are weak; if too close, they push each other.
4. Adjust the string lengths so all five balls hang at the same height and the contact points are aligned (the balls touch at their widest point). **Checkpoint:** misaligned contact (one ball higher than another) causes energy loss and messy transfers.
5. Pull one ball back ~5 cm and release. Observe: one ball should swing out the other end. **Checkpoint:** clean transfer — one in, one out.
6. If the transfer is messy (multiple balls move, or the transfer dies quickly), adjust: (a) equalise the string lengths, (b) align the contact points, (c) ensure the balls are the same mass. **Checkpoint:** tuning is the key step — a well-tuned cradle transfers cleanly.
7. Test: pull two balls and release. Two should swing out. **Checkpoint:** two in, two out — the pattern holds.

### Common Mistakes
- **Unequal string lengths** — if one ball hangs lower, the contact is misaligned and energy is lost. All strings must be the same length.
- **Bottle tops not the same mass** — unequal masses break the equal-mass assumption. Use identical bottle tops (same brand, same size).
- **V-strings too wide** — if the V is too wide, the balls swing in an arc but also move sideways. Keep the V angle small (~10–15°).
- **Balls too far apart** — if the balls don't touch, the collision is weak. Space them so they just touch.
- **Frame not rigid** — a wobbly frame absorbs energy. Use a rigid wooden frame or a sturdy box.
- **Not tuning** — the first build is rarely perfect. Adjust string lengths and contact alignment until the transfer is clean.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. One ball swings in. How many swing out? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What two quantities are conserved? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Two balls swing in. How many swing out? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Why can't one ball in produce two balls out at half speed? (Use KE conservation.)
5. Challenge: three balls swing in at speed v. How many swing out and at what speed?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. One ball swings out on the other end — momentum is transferred through the row.
2. Momentum (p = mv) and kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) — the collisions are elastic.
3. Two balls swing out — both momentum and KE are conserved.
4. One ball in: p = mv, KE = ½mv². Two balls out at v/2: p = 2 × m(v/2) = mv ✓, but KE = 2 × ½m(v/2)² = 2 × ½m × v²/4 = ¼mv² ≠ ½mv² ✗. KE is not conserved (only half the KE is accounted for). So two balls out at half speed is impossible for an elastic collision.
5. Three balls in at v: p = 3mv, KE = 3 × ½mv² = 1.5mv². Three balls out at v: p = 3mv ✓, KE = 1.5mv² ✓. So three in → three out at the same speed. The pattern generalises: N balls in → N balls out at the same speed, for equal masses and elastic collisions.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Show that for equal masses, one ball in at v cannot produce two balls out at v/2. Prove that the only solution is one ball out at v.
   **Worked Answer:** One ball in: p = mv, KE = ½mv². Two balls out at v₁' and v₂': p: mv = mv₁' + mv₂' → v = v₁' + v₂'. KE: ½mv² = ½mv₁'² + ½mv₂'² → v² = v₁'² + v₂'². From p: v₂' = v − v₁'. Substituting: v² = v₁'² + (v − v₁')² = v₁'² + v² − 2vv₁' + v₁'² → 0 = 2v₁'² − 2vv₁' → v₁'(v₁' − v) = 0. Solutions: v₁' = 0 (no collision — v₂' = v, which is one ball out at v) or v₁' = v (v₂' = 0 — no collision). The only physical solution is one ball out at v. Two balls out at v/2 each would require v₁' = v₂' = v/2, but v₁'² + v₂'² = v²/4 + v²/4 = v²/2 ≠ v². KE is not conserved. So it's impossible.
2. **Q:** Two balls in at v each. Show that the only solution is two balls out at v each.
   **Worked Answer:** Two balls in: p = 2mv, KE = mv². If one ball out at v': p: 2mv = mv' → v' = 2v. KE: mv² = ½m(2v)² = 2mv² ≠ mv². KE is not conserved (too much). If two balls out at v' each: p: 2mv = 2mv' → v' = v. KE: mv² = 2 × ½mv² = mv² ✓. Both conserved. If three balls out at v' each: p: 2mv = 3mv' → v' = 2v/3. KE: mv² = 3 × ½m(2v/3)² = 3 × ½m × 4v²/9 = 2mv²/3 ≠ mv². KE not conserved. So the only solution is two balls out at v each.
3. **Q:** A real Newton's cradle slows down and stops after a few minutes. Why? Where does the energy go?
   **Worked Answer:** The collisions are not perfectly elastic — some KE is lost in each collision to: (a) sound (the click of the balls), (b) heat (from the deformation of the balls at contact, even if it's tiny), (c) air resistance (the balls drag through the air as they swing), and (d) friction at the string attachments (the strings rub against the frame). Each of these removes a small amount of energy per swing. After hundreds of swings, the cumulative loss is significant and the cradle stops. The rate of decay is exponential: the amplitude decreases by a fixed fraction each swing. A well-tuned cradle with hard steel balls can swing for several minutes; a bottle-top cradle with soft contacts may stop in under a minute. The energy ultimately becomes heat (in the balls, the air, and the frame) and sound (the clicks).

## Safety Notes
- Do not use heavy balls (steel bearings) without supervision — they can pinch fingers.
- Bottle tops are safe but may have sharp edges — smooth them with sandpaper if needed.
- Do not pull the balls back too far — they may tangle the strings or miss the contact.
- The frame should be stable — a falling frame is a hazard.
- Scissors should be used carefully — the teacher may pre-cut the string.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 265 (elastic collisions) and 267 (conservation of momentum) provide the physics. This lesson applies it to a build project that makes the conservation laws visible.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the cradle and observing the pattern (one in → one out, two in → two out). Ages 14–17 should prove the pattern from conservation laws and explain why the cradle eventually stops.
- Local resource tips: bottle tops are free and widely available. String or fishing line is cheap. A wooden frame can be made from sticks or a cardboard box. If nuts (metal) are available, they work better than bottle tops (heavier, more elastic contact).
- This is a project lesson (project: newtons-cradle). The deliverable is a working Newton's cradle. The tuning step (adjusting string length and contact alignment) is the most important — a well-tuned cradle demonstrates the physics clearly; a poorly tuned one is confusing.
- The proof that "N in → N out" for equal masses and elastic collisions is a beautiful application of simultaneous conservation laws. It is one of the most satisfying results in introductory mechanics.
