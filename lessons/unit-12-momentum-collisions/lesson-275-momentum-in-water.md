# Lesson 275: Momentum in Water and Air

**Unit:** 12 — Momentum & Collisions  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Observe that a hose jet pushes back (reaction force) and that the push grows with flow speed.
2. Explain river-bend erosion: the fast outside bank carries more momentum and erodes the bank.
3. Demonstrate that fanning air moves paper — air has momentum (ρAv²).
4. Relate fluid momentum to everyday life: hose recoil, wind on sails, river erosion.

## Materials
- A garden hose or a plastic bottle with holes (to create a jet)
- A bucket of water
- A piece of paper or a leaf
- A hand fan or a piece of cardboard (to fan air)
- A stick or ruler — to demonstrate river-bend flow (optional, in a tray of water with sand)
- A shallow tray with sand and water (to demonstrate erosion — optional)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **fluid momentum** (mumbo wa kiowevu) — momentum of a flowing fluid; p = ρAv² for a jet of density ρ, area A, speed v
- **jet reaction** (mgongano wa tope) — the backward force on a hose or nozzle when fluid is ejected forward
- **river erosion** (mmomonyoko wa mto) — the wearing away of the outside bank of a river bend by fast-flowing water
- **air momentum** (mumbo wa hewa) — moving air carries momentum; a fan or wind pushes objects

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A hose ejects water forward. Which way does the hose push your hand?
2. Why does a river erode the outside bank of a bend more than the inside?
3. Does air have momentum? Give an example.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Backward — the water's forward momentum is balanced by the hose's backward reaction (conservation of momentum). The faster the flow, the stronger the push.
2. The water on the outside of the bend flows faster (it travels a longer path in the same time). Faster water has more momentum and exerts more force on the outside bank, eroding it. The inside bank is slower and deposits sediment.
3. Yes — air has mass and velocity, so it has momentum (p = mv). A fan pushes air, and the air pushes back (reaction). Wind moves leaves, dust, and sails. A hair dryer pushes air and can move a light object.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — turn on a hose (or squeeze a bottle with a hole). The water jets forward and the hose pushes back. Ask: *why does the hose kick back?* Board: **fluid momentum** — the water's forward momentum = the hose's backward reaction.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs test fluid momentum (see Hands-On Build). They fill a bottle with water, make a hole near the bottom, and let the water jet out. The bottle recoils (moves backward). They try: (a) a small hole (fast jet, less mass flow), (b) a large hole (slower jet, more mass flow), (c) different water levels (more pressure = faster jet). They also fan air at a piece of paper and observe it move. They simulate a river bend in a tray of sand and water: the outside bank erodes faster. Results go on a class table: hole size | water level | jet speed (estimate) | bottle recoil | observation.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Fluids (water and air) have momentum because they have mass and velocity. For a jet: the momentum flow rate (force) is F = ρAv², where ρ is the fluid density, A is the nozzle area, and v is the jet speed. The hose (or bottle) recoils backward with equal force. River bends: the water on the outside of the bend flows faster (longer path in the same time), so it has more momentum and erodes the outside bank. The inside bank is slower and deposits sediment (the water can't carry it). This is why rivers meander — the outside bends erode and the inside bends deposit, making the bend sharper over time. Air momentum: a fan pushes air, and the air pushes back (reaction). Wind on a sail transfers momentum from the air to the boat. Confront the misconception that "air is too light to have momentum" — air has a density of ~1.2 kg/m³, which is small but not zero. A 10 m/s wind hitting a 1 m² sail transfers 10 × 1.2 × 10² = 1200 N of force — enough to move a boat. Also address: "the hose pushes on the water, not on my hand" — the hose pushes the water forward (action), and the water pushes the hose backward (reaction). Your hand feels the backward push. The faster the flow, the stronger the push (F ∝ v²).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *fluids have momentum — jets recoil, rivers erode, wind pushes*. Homework: look at a river bend near your home and identify the outside bank (eroding) and the inside bank (depositing).

## Hands-On Build: Bottle Jet and River Bend
*Demonstrate fluid momentum with a bottle jet and a river-bend simulation.*

### Parts
- A plastic bottle with a hole near the bottom (the teacher should make the hole)
- A bucket of water
- A piece of paper or a leaf
- A hand fan or a piece of cardboard
- A shallow tray with sand and water (for the river-bend simulation — optional)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Fill the bottle with water. Hold it over the bucket. **Checkpoint:** water jets out of the hole — the bottle recoils (moves backward).
2. Observe the recoil direction: the bottle moves opposite to the jet. **Checkpoint:** the jet goes one way, the bottle goes the other — momentum conservation.
3. Try different hole sizes: a small hole (fast jet, narrow stream) vs a large hole (slower jet, wider stream). Compare the recoil. **Checkpoint:** the recoil force depends on the momentum flow rate (F = ρAv²) — a small hole at high pressure may give a stronger recoil than a large hole at low pressure.
4. Try different water levels: a full bottle (high pressure, fast jet) vs a half-full bottle (lower pressure, slower jet). **Checkpoint:** higher water level = faster jet = more recoil.
5. Fan air at a piece of paper. Observe it move. **Checkpoint:** the air has momentum — it pushes the paper. The fan pushes the air, and the air pushes the paper.
6. If a tray with sand and water is available: pour water slowly around a bend in the sand. Observe: the outside bank erodes (fast water), the inside bank deposits (slow water). **Checkpoint:** the outside bank collapses; the inside bank builds up. This is river meandering.

### Common Mistakes
- **Hole too large** — the water drains too fast and the jet is weak. Start with a small hole (2–3 mm).
- **Not holding the bottle over a bucket** — water goes everywhere. Always work over a bucket or tray.
- **Not observing the recoil** — the bottle's backward motion is subtle. Hold it loosely (on rollers or floating in the bucket) to see the recoil clearly.
- **Fanning too gently** — a gentle fan barely moves the paper. Fan firmly to see the effect.
- **River-bend simulation too fast** — pour the water slowly to see the erosion and deposition. A fast pour washes everything away.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A hose ejects water forward. Which way does the hose push? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why does a river erode the outside bank more? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Does air have momentum? Give an example. (mirrors pre-test)
4. A jet of water (density 1000 kg/m³) exits a 1 cm² nozzle at 5 m/s. Estimate the reaction force (F = ρAv²).
5. Challenge: explain why a sailboat can sail INTO the wind (not just downwind), using momentum.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Backward — the water's forward momentum is balanced by the hose's backward reaction (conservation of momentum).
2. The water on the outside of the bend flows faster (longer path in the same time). Faster water has more momentum and exerts more force on the outside bank, eroding it.
3. Yes — air has mass and velocity, so it has momentum. A fan pushes air, and the air pushes objects (paper, leaves, dust). Wind moves sails.
4. A = 1 cm² = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m². F = ρAv² = 1000 × 10⁻⁴ × 25 = 2.5 N. The reaction force is 2.5 N (about 250 g of push backward).
5. A sailboat sails into the wind by angling the sail. The wind hits the sail and is deflected sideways (the sail curves the airflow). By momentum conservation, the air's momentum changes direction, and the sail (and boat) gets a reaction force perpendicular to the wind. The keel (the fin under the boat) prevents sideways motion, so only the forward component of the reaction force moves the boat. The boat zigzags (tacks) into the wind — each tack makes progress at an angle to the wind. The physics: the sail deflects the air, the air's momentum change gives the sail a force, and the keel channels that force into forward motion. This is how sailboats can sail up to about 45° into the wind (not directly into it — they must tack).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A water jet (ρ = 1000 kg/m³) exits a 1 cm² nozzle at 5 m/s. Find the reaction force. Then double the speed to 10 m/s and find the new force.
   **Worked Answer:** F = ρAv² = 1000 × 10⁻⁴ × 25 = 2.5 N. At 10 m/s: F = 1000 × 10⁻⁴ × 100 = 10 N. Doubling the speed quadruples the force (F ∝ v²). This is why high-pressure hose nozzles have a strong kickback — the jet speed is high and the force grows with the square of the speed.
2. **Q:** A river bends with an inside radius of 10 m and an outside radius of 15 m. The water completes the bend in 30 s. Find the speed on the inside and outside banks. Then compare the erosion force (F ∝ v²).
   **Worked Answer:** Inside path length = 2π × 10 = 62.8 m. Outside path length = 2π × 15 = 94.2 m. Both take 30 s. v_inside = 62.8/30 = 2.09 m/s. v_outside = 94.2/30 = 3.14 m/s. Erosion force ratio: F_outside/F_inside = (v_outside/v_inside)² = (3.14/2.09)² = (1.5)² = 2.25. The outside bank experiences 2.25× the erosion force of the inside bank. This is why the outside bank erodes and the inside bank deposits — the force difference is significant.
3. **Q:** A wind of 10 m/s hits a 2 m² sail. The air density is 1.2 kg/m³. Estimate the force on the sail. Then compare with a 20 m/s wind.
   **Worked Answer:** F = ρAv² = 1.2 × 2 × 100 = 240 N. At 20 m/s: F = 1.2 × 2 × 400 = 960 N. Doubling the wind speed quadruples the force (F ∝ v²). A 240 N force (about 24 kg of push) is enough to move a small boat. A 960 N force (about 98 kg) is enough to capsize a small boat if the sail is not adjusted. This is why sailors reduce sail area in high winds (reefing) — the force grows with v², so high winds are exponentially more dangerous.

## Safety Notes
- Work over a bucket or tray — water goes everywhere.
- Do not point the hose or bottle jet at people.
- The bottle may slip when wet — hold it firmly.
- Do not fan sand or dust into the air — it can get in eyes.
- The river-bend simulation should be done outside or over a large tray — sand and water are messy.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 267–270 covered conservation of momentum and rocket propulsion. This lesson extends the concept to fluids (water and air), which are continuous (not discrete objects).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the bottle jet and the fan demo — the idea that "fluids push back." Ages 14–17 should compute jet forces, compare erosion rates, and explain sailboat physics.
- Local resource tips: a plastic bottle with a hole is the simplest jet. A bucket of water is the reservoir. A hand fan or a piece of cardboard is the air-momentum demo. The river-bend simulation needs a tray with sand and water — best done outside.
- The F = ρAv² formula is introduced for ages 14–17. It is the fluid analogue of F = Δp/Δt for a continuous flow: the mass flow rate is ṁ = ρAv, and the momentum flow rate is ṁv = ρAv².
- The river-bend erosion example connects physics to geography and environmental science. The meandering of rivers (Lake Victoria's tributaries, the Tana River) is a real-world application of fluid momentum.
- The sailboat example is an advanced application that may interest learners who have seen sailboats on Lake Victoria or the coast. The key insight is that the sail deflects the air (changing its momentum direction), and the keel channels the reaction into forward motion.
