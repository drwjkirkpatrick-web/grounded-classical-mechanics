# Lesson 277: The Materials Around Us

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Identify at least eight common materials in the school compound and name a use for each.
2. Match a material's properties (strong, stiff, flexible, heavy, light, rot-resistant) to the job it does.
3. Explain why a panga is steel and not wood, and why a sufuria is metal and not clay.
4. Build a properties matrix comparing five materials across strength, stiffness, weight, cost, and rot.

## Materials
- A "materials box" — collected by the teacher beforehand, containing small samples of: wood (offcut), steel wire or nail, sisal twine, a piece of mabati (corrugated iron), a lump of clay or mud brick, a bamboo strip, a cut-up PET bottle piece
- A second set of objects already in the classroom: a panga, a sufuria, a plastic jerrican, a wooden ruler, a chalk stick
- Notebook and pencil
- A printed or board-drawn table with five columns: Material | Strength | Stiffness | Weight | Cost | Rot risk
- A ruler or tape measure (for comparing thicknesses)

## Vocabulary
- **material** (nyenzo) — what a thing is made from; wood, metal, plastic, clay, fibre
- **property** (sifa) — how a material behaves: strong, stiff, flexible, heavy, light
- **strength** (nguvu) — how much force a material can take before breaking
- **stiffness** (ugumu) — how hard it is to bend; a stiff material hardly moves when pushed
- **flexible** (kunyumbulika) — bends easily without breaking, like rubber or sisal
- **corrosion / rot** (kutu / kuoza) — how a material degrades over time: steel rusts, wood rots, plastic lasts

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Name three different materials you can see right now in this classroom.
2. Why is a panga made of steel and not wood?
3. Which rots faster in the rain: a sisal rope or a steel wire?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Any three correct: wood (desk, door), metal (nail, window frame), plastic (jerrican, ruler), glass (window), concrete (floor), chalk (calcium sulphate).
2. Steel is harder and stronger than wood — it holds a sharp edge and does not blunt or break when cutting brush. Wood would dull and split.
3. Sisal rots faster — it is a natural fibre that absorbs water and decays. Steel rusts but takes much longer to lose its strength.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the teacher holds up a panga and a wooden ruler side by side. Ask: *why is the panga steel and the ruler wood?* Take answers. Board the key idea: **we choose materials for the job** — the panga needs hardness and strength (steel); the ruler needs lightness and cheapness (wood or plastic). Introduce the word **property** — a material's property is how it behaves when you push, pull, bend, or leave it in the rain.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in pairs, learners open the materials box and lay out the eight samples (see Hands-On Build). For each sample they: (a) hold it, bend it gently, tap it, feel its weight; (b) write down two properties they notice (e.g., "wood — stiff, light"; "sisal — flexible, rough"); (c) name one thing in the school or home made from that material. Then they walk around the classroom or compound for 5 minutes and find three more objects, noting the material and why it suits the job. Results go in the notebook.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Build a class table: Material | Best property | Chosen use | Why. Wood — stiff, light, cheap — desk, door frame — holds shape but is easy to cut. Steel — strong, hard, stiff — panga, nail, window bar — carries load and holds an edge. Sisal — flexible, strong in tension — rope, tying loads — bends but pulls hard. Mabati — stiff, waterproof, light — roof, wall — sheds rain, does not rot. Clay — mouldable, hard when dry — bricks, pots — cheap, easy to shape. Bamboo — stiff, light, hollow — scaffolding, fencing — grows fast, strong for its weight. PET — light, waterproof, flexible — bottles, containers — cheap, does not rot or rust. Confront the misconception that "strong means hard" — sisal is not hard but is strong in tension; clay is hard but brittle. **Strength and stiffness are different.**
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *every material has a job it suits best*. Homework: at home, list five objects and the material each is made from, then write one property that makes it fit for the job.

## Hands-On Build: Materials Survey and Properties Table
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Materials box with eight samples: wood, steel wire/nail, sisal twine, mabati offcut, clay lump or mud brick, bamboo strip, PET bottle piece
- Classroom objects: panga, sufuria, plastic jerrican, wooden ruler, chalk
- Notebook and pencil
- A table drawn on the board or in the notebook with columns: Material | Strength | Stiffness | Weight | Cost | Rot risk

### Workflow
1. Lay out all eight samples on the desk. Handle each one: bend it gently between your fingers, tap it with a fingernail, feel its weight in your hand. **Checkpoint:** every learner handles at least four different samples.
2. For each sample, write two properties you notice. Use words from the vocabulary list: strong, stiff, flexible, heavy, light, waterproof, rough, smooth. **Checkpoint:** each sample has at least two properties written.
3. Name one object in the school or home made from that material. Write it next to the properties. **Checkpoint:** every sample has a real use named — no guessing.
4. Walk around the classroom or compound for 5 minutes. Find three more objects not in the box. For each, note the material and one property that suits its job. **Checkpoint:** three real objects found and recorded with material and property.
5. Return to the desk. Fill in the properties table for five of the eight samples: rate each as High / Medium / Low for strength, stiffness, weight, cost, and rot risk. **Checkpoint:** the table has five rows filled with all five columns.

### Common Mistakes
- **Confusing strong and stiff** — a sisal rope is strong (holds a lot of weight) but not stiff (it bends easily). Steel wire is both strong and stiff. Write the distinction on the board before the survey.
- **Guessing uses instead of observing** — learners should name objects they can actually see or have handled, not invent uses. If unsure, go and look.
- **Ignoring rot and rust** — learners often forget that materials degrade. Ask: *what happens to this after two rainy seasons?* Steel rusts, wood rots, sisal rots, mabati rusts slowly, PET and clay do not rot.
- **Rating everything "High"** — if every material is "high" for every property, the table is useless. Encourage honest comparison: is wood stronger than steel? Is bamboo heavier than clay?

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Name three materials from the box and one use for each. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why is a sufuria made of metal and not clay?
3. Which is more flexible: a steel wire or a sisal rope? Which is stronger in tension?
4. What happens to sisal rope left in the rain for a year? What happens to a steel wire?
5. Challenge: you need to build a roof for a chicken coop. Choose between mabati and thatch. Give one property that suits your choice.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Any three correct with matching uses: wood — desk/door; steel — panga/nail; sisal — rope; mabati — roof/wall; clay — bricks/pots; bamboo — scaffolding/fence; PET — bottles.
2. Metal (aluminium or steel) is waterproof, does not crack when heated on a fire, and is light enough to carry. Clay pots crack on direct flame and are heavier.
3. Sisal rope is more flexible (bends easily). Steel wire is stronger in tension (holds more pulling force before breaking).
4. Sisal rots — it absorbs water, fungi break down the fibres, and it weakens and snaps. Steel rusts — a surface layer of iron oxide forms; it weakens slowly but takes much longer than sisal to fail.
5. Either choice with a valid property: Mabati — waterproof and rot-resistant, lasts 10+ years. Thatch — insulates (cooler inside), cheaper, but needs replacing every 3–5 years and attracts insects.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Build a properties matrix for five materials (wood, steel, sisal, mabati, PET) rating each High / Medium / Low for strength, stiffness, weight, cost, and rot. Choose one use for each and justify it from the matrix.
   **Worked Answer:**
   | Material | Strength | Stiffness | Weight | Cost | Rot | Chosen use | Justification |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | Wood | Medium | High | Low | Low | High (rots) | Door frame | Stiff enough to hold shape, light, cheap, easy to cut |
   | Steel | High | High | High | Medium | Medium (rusts) | Window bar | Very strong and stiff, carries load, resists burglary |
   | Sisal | Medium (tension) | Low | Low | Low | High (rots) | Tying luggage | Strong in tension, flexible enough to tie knots, cheap |
   | Mabati | Medium | High | Low | Low | Low (rusts slowly) | Roof | Stiff, waterproof, light, rot-resistant |
   | PET | Low | Medium | Very low | Very low | None | Water bottle | Light, waterproof, does not rot or rust, cheap |

   The matrix shows that no material is best at everything — each is chosen for the properties the job needs most.
2. **Q:** A jua kali workshop wants to make a water tank stand. They have wood, steel, and bamboo. Which do they choose for the legs and why? What property matters most?
   **Worked Answer:** Steel for the legs. A water tank is heavy (a 1000-litre tank weighs ~1000 kg full). The legs must carry that load in compression without buckling. Steel has the highest strength and stiffness — it will not bend or compress under the tank's weight. Wood could work for a small tank but may rot over time at the base where it sits in wet ground. Bamboo is too thin-walled and can split under heavy point loads. The key property is **compressive strength** — the ability to carry a pushing force without buckling or crushing.
3. **Q:** Explain why "strong" and "stiff" are different properties, using sisal rope and a steel rod as examples.
   **Worked Answer:** Strength is the force a material can take before breaking. Stiffness is how much it bends under a given force. A sisal rope can hold 200 kg without breaking (strong) but it stretches and sags visibly under just 20 kg (not stiff). A steel rod of the same size might hold 500 kg (stronger) and barely moves under 200 kg (stiffer). A material can be strong but not stiff (sisal), stiff but not strong (chalk — hard to bend but snaps easily), both (steel), or neither (a wet clay lump). Engineers choose based on which property the job needs: a bridge cable needs strength in tension; a bookshelf needs stiffness so it does not sag.

## Safety Notes
- Do not cut or break the samples — handle them gently. The teacher pre-cuts sharp edges off mabati and wire.
- Steel wire ends can puncture skin — do not poke or wave them near faces.
- Do not taste any sample — clay may contain bacteria, and steel has oil from manufacturing.
- Bamboo splinters can puncture — run hands along the strip and report any sharp splinters to the teacher.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners need no prior physics for this lesson — it is a survey and observation lesson that introduces the vocabulary the rest of Unit 13 will use (strength, stiffness, flexibility, deformation).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on handling the samples, naming properties, and matching materials to uses. Ages 14–17 should build the full properties matrix, justify choices with specific properties, and distinguish strength from stiffness.
- Local resource tips: all eight materials are free or very cheap in Kenya. Mabati offcuts come from any fundi's workshop. Sisal twine is sold at any dukia. Bamboo grows wild in many areas. PET bottles are litter — collect and wash them. Clay or mud bricks are everywhere.
- This lesson is the foundation for the entire unit. The vocabulary (strength, stiffness, flexibility, deformation) introduced here will be used in every subsequent lesson. Do not rush the handling — the physical experience of bending wood vs steel vs sisal is the point.
- The properties matrix is the advanced task and should be started in class if time allows, completed for homework.
