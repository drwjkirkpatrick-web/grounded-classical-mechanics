# Lesson 278: Stretch It: Elastic vs Plastic

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define elastic deformation — the material returns to its original shape when the force is removed.
2. Define plastic deformation — the material stays bent or stretched after the force is removed.
3. Demonstrate that rubber is elastic, plasticine is plastic, and wire does both in sequence.
4. Identify the elastic region and the yield point on a simple force-extension sketch.

## Materials
- A rubber strip (~2 cm wide, ~15 cm long) from an inner tube
- A lump of plasticine (or modelling clay / mud dough)
- A piece of soft wire (~30 cm long, ~1 mm thick — galvanised or copper wire from a jua kali workshop)
- A ruler or tape measure
- A marker pen (to mark the wire's starting length)
- Notebook and pencil
- A spring from a pen (optional — for comparison)

## Vocabulary
- **elastic deformation** (mabadiliko ya kunyumbulika) — the material springs back to its original shape when the force is removed
- **plastic deformation** (mabadiliko ya kudumu) — the material stays changed; the new shape is permanent
- **yield point** (hatua ya kutokea) — the force at which a material stops being elastic and starts being plastic
- **permanent set** (mabadiliko ya kudumu) — the amount of stretch or bend left after the force is removed
- **deformation** (mabadiliko ya umbo) — any change in shape caused by a force

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You stretch a rubber band and let go. What happens?
2. You bend a paperclip and let go. Does it spring back or stay bent?
3. What word describes a material that returns to its original shape after being stretched?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. It springs back to its original length — rubber is elastic.
2. It stays bent — metal wire has been plastically deformed; it does not return.
3. Elastic — the material returns to its original shape when the force is removed.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the teacher holds up a rubber strip and a lump of plasticine. Stretch the rubber and release — it snaps back. Squeeze the plasticine and release — it stays in the new shape. Ask: *what is the difference?* Board the two key words: **elastic** (springs back) and **plastic** (stays changed). Then hold up the wire: bend it a little and release — it springs back (elastic). Bend it more and release — it stays bent (plastic). Wire does both, in sequence. This is the key insight of the lesson.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs test all three materials (see Hands-On Build). For each material they: (a) apply a small force (stretch or bend), release, and observe whether it returns; (b) apply a larger force, release, and observe; (c) find the point where the material stops returning — the yield point. They record: material | small force (returns?) | large force (returns?) | yield point found? For the wire, they mark the starting length, bend it gently (returns), bend it further (stays bent), and measure the permanent set. They sketch what they see.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. **Elastic deformation**: the material returns to its original shape — like rubber, a spring, or a slightly bent wire. The atoms or molecules are pulled slightly apart but return when the force is removed. **Plastic deformation**: the material stays changed — like plasticine, a heavily bent wire, or a dented sufuria. The atoms or molecules have slid past each other into new positions and stay there. **The yield point** is the boundary: below it, the material is elastic; above it, the material is plastic. Wire is the clearest example: a small bend springs back (elastic), a large bend stays (plastic). Rubber is elastic up to a high force, then it tears. Plasticine is plastic from the start — it has no elastic region at room temperature. Board: **elastic → springs back; plastic → stays changed; yield point → the boundary**. Confront the misconception that "elastic means stretchy" — elastic means *returns*, not just stretches. Plasticine stretches easily but is not elastic because it does not return.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *elastic springs back, plastic stays, wire does both past the yield point*. Homework: find three objects at home that are elastic (rubber band, spring, mattress) and three that are plastic when deformed (bent spoon, dented bucket, squeezed tin).

## Hands-On Build: Elastic vs Plastic Tester
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- One rubber strip (~2 cm wide, ~15 cm long) from an inner tube
- One lump of plasticine (or modelling clay)
- One piece of soft wire (~30 cm, ~1 mm thick)
- A ruler or tape measure
- A marker pen
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. **Rubber test:** Hold the rubber strip between two fingers. Stretch it about 2 cm. Release. **Checkpoint:** it springs back to its original length — elastic. Now stretch it about 8 cm. Release. **Checkpoint:** it still springs back (rubber is elastic up to a high force). If you stretch it very hard it will tear — that is the breaking point, not the yield point.
2. **Plasticine test:** Squeeze the plasticine into a ball. Release. **Checkpoint:** it stays as a ball — plastic. Roll it into a snake shape. Release. **Checkpoint:** it stays as a snake — plastic from the start. There is no elastic region at room temperature.
3. **Wire test — elastic:** Hold the wire straight. Mark the starting length with the marker. Bend it about 1 cm to the side. Release. **Checkpoint:** it springs back to straight — elastic. The bend was below the yield point.
4. **Wire test — plastic:** Bend the wire about 5 cm to the side. Release. **Checkpoint:** it stays bent — plastic. The bend was above the yield point. Measure the permanent set: how far it stays from the original straight line.
5. **Wire test — find the yield point:** Start with a 1 cm bend (returns). Increase to 2 cm, 3 cm, 4 cm. Find the bend where the wire stops returning fully and stays slightly bent. **Checkpoint:** that is the yield point — the boundary between elastic and plastic. Record the bend angle at which it occurs.

### Common Mistakes
- **Confusing "stretchy" with "elastic"** — plasticine stretches easily but is not elastic because it does not return. Elastic means *returns to original shape*, not just *can be deformed*.
- **Overstretching the rubber** — if you pull the rubber too hard it tears. The point where it tears is the breaking point, not the yield point. Rubber's yield point is very close to its breaking point — it is elastic almost to the end.
- **Bending the wire too sharply at one point** — a sharp kink concentrates the force and snaps the wire. Bend it in a gentle curve, not a sharp fold.
- **Not marking the starting length** — without the marker line on the wire, you cannot measure the permanent set. Mark before you bend.
- **Thinking the yield point is a single exact force** — the yield point is a range, not a precise number. The wire gradually transitions from mostly-elastic to mostly-plastic. Report it as "around X cm of bend."

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. You stretch a rubber band and let go. What happens? (mirrors pre-test)
2. You bend a paperclip and let go. Does it spring back or stay bent? (mirrors pre-test)
3. What is the difference between elastic and plastic deformation?
4. A wire bends a little and springs back. You bend it more and it stays bent. What is the point called where the behaviour changes?
5. Challenge: plasticine stretches easily. Is it elastic? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. It springs back to its original length — rubber is elastic.
2. It stays bent — the wire has been plastically deformed.
3. Elastic deformation: the material returns to its original shape when the force is removed. Plastic deformation: the material stays in the new shape permanently.
4. The yield point — the force (or bend) at which the material stops being elastic and starts being plastic.
5. No — plasticine is not elastic. It stretches easily but does not return to its original shape. Elastic means *returns*, not just *stretches*. Plasticine is plastic from the start.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Sketch the force-extension curve for a wire: label the elastic region, the yield point, the plastic region, and the breaking point. Describe what happens at each stage.
   **Worked Answer:** The graph has force (F) on the y-axis and extension (x) on the x-axis. From the origin, the curve rises as a straight line — this is the **elastic region**, where F ∝ x (Hooke's law) and the wire returns to zero extension when the force is removed. At the **yield point**, the line curves and the wire stops returning fully — a small permanent set remains. Beyond the yield point is the **plastic region**: the wire extends more for less additional force (the curve flattens), and when the force is removed the wire does not return to zero — it follows a line back down parallel to the elastic line, leaving a permanent set. At the **breaking point**, the wire snaps — the curve ends. The elastic region is reversible; the plastic region is permanent.
2. **Q:** A copper wire is 30 cm long. A force of 5 N stretches it by 1 mm and it springs back (elastic). A force of 15 N stretches it by 4 mm and it stays stretched by 2 mm (plastic). What is the permanent set? How much of the 4 mm was elastic and how much was plastic?
   **Worked Answer:** Total extension at 15 N = 4 mm. Permanent set (what stays after the force is removed) = 2 mm. The elastic part = total extension − permanent set = 4 − 2 = 2 mm. So 2 mm was elastic (springs back) and 2 mm was plastic (stays). The wire was stretched beyond its yield point: below 5 N it was fully elastic (1 mm, returns); at 15 N it is partly plastic (2 mm stays, 2 mm returns). The yield point is somewhere between 5 N and 15 N.
3. **Q:** Why does a car body panel (steel) dent when hit but a rubber bumper bounces back? Use the terms elastic, plastic, and yield point.
   **Worked Answer:** The rubber bumper is designed to stay below its yield point in a low-speed impact — the force deforms it elastically, and it springs back when the force is removed. Its yield point is high relative to the forces it encounters. The steel body panel, when hit hard enough, exceeds its yield point — the force pushes the steel past the elastic region into the plastic region, and the dent stays. Steel's yield point is lower than rubber's in terms of how much energy it can absorb before permanent deformation. This is why car makers use crumple zones (Lesson 271) — areas designed to deform plastically and absorb energy, protecting the passenger cabin which is designed to stay elastic and rigid.

## Safety Notes
- Do not stretch the rubber strip near anyone's face — if it slips or tears it can whip back.
- Wire ends are sharp — do not poke or wave them. The teacher may pre-cut and deburr the ends.
- Do not bend the wire sharply — a sharp kink can snap the wire and the ends can fly.
- Plasticine is non-toxic but should not be eaten. Wash hands after handling.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 277 introduced materials and properties (strength, stiffness, flexibility). This lesson introduces the two types of deformation — elastic and plastic — which are the foundation for Hooke's law (Lesson 279) and the spring constant lab (Lesson 280).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the physical experience — stretch, release, observe. They should clearly see that rubber returns and plasticine stays. Ages 14–17 should find the yield point on the wire, measure the permanent set, and sketch the force-extension curve.
- Local resource tips: rubber strips from inner tubes are free at any tyre repair shop (vulcaniser). Plasticine is sold at bookshops; if unavailable, modelling clay or stiff mud dough works. Soft wire (galvanised or copper) is available at any hardware or jua kali workshop.
- The wire is the key teaching tool because it shows both behaviours in sequence. Rubber only shows elastic (until it tears). Plasticine only shows plastic. Wire shows elastic → yield → plastic → break. Make sure every pair tests the wire carefully.
- This lesson introduces vocabulary that will be used throughout the rest of the unit: elastic, plastic, yield point, permanent set, deformation. Write these on the board and keep them visible.
