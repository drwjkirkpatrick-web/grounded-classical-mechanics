# Lesson 279: Hooke's Law: F = kx

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. State Hooke's law: the extension of a spring (or rubber strip) is proportional to the force applied, within the elastic region.
2. Hang water-bottle weights on a rubber strip and measure the extension for each load.
3. Plot a force-extension graph and recognise the straight-line (proportional) region.
4. Calculate the spring constant k from the gradient of the graph (k = F/x).

## Materials
- A rubber strip (~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long) from an inner tube — the "spring"
- A spring from a pen (optional, for comparison)
- Four identical empty water bottles (500 ml or 1 L)
- Water — to fill the bottles as weights (500 ml water = 0.5 kg = 4.9 N)
- A marker pen — to mark the rubber strip's starting length
- A ruler or tape measure (to measure extension)
- A stand, hook, or sturdy branch to hang the rubber strip from
- Graph paper or squared notebook
- Calculator (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **Hooke's law** (sheria ya Hooke) — F = kx; force is proportional to extension, within the elastic region
- **spring constant (k)** (ugumu wa springi) — the stiffness of the spring in N/m; stiffer spring = larger k
- **extension (x)** (kunjua) — the extra length beyond the natural length, in metres
- **elastic region** (eneo la kunyumbulika) — the range where the material follows Hooke's law and returns to its original length
- **proportional** (sawia) — if force doubles, extension doubles; the graph is a straight line through the origin

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You hang a 1 kg mass on a spring and it stretches 2 cm. If you hang 2 kg, how far does it stretch? (Assume it stays elastic.)
2. Write the formula for Hooke's law.
3. What does a larger spring constant k mean — a stiffer spring or a softer spring?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. 4 cm — doubling the force doubles the extension (Hooke's law: F ∝ x).
2. F = kx, where F is the force (N), k is the spring constant (N/m), and x is the extension (m).
3. A larger k means a stiffer spring — it takes more force to produce the same extension.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the teacher hangs a rubber strip from a stand, marks the bottom end with a marker, and hangs one water bottle on it. The class sees the strip stretch. Add a second bottle — it stretches more. Ask: *if one bottle stretches it 2 cm, how far do two bottles stretch it?* Take predictions. Board: **Hooke's law — F = kx** — the extension is proportional to the force, as long as we stay in the elastic region (Lesson 278). Double the force, double the stretch.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the Hooke's law tester (see Hands-On Build). They hang the rubber strip, mark the starting length, and add water bottles one at a time. For each bottle they measure the extension and compute the force (each 500 ml bottle = 0.5 kg = 4.9 N). They record: number of bottles | mass (kg) | force (N) | extension (cm). They test with 1, 2, 3, and 4 bottles. Then they plot force (y-axis, N) vs extension (x-axis, m) on graph paper. The graph should be a straight line through the origin — the proportional (elastic) region. They compute the gradient = k = F/x.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Hooke's law: **F = kx**. The force is proportional to the extension — the graph is a straight line through the origin. The gradient of the line is the **spring constant k**, measured in N/m. A stiffer spring has a larger k (more force per unit extension). The law only holds in the **elastic region** — beyond the yield point (Lesson 278), the graph curves and the material is permanently deformed. Board: **k = gradient = F/x = ΔF/Δx**. Units: F in newtons (N), x in metres (m), k in N/m. Example: if 4.9 N stretches the strip 0.02 m, then k = 4.9/0.02 = 245 N/m. Confront the misconception that "k is the slope of the spring" — k is the slope of the *force-extension graph*, a property of the material and its shape, not of the spring alone. Also confront "heavier loads always stretch more proportionally" — only in the elastic region; beyond the yield point the proportionality breaks down.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *F = kx; the gradient is k; it only works in the elastic region*. Homework: complete the force-extension graph and compute k from the gradient. Bring the graph to the next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Water-Bottle Hooke's Law Tester
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- One rubber strip (~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long) from an inner tube
- Four identical empty water bottles (500 ml or 1 L)
- Water — to fill the bottles as weights
- A marker pen
- A ruler or tape measure
- A stand, hook, or sturdy branch
- Graph paper or squared notebook
- Calculator (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Hang the rubber strip from the stand or branch. Mark the bottom end with the marker — this is the zero point. **Checkpoint:** the strip hangs straight and is not twisted.
2. Fill one bottle with water (500 ml = 0.5 kg). Hang it on the strip. Wait for it to settle. Measure the extension from the zero mark. **Checkpoint:** measure from the mark to the new position of the bottle's hook — the extension, not the total length.
3. Record: 1 bottle | 0.5 kg | force = 0.5 × 9.8 = 4.9 N | extension = ___ cm. **Checkpoint:** convert cm to m for the graph (e.g., 2 cm = 0.02 m).
4. Add a second bottle (total 1.0 kg = 9.8 N). Measure the new extension. Record. **Checkpoint:** the extension should be roughly double the first one — if not, the strip may be non-linear or the bottles are not identical.
5. Add a third bottle (1.5 kg = 14.7 N) and a fourth (2.0 kg = 19.6 N). Measure and record each time. **Checkpoint:** if the extension suddenly jumps more than expected, you have passed the elastic region — stop adding weight.
6. Plot force (N) on the y-axis vs extension (m) on the x-axis. Draw the best-fit straight line through the origin. **Checkpoint:** the points should form a straight line through the origin in the elastic region.
7. Calculate the gradient: k = ΔF/Δx. Pick two points on the line (not data points) and compute rise/run. **Checkpoint:** k is in N/m. If the extension is 0.04 m at 9.8 N, k = 9.8/0.04 = 245 N/m.

### Common Mistakes
- **Measuring total length instead of extension** — extension is the *extra* stretch beyond the natural length, not the full length. Always measure from the zero mark.
- **Using cm instead of m on the graph** — if you plot extension in cm, the gradient is in N/cm, not N/m. Convert to metres first (divide by 100) or multiply the gradient by 100 at the end.
- **Going past the elastic region** — if the strip stretches too much, the graph curves and Hooke's law no longer holds. Use small loads (under 2 kg for a 2 cm strip) and keep extensions under 5 cm.
- **Non-identical bottles** — if the bottles hold different amounts of water, the forces are not equal. Fill them to the same level. Use the same type of bottle for all four.
- **Not waiting for the strip to settle** — rubber oscillates when loaded. Wait 2–3 seconds for it to stop bouncing before measuring.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A 1 kg mass stretches a spring 2 cm. How far does 2 kg stretch it? (Assume elastic.) (mirrors pre-test)
2. Write Hooke's law and state what each symbol means with units. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A force of 10 N stretches a rubber strip 0.05 m. Calculate k.
4. The force-extension graph for a spring is a straight line through the origin. What does this tell you?
5. Challenge: a spring has k = 200 N/m. What force is needed to stretch it 3 cm? What mass would produce this force?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 4 cm — doubling the force doubles the extension (F ∝ x in the elastic region).
2. F = kx. F = force in newtons (N), k = spring constant in N/m, x = extension in metres (m).
3. k = F/x = 10/0.05 = 200 N/m.
4. The straight line through the origin means the extension is proportional to the force — Hooke's law holds. The material is in its elastic region.
5. F = kx = 200 × 0.03 = 6 N. Mass: m = F/g = 6/9.8 = 0.61 kg ≈ 610 g.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** From your graph, compute k in N/m. Then predict the extension for a 2.5 kg load and test it. Does the prediction match?
   **Worked Answer:** Example data: 0.5 kg (4.9 N) → 0.02 m; 1.0 kg (9.8 N) → 0.04 m; 1.5 kg (14.7 N) → 0.06 m; 2.0 kg (19.6 N) → 0.08 m. The graph is a straight line through the origin. Gradient k = ΔF/Δx = (19.6 − 4.9)/(0.08 − 0.02) = 14.7/0.06 = 245 N/m. Prediction for 2.5 kg: F = 2.5 × 9.8 = 24.5 N. x = F/k = 24.5/245 = 0.10 m = 10 cm. Test: hang 2.5 kg and measure. If the measured extension is close to 10 cm, the prediction holds and the strip is still in the elastic region. If the extension is much more than 10 cm, the strip has gone non-linear (past the elastic region) and Hooke's law no longer applies — this is the topic of Lesson 280.
2. **Q:** Two rubber strips have k₁ = 200 N/m and k₂ = 500 N/m. Which is stiffer? What force stretches each by 5 cm?
   **Worked Answer:** k₂ = 500 N/m is stiffer — it takes more force per unit extension. For x = 5 cm = 0.05 m: F₁ = k₁x = 200 × 0.05 = 10 N. F₂ = k₂x = 500 × 0.05 = 25 N. The stiffer spring (k₂) needs 2.5× more force for the same extension. This is why a car suspension spring (high k) barely moves under the car's weight, while a rubber band (low k) stretches easily.
3. **Q:** A force-extension graph starts as a straight line but curves upward at high force. Explain what is happening physically.
   **Worked Answer:** The straight-line region is the elastic region — Hooke's law holds, F ∝ x, and the material returns to its original length when the force is removed. The upward curve at high force means the extension is growing faster than the force — the material is yielding. This is the **yield point** (Lesson 278): the material has passed from elastic to plastic deformation. Beyond this point, the material develops a permanent set and does not return to its original length. For rubber, the curve may curve the other way (stiffening) at very high stretch — this is the non-Hookean tail, studied in Lesson 280. For metals like wire, the curve flattens (extension grows faster than force) as the material yields and flows plastically.

## Safety Notes
- Do not overload the rubber strip — if it snaps, the bottles can fall on feet. Use a soft surface (grass or a mat) under the hanging bottles.
- Do not stretch the strip near your face — if it slips it can whip back.
- Do not use bottles larger than 1 L — the weights get too heavy for a thin rubber strip and the strip may exceed its elastic limit or break.
- Water bottles can roll off desks — place them on the floor when not in use.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 278 introduced elastic and plastic deformation and the yield point. This lesson uses the elastic region to discover Hooke's law quantitatively. Lesson 280 will extend this to the non-Hookean (non-linear) behaviour of rubber at high stretch.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the physical observation — more bottles = more stretch, and the numbers roughly double. They should see the straight line on the graph. Ages 14–17 should compute k from the gradient, predict the extension for a new load, and test the prediction.
- Local resource tips: rubber strips from inner tubes are free at any vulcaniser. Water bottles are everywhere — use 500 ml bottles for smaller forces or 1 L bottles for larger. A branch, nail in a wall, or the edge of a desk can serve as the stand. Graph paper is in any squared notebook.
- The key result is the straight-line graph through the origin. If the graph curves, the loads were too heavy or the strip was already stretched. Use small loads and a fresh strip.
- This lesson directly sets up Lesson 280 (Spring Constant Lab), where learners measure k for multiple strips and discover that rubber is non-Hookean at high stretch. Keep the graphs — they will be compared in the next lesson.
