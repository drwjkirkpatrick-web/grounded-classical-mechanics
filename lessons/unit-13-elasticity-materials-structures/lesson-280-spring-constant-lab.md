# Lesson 280: Spring Constant Lab: Calibrating Rubber

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Measure the spring constant k for three different rubber strips using the force-extension method.
2. Discover that rubber stiffens at high stretch — the force-extension graph curves upward (non-Hookean tail).
3. Report k as an honest range with the valid force window, not a single number.
4. Explain the non-Hookean tail using the polymer chain alignment model.

## Materials
- Three rubber strips of different widths or thicknesses (e.g., 1 cm, 2 cm, 3 cm wide, all ~20 cm long) from inner tubes
- Water bottles (500 ml or 1 L) — at least four identical bottles
- Water — to fill the bottles as weights
- A marker pen — to mark each strip's starting length
- A ruler or tape measure
- A stand, hook, or sturdy branch
- Graph paper or squared notebook
- Calculator (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **spring constant (k)** (ugumu wa springi) — the stiffness of the strip in N/m; measured as the gradient of the force-extension graph
- **non-Hookean** (haifuati sheria ya Hooke) — the material does not follow F = kx; the graph curves
- **stiffening tail** (ukakamavu wa mwisho) — at high stretch, rubber gets harder to pull; the graph curves upward
- **polymer chain** (nyuzi za polima) — long tangled molecules in rubber that straighten under stretch
- **valid force window** (dirisha la nguvu halali) — the range of forces where k is roughly constant and Hooke's law holds

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Write Hooke's law. What does the graph of force vs extension look like in the elastic region?
2. A rubber strip has k = 200 N/m. What force stretches it 3 cm?
3. If you pull a rubber strip very hard, does it get easier or harder to pull as it stretches more?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. F = kx. In the elastic region, the graph is a straight line through the origin — force is proportional to extension.
2. F = kx = 200 × 0.03 = 6 N.
3. Harder — rubber stiffens at high stretch. The polymer chains straighten and resist further extension, so the force grows faster than the extension. The graph curves upward.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — recap: in Lesson 279, the force-extension graph was a straight line — Hooke's law held. Ask: *does the straight line go on forever?* The teacher takes a rubber strip, stretches it a little (easy), then stretches it a lot (much harder). The class feels the difference: at first it pulls easily, then it gets very stiff. Board: **rubber is non-Hookean at high stretch — the graph curves upward**. Today we measure k for three strips and find where the straight line ends.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs test all three rubber strips (see Hands-On Build). For each strip they: (a) mark the starting length, (b) add water bottles one at a time, measuring extension at each load, (c) keep going until the graph clearly curves, (d) plot force vs extension and identify the straight (Hookean) region and the curved (non-Hookean) tail. They compute k from the gradient of the straight region only. They record: strip | width | k (N/m) | valid force window (N). Results go on a class table comparing the three strips.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. In the **Hookean region** (small extensions), F = kx and the graph is a straight line — the gradient is k. In the **non-Hookean tail** (large extensions), the graph curves upward — rubber gets stiffer. Why? Rubber is made of **polymer chains** — long, tangled molecules like cooked spaghetti. At small stretch, the chains uncoil easily (low force, easy extension — Hookean). At large stretch, the chains are fully straightened and cannot uncoil further — pulling them apart requires much more force (high force, small extra extension — the graph curves upward). Board: **k is only valid in the Hookean region — report it as a range with the force window**. Example: "k = 200–250 N/m for forces of 5–15 N; above 15 N the strip is non-Hookean." Confront the misconception that "k is a fixed property of the material" — k depends on the strip's width, thickness, length, and the force range. A single number for k is dishonest; a range with a window is honest.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *rubber follows Hooke's law only at small stretch; report k as a range with a valid force window*. Homework: write up the three-strip results table with k values and force windows, and bring it to the next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Three-Strip Calibration Rig
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Three rubber strips of different widths (1 cm, 2 cm, 3 cm) from inner tubes, all ~20 cm long
- Four identical water bottles (500 ml or 1 L)
- Water — to fill the bottles as weights
- A marker pen
- A ruler or tape measure
- A stand, hook, or sturdy branch
- Graph paper or squared notebook
- Calculator (phone)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Hang the first strip (1 cm wide) from the stand. Mark the bottom end — the zero point. **Checkpoint:** the strip hangs straight and is not twisted.
2. Add one bottle (0.5 kg = 4.9 N). Wait for it to settle. Measure the extension from the zero mark. Record: force (N) | extension (m). **Checkpoint:** measure extension, not total length.
3. Add bottles one at a time. After each bottle, measure and record. Keep going until the extension grows much more than expected for an added bottle — that is the non-Hookean tail. **Checkpoint:** if the strip stretches more than 8 cm, stop — you are deep in the non-Hookean region and the strip may tear.
4. Plot force (N) vs extension (m) on graph paper. Identify the straight-line region (Hookean) and the curved region (non-Hookean tail). **Checkpoint:** the straight region is near the origin; the curve bends upward at higher force.
5. Compute k from the gradient of the straight region only: k = ΔF/Δx. Pick two points on the straight part of the line. **Checkpoint:** do not use points from the curved region — that would give a wrong k.
6. Record the valid force window: the range of forces where the graph is straight. Example: "straight from 0 to 15 N; k = 240 N/m; valid window 0–15 N." **Checkpoint:** the window is the range where Hooke's law holds.
7. Repeat for the 2 cm and 3 cm strips. Compare: does the wider strip have a larger k? **Checkpoint:** wider strips should be stiffer (larger k) because more material shares the load.
8. Fill in the class table: strip width | k (N/m) | valid force window (N) | notes.

### Common Mistakes
- **Computing k from the whole graph including the curve** — k must be computed from the straight (Hookean) region only. Using the curved tail gives a wrong, inflated k.
- **Reporting k as a single number** — rubber's k changes with the force range. Report k as a range (e.g., 230–250 N/m) with the valid force window (e.g., 0–15 N). A single number without a window is misleading.
- **Not going far enough to see the curve** — if you stop at 2 bottles, you only see the straight line and miss the non-Hookean tail. Go to at least 4 bottles or until the curve is visible.
- **Going too far and tearing the strip** — if you stretch beyond 10 cm on a 20 cm strip, it may tear. Stop when the curve is clear.
- **Comparing strips of different lengths** — k depends on length: a longer strip is softer (lower k). Keep all three strips the same length (~20 cm) so the comparison is fair.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Write Hooke's law. What does the force-extension graph look like in the elastic region? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A rubber strip has k = 250 N/m in its Hookean region. What force stretches it 4 cm?
3. What happens to the force-extension graph of rubber at high stretch? Why?
4. Why should you report k as a range with a valid force window, not a single number?
5. Challenge: a 1 cm strip has k = 100 N/m and a 3 cm strip has k = 300 N/m. What does this tell you about the relationship between width and stiffness?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. F = kx. In the elastic region, the graph is a straight line through the origin — force is proportional to extension.
2. F = kx = 250 × 0.04 = 10 N.
3. The graph curves upward — rubber stiffens at high stretch. The polymer chains are fully straightened and resist further extension, so more force is needed for less extra extension.
4. Because rubber's k changes with the force range — it is constant (Hookean) only in the straight-line region and increases in the non-Hookean tail. A single number without a window is misleading because it implies k is fixed for all forces.
5. The 3 cm strip is 3× stiffer than the 1 cm strip. The relationship is roughly linear: tripling the width triples k, because three times as much material shares the load. (In practice it is not perfectly linear due to edge effects, but the trend is clear.)

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Quote k as a range with the valid force window for one of your strips. Explain why the tail curves upward using the polymer chain alignment model.
   **Worked Answer:** Example for a 2 cm strip: In the Hookean region (0–12 N), the gradient is roughly constant: k ≈ 230–250 N/m. Valid force window: 0–12 N. Above 12 N, the graph curves upward — the extension grows more slowly than the force, meaning the strip is getting stiffer. Explanation: rubber is made of long, coiled polymer chains (like cooked spaghetti). At small stretch, the chains uncoil easily — small force, large extension (Hookean, constant k). At large stretch, the chains are fully straightened and cannot uncoil further. Pulling them apart now means stretching the chemical bonds along the chain backbone, which requires much more force. So the force rises sharply for little extra extension — the graph curves upward. This is the **non-Hookean tail**. The material has not yielded (it still returns when released) — it has simply run out of easy uncoiling and hit the hard bond-stretching regime.
2. **Q:** A 1 cm strip gives k = 100 N/m, a 2 cm strip gives k = 195 N/m, and a 3 cm strip gives k = 280 N/m. Is the relationship between width and k perfectly linear? Explain any deviation.
   **Worked Answer:** If perfectly linear, doubling the width should double k: 1 cm → 100, 2 cm → 200, 3 cm → 300. Actual: 2 cm → 195 (not 200), 3 cm → 280 (not 300). The 2 cm strip is 97.5% of the ideal (195/200 = 0.975). The 3 cm strip is 93% of the ideal (280/300 = 0.933). The shortfall grows with width. Two reasons: (a) **edge effects** — the edges of the strip are not fully loaded because the stress concentrates toward the centre, so wider strips are slightly less efficient per unit width; (b) **thickness variation** — cut strips from different parts of the inner tube may have slightly different thicknesses, so the comparison is not perfectly controlled. The trend is approximately linear (k ∝ width) but not perfectly linear.
3. **Q:** A rubber strip follows Hooke's law up to 15 N with k = 240 N/m. Above 15 N it stiffens. Predict the extension at 10 N and at 25 N. Which prediction is reliable and why?
   **Worked Answer:** At 10 N (within the Hookean region): x = F/k = 10/240 = 0.0417 m ≈ 4.2 cm. This prediction is reliable because 10 N is within the valid force window (0–15 N) where Hooke's law holds. At 25 N (above the Hookean region): if we blindly apply x = F/k = 25/240 = 0.104 m ≈ 10.4 cm. But this prediction is **not reliable** because 25 N is above the 15 N window — the strip is non-Hookean. The actual extension will be **less** than 10.4 cm because the strip stiffens (the graph curves upward, so for the same force increment the extension is smaller than the straight line predicts). To predict accurately above 15 N, you would need to fit a curve to the non-Hookean data, not use the straight-line k. This is why reporting k with a force window is essential — using k outside the window gives wrong answers.

## Safety Notes
- Do not overload the rubber strips — at high stretch they can tear and whip back. Stop when the non-Hookean curve is clear (usually under 8 cm extension).
- Do not stretch near faces — a snapping strip can injure eyes.
- Water bottles can fall when the strip tears — use a soft surface underneath and keep feet clear.
- Do not use bottles larger than 1 L — the forces exceed the elastic limit of thin strips.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 279 introduced Hooke's law (F = kx) and the straight-line graph. This lesson extends it by showing where the straight line ends and the non-Hookean tail begins. The polymer chain explanation is the key conceptual advance.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on measuring k for each strip and seeing that wider strips are stiffer. They should observe the curve at high stretch but do not need to explain the polymer model. Ages 14–17 should compute k from the gradient, identify the valid force window, report k as a range, and explain the non-Hookean tail using the polymer chain alignment model.
- Local resource tips: three strips of different widths can be cut from the same inner tube (mark and cut with scissors). Water bottles are the standard weight set used throughout the unit. Graph paper is in any squared notebook.
- The key honest-science message of this lesson is: **report k as a range with a force window, not a single number**. This is a habit of good experimental practice — state the conditions under which your measurement is valid. Emphasise this in the Explain block and in the post-test.
- The polymer chain model (spaghetti uncoiling, then bond-stretching) is a qualitative explanation suitable for this level. Do not introduce the full statistical mechanics of rubber elasticity — the visual model is enough.
- This lesson feeds directly into Lesson 281 (Beyond the Elastic Limit), where learners stretch strips to permanent set and to breaking. The non-Hookean tail studied here is the precursor to yielding and breaking.
