# Lesson 281: Beyond the Elastic Limit

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Stretch a rubber strip past its elastic limit and observe the permanent set — the strip no longer returns to its original length.
2. Distinguish the elastic region (Hooke's law holds, strip recovers) from the plastic region (permanent deformation, strip does not recover).
3. Record the breaking force for at least two materials and describe the mode of failure (ductile stretch vs brittle snap).
4. Explain why engineers stay well below the elastic limit in real structures.

## Materials
- Rubber strips cut from an old inner tube, ~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long — one per pair
- Thin copper wire or galvanised wire, ~30 cm — from a jua kali workshop or hardware
- A ruler or tape measure
- A marker pen
- A bucket or debe with a string loop
- Dry sand, measured in cups (one 500 ml cup ≈ 0.8 kg)
- Safety goggles or sunglasses — for eye protection when strands snap
- A soft landing surface (a folded sack or mat under the bucket)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **elastic limit** (kikomo cha kunyooka) — the maximum stretch a material can take and still return to its original length
- **permanent set** (mabadiliko ya kudumu) — the deformation left after a material is stretched past its elastic limit; it does not recover
- **plastic region** (eneo la plastiki) — beyond the elastic limit; the material deforms permanently before breaking
- **breaking force** (nguvu ya kuvunja) — the force at which the material snaps
- **ductile** (kunyooka) — stretches and thins before breaking, like copper wire
- **brittle** (mena) — snaps with little stretching, like dry sisal or chalk

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You stretch a rubber band and let go. It snaps back. But if you stretch it too far, it stays longer. What is the name of the point where it stops returning?
2. A wire that stretches and thins before snapping is called ______. A material that snaps with no warning is called ______.
3. Why does a bridge engineer keep the load well below the elastic limit of the steel?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The elastic limit — beyond it, the material suffers permanent set and does not return to its original length.
2. Ductile (stretches before breaking, e.g., copper wire). Brittle (snaps with little stretching, e.g., chalk, dry sisal).
3. So the steel stays in the elastic region — it recovers when the load is removed. Past the elastic limit, the bridge would bend permanently and never return to shape, leading to failure.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — take a rubber strip and stretch it gently: it returns. Stretch it hard: it stays longer. Mark the original length on the board, then show the stretched strip — it no longer matches. Ask: *what happened?* Board: **elastic limit** — the border between "recovers" and "stays deformed." Beyond it lies the **plastic region**, ending in **breaking**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs stretch a rubber strip in stages and record the permanent set (see Hands-On Build). They mark the original length, add load cup by cup, remove the load after each cup, and measure the new length. They find the cup count where the strip first fails to return. Then they continue loading until the strip breaks, recording the breaking force. They repeat with copper wire and note the difference: wire stretches and thins (ductile) before snapping; rubber may snap more suddenly if old. Results go on a class table: material | elastic limit (cups) | breaking force (cups) | mode of failure.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The load-extension graph has two regions: (a) the **elastic region** — a straight line through the origin (Hooke's law, F = kx); remove the load and the material returns. (b) the **plastic region** — the line curves; remove the load and the material keeps a permanent set. The **elastic limit** is the boundary. Beyond it, the internal structure (molecular bonds in rubber, atomic planes in metal) has slipped and cannot return. Eventually the material **necks** (thins locally) and **breaks**. Ductile materials (copper wire) show a long plastic region with visible necking — a warning. Brittle materials (chalk, dry sisal) have almost no plastic region — they snap without warning. Engineers design structures to stay in the elastic region with a large safety margin, so everyday loads never cause permanent set. Confront the misconception that "stronger means it stretches more" — a strong material resists stretching; a ductile one stretches before breaking. Strength and ductility are different properties.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *past the elastic limit, deformation is permanent; past the breaking force, it fails*. Homework: find one object at home that has been permanently bent or stretched past its elastic limit (a bent sufuria, a stretched sandal strap, a dented debe) and write what happened.

## Hands-On Build: Stretch-to-Break Tester
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- One rubber strip ~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long, from an inner tube
- One thin copper or galvanised wire ~30 cm
- A ruler and marker pen
- A bucket or debe with a string loop
- Dry sand, measured in cups (one 500 ml cup ≈ 0.8 kg)
- Safety goggles or sunglasses
- A folded sack or mat under the bucket
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Mark the rubber strip at two points 15 cm apart (the gauge length). Hang the strip from a fixed point (a nail or broom handle). **Checkpoint:** the marks are visible and the strip hangs straight.
2. Tie the bucket to the bottom of the strip. Add one cup of sand. Remove the bucket. Measure the distance between the marks. **Checkpoint:** the strip returns to 15 cm — still in the elastic region.
3. Add one more cup. Remove the bucket. Measure again. Repeat cup by cup. **Checkpoint:** at some cup count (typically 3–5 cups for a 2 cm strip), the strip no longer returns to 15 cm — you have passed the elastic limit. Record the permanent set (e.g., 15.5 cm).
4. Continue adding cups without removing the bucket. Watch for necking — the strip thins locally. **Checkpoint:** the strip stretches faster now (the plastic region — less force needed for more extension).
5. Record the cup count when the strip breaks. Note the mode: did it neck and stretch first (ductile) or snap suddenly (brittle)? **Checkpoint:** the breaking force is recorded; the bucket lands on the soft mat.
6. Repeat steps 1–5 with copper wire. Compare: wire necks visibly and stretches a long way before snapping; rubber may snap sooner if old or perished.
7. Tabulate on the class table: material | elastic limit (cups) | permanent set at break (cm) | breaking force (cups) | mode of failure.

### Common Mistakes
- **Adding sand too fast** — a sudden cup is an impact load, not a static one. Add slowly and wait 3 seconds after each cup.
- **Not removing the bucket to check recovery** — you must remove the load after each cup to see whether the strip returns. Only after finding the elastic limit do you continue loading to break.
- **Old, perished rubber** — cracked inner-tube rubber snaps early and gives a false brittle result. Use supple black rubber.
- **No soft landing** — a bucket of sand falling from height can bruise a foot. Place a folded sack underneath.
- **No eye protection** — wire whips when it snaps. Wear goggles or sunglasses and keep faces away.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What is the elastic limit? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A wire that stretches and thins before snapping is called ______. A material that snaps with no warning is called ______. (mirrors pre-test)
3. You stretch a rubber strip to 18 cm (original 15 cm) and let go. It returns to 15.5 cm. What happened?
4. Why do engineers keep bridge loads well below the elastic limit of the steel?
5. Challenge: a copper wire breaks at 40 N with a cross-section of 0.2 mm². Calculate the breaking stress in MPa.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The elastic limit is the maximum stretch a material can take and still return to its original length. Beyond it, the material suffers permanent set.
2. Ductile (stretches before breaking). Brittle (snaps with little stretching).
3. The strip was stretched past its elastic limit — it has a permanent set of 0.5 cm (15.5 − 15 = 0.5 cm). It did not return to its original length.
4. So the steel stays in the elastic region and recovers when the load is removed. Past the elastic limit, the bridge would bend permanently, leading to progressive failure. The safety margin ensures everyday loads never cause permanent deformation.
5. A = 0.2 mm² = 0.2 × 10⁻⁶ m² = 2 × 10⁻⁷ m². Breaking stress σ = F/A = 40 / (2 × 10⁻⁷) = 2 × 10⁸ Pa = 200 MPa.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Define breaking stress. A rubber strip of cross-section 4 mm² breaks at 12 N. Compute the breaking stress and compare it with steel wire (breaking stress ~1000 MPa).
   **Worked Answer:** Breaking stress is the stress at which a material snaps — σ_break = F_break / A. It is a property of the material, not the size. For the rubber strip: A = 4 mm² = 4 × 10⁻⁶ m². σ = 12 / (4 × 10⁻⁶) = 3 × 10⁶ Pa = 3 MPa. Steel wire: σ_break ≈ 1000 MPa. Ratio: 1000/3 ≈ 333. Steel is ~333× stronger per unit area than the rubber strip. This is why load-bearing structures use steel, not rubber — rubber's strength comes from its large stretch (strain), not from resisting high stress.
2. **Q:** A load-extension graph for copper wire shows a straight line to 30 N, then a curve to 45 N (breaking). Identify the elastic and plastic regions, the elastic limit, and explain what happens to the bonds in each region.
   **Worked Answer:** Elastic region: 0–30 N (the straight line, Hooke's law holds). Plastic region: 30–45 N (the curve, permanent deformation). The elastic limit is at 30 N. In the elastic region, the atomic bonds stretch but do not slip — remove the load and the atoms spring back. In the plastic region, the atomic planes slide past each other (dislocation movement) — the bonds break and reform in new positions, so the deformation is permanent. At 45 N, the necking becomes severe: the cross-section shrinks, the local stress rises even though the total force is constant, and the wire snaps. The straight line (elastic) has a constant slope k = F/x; the curve (plastic) has a decreasing slope — less force is needed for each additional unit of extension because the material is already yielding.
3. **Q:** A boda-boda brake cable is designed with a safety factor of 3. The cable breaks at 900 N. What is the maximum working load, and why is the safety factor necessary?
   **Worked Answer:** Safety factor = breaking load / working load. So working load = breaking load / safety factor = 900 / 3 = 300 N. The safety factor ensures the cable never approaches its elastic limit in normal use. A brake cable experiences dynamic loads (sudden braking) that can spike above the static load. With a safety factor of 3, even a 3× spike stays below the breaking load. Without the margin, a single hard brake could push the cable past its elastic limit — it would stretch permanently, the brake would go slack, and the next brake would fail completely. The safety factor is the engineer's way of keeping real-world loads in the elastic region despite uncertainty.

## Safety Notes
- Wire whips violently when it snaps — wear goggles or sunglasses and keep all faces at least 1 m away from the test area.
- The bucket of sand falls when the strip breaks — place a folded sack or mat underneath and keep feet clear.
- Do not exceed 6 cups of sand on a single rubber strip — if it has not broken, stop and record the result as "exceeded test range."
- Copper wire ends are sharp after snapping — do not touch the broken ends with bare hands; use pliers to pick them up.
- Do not stand under the bucket at any point during loading.
