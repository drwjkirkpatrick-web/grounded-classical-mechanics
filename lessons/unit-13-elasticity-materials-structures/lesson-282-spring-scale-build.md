# Lesson 282: Build: A Calibrated Spring Scale I

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** spring-scale

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Refine the Unit 2 rubber-strip force meter into a calibrated spring scale with a better pointer, a card scale, and a zero-adjustment screw.
2. Calibrate the scale from 0 to 10 N using water as the known-force standard (1 litre ≈ 10 N).
3. Explain why zero adjustment is necessary and how temperature or ageing changes the strip's rest length.
4. Test the scale against a known mass and report the reading error.

## Materials
- A rubber strip ~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long, from an old inner tube (the "spring")
- A wooden frame or sturdy cardboard box (~30 cm tall) — to hold the scale vertically
- Stiff card or a piece of carton box, ~25 cm × 6 cm — for the scale face
- Two large paperclips — one as a hook, one as a pointer
- A short bolt or screw with two nuts — for the zero-adjustment (or a tight string loop that can be re-tied)
- String — to hang the frame and the calibration masses
- A 1-litre bottle and water — the calibration standard
- A measuring jug or 500 ml bottle — for half-litre steps
- A marker pen (fine tip) and a ruler
- A reference spring balance (if available) — for comparison
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **calibrate** (kalibrisha) — to mark a scale using known forces so the readings are true
- **zero adjustment** (marekebisho ya sifuri) — a way to set the pointer to 0 N with no load, so the scale reads true even if the strip stretches over time
- **pointer** (sheria) — the indicator that moves along the scale as the strip stretches
- **scale face** (kioo ya skeli) — the card with marked divisions (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 N)
- **nonlinear** (isio mstari) — when equal force steps do not give equal pointer steps; the scale is not a straight line

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. In Unit 2 you built a rubber-strip force meter. What was the calibration standard — the known force you used to mark the scale?
2. If your force meter reads 0 N but there is already a small mass on the hook, what is wrong and how would you fix it?
3. Why might a rubber strip give a nonlinear scale (uneven spacing) at large forces?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Water — 1 litre of water has a mass of 1 kg and weighs about 10 N. So 1 litre = 10 N, 2 litres = 20 N, etc.
2. The zero is wrong — the pointer does not point to 0 N with no load. Fix: adjust the top attachment (a screw or string loop) so the pointer sits at 0 N when nothing is hanging. This is zero adjustment.
3. Rubber softens at large extensions — the molecular chains uncoil and the stiffness k decreases, so each extra newton gives more extension than the last. The scale spacing widens at the top instead of staying even.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the Unit 2 force meter (or recall it). Ask: *what was wrong with it?* Common answers: the pointer wobbled, the zero drifted, the scale was guessed. Today we build a better one — a **calibrated spring scale** with a stiff pointer, a card scale marked in 2 N steps, and a zero-adjustment screw. Board: **calibrate with water, adjust zero, mark every 2 N**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the refined spring scale (see Hands-On Build). They mount the strip in the frame, add the pointer and card, set the zero with the adjustment screw, then calibrate with water: 0 L → 0 N, 0.5 L → 5 N, 1 L → 10 N. They mark every 2 N by interpolation (or by adding 200 ml steps). They then weigh a mystery object (a stone in a bag) and compare with a reference scale if available. Results go on a class table: team | mystery reading (N) | reference reading (N) | error (N).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. A calibrated spring scale has three improvements over the Unit 2 meter: (a) a **stiff pointer** (a paperclip bent straight, taped to the strip) — it does not wobble, so readings are repeatable; (b) a **card scale** marked from known forces, not guessed — every 2 N is a real mark, not an assumption; (c) a **zero adjustment** — a screw or string loop at the top that lets you re-set 0 N if the strip ages or the temperature changes. Rubber creeps (stretches slowly under constant load) and softens in heat, so the zero drifts over days. Zero adjustment fixes this in seconds. Confront the misconception that "a spring scale measures mass" — it measures force (weight). The kg marks on a market scale are the force divided by g ≈ 10 N/kg. If g changed (on the Moon), the same scale would read a different mass for the same object. Also address nonlinearity: if the 5 N and 10 N marks are not evenly spaced, the strip is softening — do not assume even spacing between them. Mark each known point and read from the actual marks.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *calibrate with water, adjust zero, mark every 2 N*. Homework: weigh three objects at home with your scale and write the readings. Bring the scale to the next lesson — we will certify it.

## Hands-On Build: Calibrated Spring Scale (0–10 N)
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Gather parts the day before.*

### Parts
- Rubber strip ~2 cm wide, ~20 cm long, from an inner tube
- A wooden frame or sturdy cardboard box (~30 cm tall)
- Stiff card ~25 cm × 6 cm (from a carton box)
- Two large paperclips — one as a hook, one as a pointer
- A short bolt or screw with two nuts — for zero adjustment (or a tight string loop)
- String — to hang the frame
- A 1-litre bottle and water
- A measuring jug or 500 ml bottle — for half-litre steps
- A marker pen (fine tip) and a ruler
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Build the frame: a vertical post or a box with the top open, so the strip can hang straight down beside the card. **Checkpoint:** the frame is stable and does not tip when loaded.
2. Attach the zero-adjustment screw at the top: drill or poke a hole, pass the bolt through, and clamp the top of the rubber strip between two nuts. By turning the nut, you can raise or lower the strip by a few millimetres. (If no bolt, use a string loop that can be re-tied higher or lower.) **Checkpoint:** the strip hangs vertically and the screw can move it up or down.
3. Tape the stiff card to the frame beside the strip, so the pointer will sweep along it. **Checkpoint:** the card is vertical and close to the strip but not touching it.
4. Bend one paperclip into a hook and attach it to the bottom of the strip. Bend the other into a straight pointer and tape it to the strip just above the hook, pointing sideways at the card. **Checkpoint:** the pointer is stiff and points cleanly at the card — no wobble.
5. With no load on the hook, turn the zero-adjustment screw until the pointer sits exactly at the bottom of the card. Mark this position "0 N" with the marker. **Checkpoint:** the pointer sits at 0 N with no load — zero is set.
6. Hang 0.5 L of water (in the bottle, ~5 N). The pointer moves down. Mark the new position "5 N." **Checkpoint:** the pointer is steady — wait 2 seconds for it to settle before marking.
7. Hang 1 L of water (~10 N). Mark the new position "10 N." **Checkpoint:** the strip is not over-stretched — if it is near the elastic limit, do not go further. Stay within 0–10 N.
8. Divide the 0–5 N and 5–10 N gaps into 2 N steps by eye (or add 200 ml steps for 2 N each). Mark 2, 4, 6, 8 N. **Checkpoint:** if the 5–10 N gap is wider than the 0–5 N gap, the strip is nonlinear — mark the actual positions, do not assume even spacing.
9. Remove the water. Check that the pointer returns to 0 N. If it sits above or below, re-adjust the zero screw. **Checkpoint:** the zero is repeatable — remove and re-add a load and the pointer returns to 0 N.
10. Weigh the mystery object (a stone in a bag). Record the reading. If a reference scale is available, compare. **Checkpoint:** the reading should be within 1–2 N of the reference. If not, check the zero and the calibration marks.

### Common Mistakes
- **No zero adjustment** — without it, the zero drifts and every reading is wrong. Always set zero with no load before calibrating and before measuring.
- **Strip touching the card** — friction between the strip and the card makes the pointer stick. The strip must hang free, 1–2 cm from the card.
- **Wobbly pointer** — a loose pointer gives different readings each time. Tape it firmly so it is stiff.
- **Assuming even spacing** — rubber softens at high force, so the 5–10 N gap may be wider than 0–5 N. Mark the actual positions of known forces; do not divide evenly.
- **Overstretching** — if you load past 10 N and the strip does not return to 0, you have passed the elastic limit. Use a fresh strip and stay within 0–10 N.
- **Reading at an angle** — read the pointer with your eye level with it. Reading from above or below shifts the apparent position.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What known force standard did you use to calibrate the spring scale? (mirrors pre-test)
2. The pointer sits at 0.5 N with no load. What is wrong and how do you fix it? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why might the 5–10 N marks be wider apart than the 0–5 N marks on your scale?
4. Your scale reads 6 N for a stone. A reference scale reads 7 N. What is the error, and what is one likely cause?
5. Challenge: your scale reads 4% low at 8 N. What is the true force, and what would you do to correct the scale?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Water — 1 litre weighs about 10 N, so 0.5 L = 5 N and 1 L = 10 N. The marks are placed at the pointer positions for these known forces.
2. The zero is wrong — the pointer does not sit at 0 N with no load. Fix: turn the zero-adjustment screw (or re-tie the string loop) until the pointer sits at 0 N with nothing on the hook.
3. Rubber softens at large extensions — the stiffness k decreases, so each extra newton gives more extension. The scale spacing widens at the top. This is nonlinearity.
4. Error = 6 − 7 = −1 N (the scale reads 1 N low). Likely cause: the zero has drifted (the strip crept), or the calibration marks were placed with the strip warm (now it is cold and stiffer), or the pointer is bent.
5. If the scale reads 4% low at 8 N, the true force is 8 / 0.96 ≈ 8.33 N. To correct: re-calibrate at 8 N (hang 0.83 L of water, ~8.3 N) and mark the actual pointer position, or build a correction table (measured → true) from several known points.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Explain how adding a second identical rubber strip in parallel changes the spring constant k. Verify with a measurement.
   **Worked Answer:** Two identical strips in parallel share the load — each carries half the force for the same extension. So the total force for a given extension doubles: F_total = 2 × (k × x) = (2k) × x. The effective spring constant is 2k. Verification: calibrate the single strip (mark 5 N and 10 N). Add a second identical strip beside the first, both attached to the same hook. Hang 5 N of water — the pointer should move to the old 2.5 N position (half the extension), confirming k has doubled. Hang 10 N — the pointer should move to the old 5 N position. The scale now reads 0–20 N with the same card, but each mark represents twice the force. This is why parallel springs (or rubber strips) are used when a stiffer, higher-capacity scale is needed.
2. **Q:** Your scale reads 4% low at 8 N but true at 2 N. Model the fault as a nonlinear strip and propose a correction table.
   **Worked Answer:** At 2 N the scale is true, so the 0–2 N region is linear (k is constant). At 8 N the scale reads 4% low: the pointer is at the 7.68 N position (8 × 0.96 = 7.68), so the true 8 N gives less extension than the linear scale predicts. This means the strip is stiffening at high force (k is increasing), not softening — the opposite of typical rubber. Possible cause: the strip is thick or reinforced, or it is nearing a non-Hookean region where the polymer chains are fully aligned and resist further stretch. Correction table: measure the pointer position for known forces (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 N using water), and record: scale reading → true force. Example: 2 N → 2.0 N, 4 N → 4.2 N, 6 N → 6.5 N, 8 N → 8.3 N, 10 N → 10.6 N (estimated from the 4% error at 8 N, linearly interpolated). The user reads the scale, then looks up the true force in the table. This is how real non-linear force gauges are corrected.
3. **Q:** A steel spring has k = 50 N/m. A rubber strip has k = 10 N/m. Which makes a more sensitive scale for measuring small forces (0–2 N), and why?
   **Worked Answer:** The rubber strip. Sensitivity = extension per unit force = 1/k. The rubber strip gives 1/10 = 0.1 m/N = 10 cm/N — a 2 N load moves the pointer 20 cm, which is easy to read. The steel spring gives 1/50 = 0.02 m/N = 2 cm/N — a 2 N load moves the pointer only 4 cm, which is harder to read precisely. So the rubber strip is more sensitive (more pointer movement per newton). The trade-off: the rubber strip is nonlinear and creeps, so it is less accurate over time. The steel spring is linear and stable, so it is more accurate but less sensitive. For a 0–2 N scale, use rubber (sensitivity matters more than linearity). For a 0–100 N scale, use steel (linearity and stability matter more than sensitivity).

## Safety Notes
- Do not load the strip past 10 N — if it is near the elastic limit, it may snap and whip. Stay within the calibrated range.
- A 1-litre bottle dropped on a foot hurts — hang loads low over a soft surface and keep toes clear.
- Paperclip hooks have sharp ends once unbent — bend them fully closed so no point faces skin.
- Spilled water on a mud or cement floor is slippery — mop at once.
- If using a bolt for zero adjustment, the nut edges can pinch fingers — turn it gently.
