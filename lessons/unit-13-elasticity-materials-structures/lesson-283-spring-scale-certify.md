# Lesson 283: Build: A Calibrated Spring Scale II — Certification

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** spring-scale

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Blind-test their spring scale against five mystery masses and record the measured force for each.
2. Compare each measured value against a reference scale and compute the error and percentage error.
3. Build an error table showing measured, reference, error, and percentage error for all five masses.
4. Issue an "inspection certificate" for their scale stating the range, the maximum error, and whether it passes or fails a tolerance (±10%).

## Materials
- The calibrated spring scale built in Lesson 282 (one per pair)
- Five mystery masses — prepared by the teacher in sealed bags or wrapped in cloth (e.g., a stone, a bundle of nails, a small bottle of water, a lump of clay, a metal piece) — each labelled A–E
- A reference spring balance or hanging scale (if available) — or the teacher's own calibrated scale
- A weighing scale (kitchen scale or market balance) — to determine the true mass of each mystery object, if no reference force meter is available
- A piece of card or paper for the inspection certificate
- A marker pen
- Notebook and pencil
- Calculator (phone) — optional

## Vocabulary
- **blind test** (mtihani wa kipofu) — testing a scale without knowing the true value, so you cannot adjust the answer to match
- **error** (kasoro) — the difference between the measured value and the true value: error = measured − true
- **percentage error** (kasoro ya asilimia) — error as a percentage of the true value: (error / true) × 100%
- **tolerance** (uvumilivu) — the maximum acceptable error; if the error is within tolerance, the scale passes
- **inspection certificate** (cheti cha ukaguzi) — a document stating the scale's range, maximum error, and pass/fail status

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Your scale reads 7 N for a mass. The true force is 8 N. What is the error? What is the percentage error?
2. Why is a blind test better than a test where you know the true value in advance?
3. If a scale has a maximum error of 0.5 N over a 0–10 N range, is it acceptable for a tolerance of ±10%? Show your working.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Error = 7 − 8 = −1 N (the scale reads 1 N low). Percentage error = (−1 / 8) × 100% = −12.5%. The scale reads 12.5% low.
2. In a blind test you do not know the true value, so you cannot unconsciously adjust your reading to match. It tests the scale honestly.
3. Tolerance of ±10% over 0–10 N means the maximum acceptable error is 10% × 10 N = 1 N. The scale's maximum error is 0.5 N, which is less than 1 N. So it passes.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show a real inspection certificate or a market scale's verification stamp. Ask: *how do you know a scale is telling the truth?* In Kenya, the Weights and Measures Agency stamps market scales every year. Today, learners are the inspectors — they will blind-test their own scales and issue certificates. Board: **blind test → error table → certificate**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs collect the five mystery masses (A–E) from the teacher's table. Without knowing the true values, they weigh each one on their spring scale and record the reading. Then the teacher reveals the true values (measured on the reference scale or computed from mass × g). Pairs fill in the error table: mass | measured (N) | true (N) | error (N) | % error. They compute the maximum error and check it against the tolerance (±10% of the full-scale range, i.e., ±1 N for a 0–10 N scale). They issue an inspection certificate: scale ID, range, max error, pass/fail (see Hands-On Build).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. A scale is certified by comparing its readings against known standards (the mystery masses weighed on a reference). The **error** is measured minus true. The **percentage error** normalises this: (error / true) × 100%. A **tolerance** is the maximum acceptable error — for a 0–10 N scale, ±10% means ±1 N. If the maximum error across all five tests is within tolerance, the scale **passes** and gets a certificate. If not, it **fails** and must be re-calibrated or repaired. Confront the misconception that "a good scale has zero error" — no real scale has zero error. The question is whether the error is small enough for the purpose. A market scale with ±2% error is fine for selling sukuma; a laboratory scale with ±2% error is unacceptable. The tolerance depends on the application. Also address systematic vs random error: if all five readings are low by the same amount, the zero has drifted (systematic — fix by re-zeroing). If the readings scatter above and below, the scale is noisy (random — harder to fix, may need a better pointer or a stiffer strip).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *blind test, error table, certificate*. Homework: write one paragraph explaining why a market scale must be certified by the Weights and Measures Agency, and what could go wrong if it is not.

## Hands-On Build: Blind Test and Inspection Certificate
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- The calibrated spring scale from Lesson 282
- Five mystery masses (A–E), prepared by the teacher in sealed bags or wrapped in cloth
- A reference spring balance or a kitchen/market scale (to determine true values)
- A piece of card or paper for the certificate
- A marker pen
- Notebook and pencil
- Calculator (phone) — optional

### Workflow
1. Collect the five mystery masses from the teacher's table. Do NOT look at the true values yet. **Checkpoint:** the test is blind — you do not know the true forces.
2. Zero your scale (adjust the zero screw so the pointer sits at 0 N with no load). **Checkpoint:** the zero is set before any measurement.
3. Weigh mass A on your scale. Record the reading in the table. Remove the mass and check the zero returns to 0 N. **Checkpoint:** the pointer returns to zero — if not, re-zero and re-weigh.
4. Repeat for masses B, C, D, and E. Record all five readings. **Checkpoint:** all five readings are recorded before the true values are revealed.
5. The teacher reveals the true values (from the reference scale or from mass × g = mass × 10 N/kg). Fill in the error table: mass | measured (N) | true (N) | error (N) | % error. **Checkpoint:** error = measured − true; % error = (error / true) × 100%.
6. Find the maximum absolute error across all five tests. Compare with the tolerance: ±10% of the full-scale range = ±1 N for a 0–10 N scale. **Checkpoint:** if max |error| ≤ 1 N, the scale passes; if not, it fails.
7. Issue the inspection certificate on the card: Scale ID | Range (0–10 N) | Date | Max error (N) | Max % error | Tolerance (±1 N) | Result (PASS/FAIL) | Inspector signature. **Checkpoint:** the certificate is complete and signed.
8. If the scale fails, diagnose: are all readings low (zero drift — re-zero), or do they scatter (noise — improve the pointer)? Re-calibrate if needed and re-test. **Checkpoint:** a re-tested scale that now passes can be issued a new certificate.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not zeroing before each measurement** — the zero drifts between masses. Check and re-zero before each weighing.
- **Peeking at the true values** — the test is only valid if it is blind. Do not look at the reference readings until all five are recorded.
- **Computing percentage error wrong** — % error = (error / true) × 100%, not (error / measured) × 100%. The true value is the reference, not your reading.
- **Confusing pass/fail with "zero error"** — a scale can pass with a small error. The question is whether the error is within tolerance, not whether it is zero.
- **Not diagnosing a failure** — if the scale fails, find the cause (zero drift, nonlinearity, pointer wobble) and fix it before re-testing. A failed scale with no diagnosis will fail again.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Your scale reads 6 N. The true force is 5 N. What is the error? What is the percentage error? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why is a blind test better than a test where you know the true value? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A scale has a maximum error of 0.8 N over a 0–10 N range. Tolerance is ±10%. Does it pass? Show your working. (mirrors pre-test)
4. All five of your readings are 1 N low. What is the likely cause, and how do you fix it?
5. Challenge: your scale reads 4% low at 8 N but true at 2 N. Is this a zero error or a nonlinearity? How would you correct it?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Error = 6 − 5 = +1 N (the scale reads 1 N high). Percentage error = (1 / 5) × 100% = 20%. The scale reads 20% high.
2. In a blind test you do not know the true value, so you cannot unconsciously adjust your reading to match. It tests the scale honestly.
3. Tolerance of ±10% over 0–10 N = ±1 N. The scale's maximum error is 0.8 N, which is less than 1 N. So it passes.
4. All readings are 1 N low — this is a systematic error, most likely zero drift (the pointer sits at −1 N with no load). Fix: re-zero the scale (adjust the zero screw so the pointer sits at 0 N with no load), then re-test.
5. This is a nonlinearity, not a zero error. If it were a zero error, all readings would be off by the same amount. But the scale is true at 2 N and 4% low at 8 N — the error grows with force, which means the strip's stiffness k is changing (the scale is not linear). To correct: build a correction table. Weigh known forces (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 N using water), record the scale reading for each, and tabulate: scale reading → true force. When measuring an unknown, read the scale and look up the true force in the table.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your scale reads 4% low at 8 N but true at 2 N. Model the fault (nonlinear strip?) and propose a correction table.
   **Worked Answer:** At 2 N the scale is true, so the 0–2 N region is linear. At 8 N the scale reads 4% low: the pointer is at the 7.68 N position (8 × 0.96 = 7.68), so the true 8 N gives less extension than the linear scale predicts. This means the strip is stiffening at high force — k is increasing, not decreasing. This is unusual for rubber (which normally softens) but can happen if the strip is thick, reinforced, or approaching a fully-aligned state. Correction table: measure the pointer position for known forces (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 N using water). Example: 2 N → 2.0 N (true), 4 N → 4.1 N, 6 N → 6.3 N, 8 N → 8.3 N, 10 N → 10.6 N (interpolated from the 4% error at 8 N, assuming the error grows linearly with force). The user reads the scale, then looks up the true force in the table. This is how real nonlinear gauges are corrected — a calibration curve, not a single screw adjustment.
2. **Q:** A scale has errors of −0.3, −0.4, −0.3, −0.5, −0.4 N across five masses (true values 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 N). Is this a zero error or a nonlinearity? Compute the percentage error at each point and state whether the scale passes a ±5% tolerance.
   **Worked Answer:** The errors are all negative and roughly constant (−0.3 to −0.5 N), not growing with force. This is a systematic zero error — the pointer sits about 0.4 N below zero with no load. Percentage errors: (−0.3/2) × 100 = −15%; (−0.4/4) × 100 = −10%; (−0.3/6) × 100 = −5%; (−0.5/8) × 100 = −6.25%; (−0.4/10) × 100 = −4%. The scale fails the ±5% tolerance at 2 N (−15%), 4 N (−10%), and 8 N (−6.25%). It passes only at 6 N and 10 N. Fix: re-zero the scale (add 0.4 N worth of adjustment), which should bring all errors close to zero. After re-zeroing, the residual errors should be small (the scatter of ±0.1 N is random noise, not systematic). The lesson: a constant absolute error becomes a large percentage error at small forces, which is why zero adjustment matters most at the low end of the scale.
3. **Q:** A market spring balance is certified to ±2% over 0–50 kg. A learner's rubber-strip scale is certified to ±10% over 0–10 N. Which is more accurate, and why might the learner's scale still be good enough for a classroom experiment?
   **Worked Answer:** The market scale is more accurate: ±2% of 50 kg = ±1 kg anywhere on the scale. The learner's scale is ±10% of 10 N = ±1 N. In relative terms, the market scale is 5× more accurate (2% vs 10%). But for a classroom experiment measuring forces of 2–8 N, an error of ±1 N is often acceptable — the goal is to see the trend (e.g., F = kx), not to get a certified value. The market scale costs thousands of shillings and is verified annually by the Weights and Measures Agency; the learner's scale costs nothing and is built from an inner tube. For teaching physics, the rubber-strip scale is good enough. For selling goods in a market, it is not — the ±10% error would cheat either the buyer or the seller. The tolerance depends on the purpose.

## Safety Notes
- Do not load the scale past its calibrated range (10 N) — the strip may snap.
- Mystery masses in bags should not contain sharp objects — the teacher should check them before the lesson.
- A dropped mass can land on a foot — keep toes clear and weigh over a soft surface.
- If a scale strip snaps during testing, retire it — do not re-tie a broken strip (the knot weakens it).
