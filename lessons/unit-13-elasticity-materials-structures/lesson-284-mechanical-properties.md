# Lesson 284: Mechanical Properties of Materials (CBE 1.3)

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define and demonstrate six mechanical properties: ductility, malleability, brittleness, hardness, stiffness, and strength.
2. Test each property with a simple local-material demo and record observations in a properties table.
3. Distinguish strength (resists breaking) from stiffness (resists deformation) using a rubber strip vs a wire.
4. Match each property to a real product and explain why that property matters for that product.

## Materials
- A piece of thin copper or galvanised wire (~30 cm) — for ductility
- A used bottle top (crown cap) or a small piece of mabati — for malleability
- A piece of chalk or a dry sisal strand — for brittleness
- A steel nail, a copper wire, a piece of glass or a stone, and a piece of wood — for the hardness scratch test
- A rubber strip and a steel wire of the same length — for stiffness vs strength
- A hammer or a heavy stone — for hammering
- Pliers or wire cutters
- Safety goggles or sunglasses
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **ductility** (kunyooka) — the ability to be drawn into a wire; a ductile material stretches and thins before breaking
- **malleability** (kubanwa) — the ability to be hammered or rolled into a sheet; a malleable material flattens without cracking
- **brittleness** (mena) — the tendency to snap or shatter with little or no deformation; a brittle material gives no warning
- **hardness** (ugumu) — the ability to resist scratching or indentation; a harder material scratches a softer one
- **stiffness** (rigidity) — resistance to deformation; a stiff material needs a large force to stretch or bend (high k or high E)
- **strength** (nguvu) — the force a material can take before breaking; a strong material resists fracture

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A wire stretches and thins before it snaps. A piece of chalk snaps with no warning. Which is ductile and which is brittle?
2. A steel nail scratches a copper wire, but the copper wire cannot scratch the steel. Which is harder?
3. A rubber strip stretches a long way under a small force. A steel wire barely moves under the same force. Which is stiffer? Which is stronger?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The wire is ductile (it stretches and thins before breaking). The chalk is brittle (it snaps with no deformation).
2. Steel is harder — it scratches the copper, but the copper cannot scratch it. Hardness is the ability to resist being scratched.
3. The steel wire is stiffer (it barely moves — high resistance to deformation). The rubber strip is less stiff (it stretches easily). Strength is different: the steel wire is also stronger (it takes a larger force to break), but a material can be stiff and weak (like glass — hard to bend but breaks easily) or flexible and strong (like nylon rope — stretches a lot but holds a large load).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a panga, a sufuria, a piece of chalk, and a rubber strip. Ask: *why is the panga steel, the sufuria aluminium, the chalk gypsum, and the strap rubber?* Each material is chosen for its mechanical properties. Board the six properties: **ductility, malleability, brittleness, hardness, stiffness, strength**. Today we test each one with a simple demo.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs rotate through six demo stations (or the teacher demonstrates each in sequence; see Hands-On Build). At each station, learners do the test and record observations in a properties table: property | material tested | observation | what it means. The six demos: (a) **ductility** — pull copper wire until it necks and thins; (b) **malleability** — hammer a bottle top flat on a stone; (c) **brittleness** — snap a piece of chalk; (d) **hardness** — scratch a copper wire with a steel nail, then try the reverse; (e) **stiffness** — hang the same load on a rubber strip and a steel wire, compare the extension; (f) **strength** — pull a rubber strip and a cotton thread to breaking, compare the breaking force.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The six properties are independent — a material can be strong but not stiff (nylon rope), stiff but not strong (glass), hard but brittle (chalk), ductile but not hard (copper). Board the key distinctions: **ductile vs brittle** — does it deform before breaking? **hard vs soft** — does it scratch or get scratched? **stiff vs flexible** — does it resist deformation? **strong vs weak** — does it resist breaking? Confront the misconception that "hard means strong" — glass is hard (scratches steel) but brittle (shatters). Rubber is soft (scratches easily) but strong (stretches a long way before breaking). Hardness is about the surface; strength is about the whole material. Also address: "stiff means strong" — a steel wire is both stiff and strong, but a glass rod is stiff (barely bends) and weak (shatters). Stiffness is about deformation; strength is about fracture. Engineers choose materials by matching the property to the job: a panga blade must be hard (holds an edge) and strong (does not break), but not brittle (must not shatter on impact).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *six properties, six tests — match the property to the product*. Homework: find one object at home for each of the six properties and write which property matters most for that object (e.g., sufuria → malleable; panga → hard; rope → strong and flexible).

## Hands-On Build: Six-Station Properties Tour
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Set up six stations or demo each in sequence.*

### Parts
- Thin copper or galvanised wire (~30 cm) — for ductility
- A used bottle top (crown cap) or a small piece of mabati — for malleability
- A piece of chalk or a dry sisal strand — for brittleness
- A steel nail, a copper wire, a piece of glass or a hard stone, a piece of wood — for hardness
- A rubber strip and a steel wire of the same length — for stiffness
- A cotton thread and a rubber strip — for strength
- A hammer or a heavy stone
- Pliers or wire cutters
- Safety goggles or sunglasses
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. **Ductility station:** Grip the copper wire with pliers at both ends. Pull steadily. Observe: the wire stretches and thins (necks) before breaking. **Checkpoint:** the wire shows visible necking — a ductile failure with warning.
2. **Malleability station:** Place the bottle top on a flat stone. Hammer it flat. Observe: it flattens into a thin disc without cracking. **Checkpoint:** the metal flattens — it is malleable. If it cracks, it is brittle (cast iron would crack; aluminium and copper flatten).
3. **Brittleness station:** Snap a piece of chalk by bending it. Observe: it breaks suddenly with no stretching or bending. **Checkpoint:** the chalk snaps with no warning — a brittle failure.
4. **Hardness station:** Press a steel nail against a copper wire and drag it. Observe: the steel scratches the copper. Now press the copper against the steel and drag. Observe: the copper cannot scratch the steel. **Checkpoint:** steel is harder than copper. Arrange the scratch order: glass/stone > steel > copper > wood. The harder material scratches the softer one.
5. **Stiffness station:** Hang the same load (e.g., 1 litre of water, ~10 N) on a rubber strip and on a steel wire of the same length. Measure the extension of each. **Checkpoint:** the rubber stretches several centimetres; the steel wire barely moves. Steel is stiffer (higher Young's modulus E).
6. **Strength station:** Pull a cotton thread and a rubber strip until one breaks. Compare the force needed. **Checkpoint:** the cotton thread may break first (weaker) even though it is stiffer (less stretch). The rubber strip stretches more but may hold more force before breaking. Strength is about the breaking force, not the stiffness.
7. Fill in the properties table: property | material tested | observation | what it means. **Checkpoint:** all six rows are filled with real observations, not guesses.

### Common Mistakes
- **Confusing hard and strong** — glass is hard (scratches steel) but brittle (shatters). Hardness is about the surface; strength is about the whole material. Do not assume a hard material is strong.
- **Confusing stiff and strong** — a steel wire is stiff and strong, but a glass rod is stiff and weak. Stiffness is about deformation; strength is about fracture.
- **Not wearing goggles for the brittleness station** — chalk pieces fly when snapped. Wear eye protection.
- **Hammering on an unstable surface** — the bottle top can fly off. Hammer on a flat, stable stone or anvil.
- **Scratching with too much force** — a light drag is enough for the hardness test. Pressing too hard bends the softer material and gives a false result.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A wire stretches and thins before snapping. A piece of chalk snaps with no warning. Which is ductile and which is brittle? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A steel nail scratches copper but copper cannot scratch steel. Which is harder? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A rubber strip stretches a long way under a small force. A steel wire barely moves. Which is stiffer? Are they necessarily different in strength? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Name one material that is hard but brittle, and one that is soft but strong. Explain.
5. Challenge: match each property to a product — panga blade, sufuria, window glass, spring, hammer face — and justify each choice.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The wire is ductile (stretches and thins before breaking). The chalk is brittle (snaps with no deformation).
2. Steel is harder — it scratches the copper, but the copper cannot scratch it.
3. The steel wire is stiffer (barely moves under the load). They are not necessarily different in strength — stiffness is about deformation, strength is about breaking. The rubber may be strong (holds a large load before breaking) even though it is not stiff (stretches easily). A material can be stiff and weak (glass) or flexible and strong (nylon rope).
4. Hard but brittle: glass (scratches steel but shatters on impact) or chalk (hard surface but snaps easily). Soft but strong: rubber (scratches easily but stretches a long way before breaking) or nylon rope (soft to the touch but holds a large load).
5. Panga blade: hard (holds a sharp edge) and strong (does not break on impact) but not brittle (must not shatter). Sufuria: malleable (hammered or pressed into shape from a flat sheet of aluminium or steel). Window glass: transparent and hard (resists scratching) but brittle (shatters on impact). Spring: stiff (returns to shape after loading) and ductile (can be drawn into wire and coiled) — but not brittle (must not snap). Hammer face: hard (does not dent on impact) and strong (does not chip) — the hardest part of the tool.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Match each property to a product requirement and justify: panga blade, bucket, window, spring, hammer face.
   **Worked Answer:** **Panga blade:** hard (holds a sharp edge for cutting brush), strong (does not break when striking a tree or bone), tough (not brittle — must not shatter on impact). Steel is chosen because it is hard, strong, and can be heat-treated to balance hardness and toughness. **Bucket (debe or sufuria):** malleable (pressed or hammered from a flat sheet into a hollow shape), ductile (the sheet was rolled from a block), corrosion-resistant (aluminium or galvanised steel does not rust). Aluminium is chosen because it is malleable, lightweight, and does not corrode. **Window:** hard (resists scratching so the glass stays clear), brittle (shatters on impact — a safety risk, so tempered or laminated glass is used in cars). Glass is chosen because it is transparent and hard, despite its brittleness. **Spring:** stiff (high Young's modulus — returns to shape after loading), ductile (drawn into wire and coiled), strong (does not break under repeated loading), and fatigue-resistant (survives many cycles). Steel is chosen because it is stiff, strong, and ductile. **Hammer face:** hard (does not dent on impact with a nail), strong (does not chip), and tough (absorbs impact energy without cracking). Hardened steel is chosen for the face; the handle is wood or fibreglass (different properties — shock-absorbing and light).
2. **Q:** A steel wire and a rubber strip have the same breaking force (50 N). The steel wire barely stretches before breaking; the rubber strip stretches to 3× its length. Which is stiffer? Which is stronger? Which has the higher breaking strain?
   **Worked Answer:** Stiffness: the steel wire is stiffer — it barely deforms under load (high E ≈ 200 GPa). The rubber strip is flexible (low E ≈ 0.01–1 MPa). Strength: both have the same breaking force (50 N), so they are equally strong in absolute terms. But the breaking stress differs: if the steel wire has cross-section 0.1 mm² and the rubber strip has cross-section 4 mm², the steel's breaking stress is 50 / (0.1 × 10⁻⁶) = 5 × 10⁸ Pa = 500 MPa, while the rubber's is 50 / (4 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.25 × 10⁷ Pa = 12.5 MPa. So the steel is stronger per unit area (higher breaking stress). Breaking strain: the rubber strip wins — it stretches to 3× its length (strain = 2.0, or 200%), while the steel wire might stretch only 0.1% before breaking (strain ≈ 0.001). So the rubber has ~2000× the breaking strain of the steel. The lesson: strength (breaking force) is not the same as stiffness (resistance to deformation) or ductility (stretch before breaking). A material can be strong and stiff (steel), strong and flexible (nylon), or weak and stiff (glass).
3. **Q:** Explain why a jua kali artisan chooses different materials for different parts of a sufuria: the body is aluminium, the handle is steel, and the rivets are copper.
   **Worked Answer:** The **body** is aluminium because it is malleable (can be pressed into a deep bowl shape), lightweight (low density), a good conductor of heat (cooks food evenly), and corrosion-resistant (does not rust — aluminium forms a protective oxide layer). The **handle** is steel because it is strong (does not bend when the sufuria is full and heavy), stiff (holds its shape), and heat-resistant (does not soften near the fire). Steel is heavier and cheaper than aluminium for the handle, and it does not need to conduct heat — in fact, a steel handle stays cooler than an aluminium one. The **rivets** are copper because it is ductile (can be drawn into wire and shaped into rivets), malleable (flattens when hammered to seal the joint), and corrosion-resistant (does not rust, so the rivet does not weaken over time). Copper is also softer than steel, so the rivet deforms to fill the hole and make a tight seal, while the harder steel handle and aluminium body hold their shape. Each material is chosen for the specific property its job demands — this is materials selection, the heart of engineering.

## Safety Notes
- Wear goggles or sunglasses at the brittleness station — chalk pieces fly when snapped.
- Hammer on a flat, stable stone or anvil — the bottle top can fly off an unstable surface.
- Wire ends are sharp after cutting — use pliers to handle them, not bare fingers.
- Do not pull wire to breaking near the face — the wire whips when it snaps.
- The hardness scratch test uses light pressure — pressing too hard can slip and cut a finger.
