# Lesson 285: Tensile Test Day: Sisal vs Cotton vs Wire

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Set up a simple tensile tester using a bucket of sand as a controlled load.
2. Pull sisal, cotton, and thin wire strands to breaking and record the breaking mass for each.
3. Compare breaking loads across materials and explain why thicker or multi-strand samples hold more.
4. Predict and test how doubling the number of strands changes the breaking load.

## Materials
- Sisal twine (mfumo wa sisali) — available at any dukia or hardware shop
- Cotton string (kamba ya pamba) — from a tailor or market
- Thin galvanised wire (waya) — from a jua kali workshop or hardware
- A sturdy bucket (debe or small sufuria)
- Dry sand — to add as load in measured cups
- A measuring cup or empty 500 ml water bottle (one cup ≈ 0.8 kg of sand)
- A spring scale or hanging scale (if available) — otherwise count cups of sand
- A wooden broom handle or two sturdy chair backs — to hang the strand over
- Scissors or wire cutters
- A ruler or tape measure
- Notebook and pencil
- Safety goggles or sunglasses (to protect eyes from snapping strands)

## Vocabulary
- **tensile strength** (nguvu ya kuvuta) — the pulling force a material can take before it breaks
- **breaking load** (mzigo wa kuvunja) — the mass (or force) at which a strand snaps
- **strand** (uzito) — a single fibre or wire; more strands share the load
- **ductile** (kunyooka) — stretches before breaking, like wire or copper
- **brittle** (mena) — snaps with little stretching, like dry sisal
- **cross-section** (kitalu) — the thickness area of the strand; more area = more strength

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Which holds more weight before snapping: a thin sisal string or a thick sisal rope? Why?
2. If you twist two strands together, do you expect the breaking load to double? Why or why not?
3. Name one material that stretches a lot before breaking and one that snaps suddenly.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The thick rope — it has more fibres and a larger cross-section, so more material shares the load.
2. Ideally yes — two strands share the load, so the breaking load should be about double. In practice it may be slightly less because the strands are never perfectly equal.
3. Wire (ductile) stretches before breaking; dry sisal (brittle) snaps with little warning.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a single sisal strand and a thin wire. Ask: *which one holds more sand before snapping?* Take a show of hands. Then ask: *what if we use two strands instead of one?* Board the predictions. Introduce the term **tensile strength** — the pulling force a material takes before breaking.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the tensile tester (see Hands-On Build). They hang a single sisal strand over the broom handle, tie the bucket to the strand, and add sand cup by cup until the strand snaps. They record the number of cups (or the mass) at break. They repeat for cotton string and thin wire. Then they test two strands of sisal twisted together. Results go on a class table: material | strands | breaking load (cups or kg).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The breaking load depends on two things: (a) the material itself — wire is stronger than sisal per unit area — and (b) the cross-section — more strands means more area sharing the load. When two strands are used, the load is shared, so the breaking load should be roughly double. In practice it is often slightly less because the strands are not perfectly equal: one stretches a bit more and takes more load, so it snaps first, then the second snaps immediately after. Ductile materials (wire) stretch and thin before breaking — a visible warning. Brittle materials (dry sisal) snap with little stretching. Board: **breaking load ∝ cross-section × material strength**.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *strength = material × cross-section; more strands share the load*. Homework: find three items at home or in the market that use multiple strands to carry load (e.g., rope, bicycle brake cable, suspension bridge cable). Write down the material and estimate the number of strands.

## Hands-On Build: Sand-Bucket Tensile Tester
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- A wooden broom handle or two sturdy chair backs (to hang the strand over)
- One sisal strand, one cotton string, one thin wire (each ~50 cm long)
- A bucket (debe or small sufuria) with a handle or a string loop
- Dry sand, measured in cups (one 500 ml cup ≈ 0.8 kg)
- Scissors or wire cutters
- Safety goggles or sunglasses
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Set up the broom handle between two chairs or clamp it to a table edge so it is horizontal and sturdy. **Checkpoint:** the handle does not roll or slip when loaded.
2. Tie one end of the sisal strand to the bucket handle (or a string loop on the bucket). Loop the strand over the broom handle. Hold the free end or tie it to a fixed point so the bucket hangs freely. **Checkpoint:** the strand is vertical and the bucket hangs clear of the ground.
3. Add sand one cup at a time. Wait 3 seconds after each cup to let the strand settle. Count the cups. **Checkpoint:** add slowly — dropping a cup suddenly adds a dynamic load (impact) that breaks the strand early.
4. When the strand snaps, record the number of cups (or the mass: cups × 0.8 kg). Note the mode of failure: did it stretch first (ductile) or snap suddenly (brittle)?
5. Repeat with a fresh cotton string and then with thin wire. **Checkpoint:** use the same bucket and the same cup size so the results are comparable.
6. Now twist two sisal strands together and repeat. Record the breaking load. Compare with the single-strand result — is it double? **Checkpoint:** the two strands should be the same length and thickness; if one is shorter, it takes all the load and the test is invalid.
7. Tabulate all results on the class table.

### Common Mistakes
- **Adding sand too fast** — a sudden cup of sand is an impact load, not a static load. Add slowly and wait after each cup.
- **Unequal strands** — if one strand is shorter or thicker, it takes more load and snaps first. Match the strands as closely as possible.
- **Knots in the test section** — a knot weakens the strand. Tie the knot at the bucket end, not in the middle of the test length.
- **Worn strand** — a strand that has been used before is already fatigued. Use a fresh strand for each test.
- **Wire whipping on break** — when wire snaps, the ends can fly. Wear goggles and keep faces away. Point the wire away from people.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Which material held the most sand before breaking in your test? Which held the least? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If a single sisal strand broke at 4 cups, what would you predict for two strands? What did you actually get?
3. Why does a thicker rope hold more weight than a thin string of the same material?
4. What is the difference between a ductile and a brittle material? Give one example of each from your test.
5. Challenge: a steel wire with cross-section 0.1 mm² breaks at 20 N. What force would break a wire of the same material with cross-section 0.4 mm²?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Wire held the most; sisal or cotton held the least (answer depends on the class data, but wire is typically strongest per unit area).
2. Ideal prediction: 8 cups (double). Actual result is usually slightly less (e.g., 7 cups) because the strands are not perfectly equal — one takes more load and snaps first.
3. A thicker rope has a larger cross-section — more fibres share the load, so the total breaking force is larger. The material strength per unit area is the same.
4. Ductile: stretches and thins before breaking (e.g., wire). Brittle: snaps with little or no stretching (e.g., dry sisal).
5. Breaking force ∝ cross-section. If 0.1 mm² breaks at 20 N, then 0.4 mm² (4× the area) breaks at 4 × 20 = 80 N.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A single sisal strand breaks at 3.2 kg. Two strands twisted together break at 5.9 kg instead of the ideal 6.4 kg. Explain the shortfall and calculate the efficiency.
   **Worked Answer:** Ideal: 2 × 3.2 = 6.4 kg. Actual: 5.9 kg. Efficiency = 5.9/6.4 = 0.92 = 92%. The shortfall (8%) arises because the two strands are never perfectly equal in length or thickness. The shorter or thinner strand reaches its breaking load first and snaps, transferring the full load to the remaining strand, which then snaps immediately. Also, twisting the strands together introduces a slight sideways (shear) component that reduces the pure tensile capacity. In engineering, bundled cables are typically 85–95% efficient compared to the sum of individual strands.
2. **Q:** Double the strands: predict and test the breaking load; explain any shortfall from ideal doubling.
   **Worked Answer:** Prediction: if one strand breaks at load F, two strands should break at 2F (ideal). Test: typically 0.85F to 0.95F per strand in the bundle. The shortfall comes from three sources: (a) unequal load sharing — one strand is slightly shorter and takes more load; (b) twist-induced shear — twisting the strands introduces a sideways force that reduces the pure tensile strength; (c) friction and contact — the strands rub against each other, causing local heating and wear at the contact points. To minimise the shortfall, use strands of exactly the same length and thickness, and keep the twist loose. In real engineering, parallel-lay cables (not twisted) achieve higher efficiency than stranded cables.
3. **Q:** A boda-boda brake cable has 19 strands of steel wire, each breaking at 50 N. The cable breaks at 850 N. What is the efficiency, and why is it less than 100%?
   **Worked Answer:** Ideal: 19 × 50 = 950 N. Actual: 850 N. Efficiency = 850/950 = 0.89 = 89%. The 11% shortfall is due to: (a) unequal load sharing among the 19 strands (some are shorter inside the bundle), (b) the helical twist of the strands reducing the effective tensile component (the force along the cable is the strand force × cos θ, where θ is the helix angle), and (c) friction between strands causing local stress concentrations. Despite the shortfall, a multi-strand cable is used because it is flexible (can bend around the handlebar) and fails progressively (strands snap one by one, giving warning) rather than catastrophically.

## Safety Notes
- Wire strands can whip when they snap — wear goggles or sunglasses and keep faces away from the test area.
- Do not stand directly under the bucket when it is loaded — it can fall suddenly when the strand breaks.
- Do not exceed the bucket's capacity — a falling bucket of sand is heavy. Use a small bucket (debe).
- Wire cutters can pinch fingers — the teacher may pre-cut the wire.
- Clean up spilled sand immediately — it is a slip hazard.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that forces can stretch and break materials (from everyday experience). No prior formula is needed — this lesson introduces tensile testing qualitatively and quantitatively.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the build, recording breaking loads in cups, and the qualitative comparison (which is strongest?). Ages 14–17 should calculate the efficiency of doubling strands and explain the shortfall from ideal doubling.
- Local resource tips: sisal twine, cotton string, and thin wire are all available at any Kenyan market or hardware shop. Sand is free. A debe or small sufuria works as the bucket. A broom handle or two chairs serve as the support. No specialised equipment is needed.
- This lesson produces real data. Record the class results on the board and compare across groups. Variation between groups is expected (different strand thicknesses, different sand density) — discuss why the results vary and why controlled testing matters.
- The lesson sets up Lesson 286 (Stress and Strain), where learners normalise these results by cross-section and original length to compare materials fairly.
