# Lesson 286: Stress and Strain: Making Tests Comparable

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Define stress (force per unit area) and strain (extension per unit original length) and write their formulas.
2. Explain why a thick rope is not a "stronger material" than a thin rope of the same fibre — it just has more cross-section.
3. Compute stress and strain from the tensile test data collected in Lesson 285.
4. Compare the breaking stress of sisal with a tabulated value for steel wire and explain the large difference.

## Materials
- The class data table from Lesson 285 (breaking loads for sisal, cotton, wire)
- A ruler or tape measure (to measure strand diameters if not already done)
- A notebook and pencil
- A calculator (if available) or pen-and-paper arithmetic
- A printed reference table of typical breaking stresses (or the teacher writes values on the board)

## Vocabulary
- **stress** (msongamko) — force per unit area, σ = F/A; measured in pascals (Pa) or N/m²
- **strain** (mnyooko) — extension per original length, ε = ΔL/L₀; dimensionless (no units)
- **cross-sectional area** (eneo la kitalu) — A, the area of the cut face of the strand; for a circular strand, A = πr²
- **breaking stress** (msongamko wa kuvunja) — the stress at which a material snaps; a property of the material, not the size
- **Young's modulus** (moduli ya Young) — the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region; a measure of stiffness

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A thick sisal rope and a thin sisal string are made of the same fibre. Which holds more weight? Which has the higher breaking stress?
2. Write the formula for stress. What are its units?
3. Write the formula for strain. Does it have units?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The thick rope holds more weight (more cross-section shares the load). But both have the same breaking stress — it is a property of the material (sisal), not the size.
2. Stress σ = F/A, where F is force (N) and A is cross-sectional area (m²). Units: pascals (Pa) = N/m².
3. Strain ε = ΔL/L₀, where ΔL is the extension and L₀ is the original length. It is dimensionless (m/m cancels) — no units.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — recall the class data from Lesson 285. A single sisal strand broke at, say, 3.2 kg. A two-strand bundle broke at 5.9 kg. Ask: *is the two-strand bundle made of a stronger material?* No — it is the same sisal, just more of it. So how do we compare materials fairly, regardless of size? Board: **divide force by area → stress; divide extension by original length → strain**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — learners work through the class data. They estimate the diameter of each strand (using a ruler or the teacher provides values: sisal ~2 mm, cotton ~1.5 mm, wire ~0.5 mm). They compute the cross-sectional area A = πr² for each. They convert the breaking load (cups × 0.8 kg × 9.8 m/s²) to force in newtons. They compute the breaking stress σ = F/A for each material. They compare: which material has the highest breaking stress? They also estimate the extension at break (if they measured it in Lesson 285) and compute strain ε = ΔL/L₀.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Stress (σ = F/A) normalises force by area — it tells you the force per square metre of material. Strain (ε = ΔL/L₀) normalises extension by original length — it tells you how much it stretched relative to its size. A thick rope holds more force not because the material is stronger, but because it has more area. The breaking stress is a property of the material: sisal ~40 MPa, cotton ~80 MPa, steel wire ~500–2000 MPa. Steel is 10–50× stronger than sisal per unit area. Board: **breaking stress is a material property; breaking load depends on size**. Introduce Young's modulus E = σ/ε as the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region — a stiff material (steel) has a high E; a stretchy material (rubber) has a low E.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *stress = force ÷ area; strain = extension ÷ original length; breaking stress is a material property*. Homework: look up the breaking stress of a material used in construction (e.g., concrete, timber, steel) and compare it with the sisal value from today's lesson.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Write the formula for stress and state its units. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Write the formula for strain. Does it have units? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A sisal strand with cross-section 3 mm² breaks at 15 N. Compute the breaking stress in Pa and MPa. (mirrors pre-test)
4. A thick sisal rope and a thin sisal string are the same material. Which has the higher breaking stress? Explain. (mirrors pre-test)
5. Challenge: the breaking stress of steel wire is 1000 MPa. A steel wire has cross-section 0.2 mm². What force breaks it?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Stress σ = F/A. Units: pascals (Pa) = N/m².
2. Strain ε = ΔL/L₀. It is dimensionless — no units (m/m cancels).
3. A = 3 mm² = 3 × 10⁻⁶ m². σ = F/A = 15 / (3 × 10⁻⁶) = 5 × 10⁶ Pa = 5 MPa.
4. Both have the same breaking stress — it is a property of the material (sisal), not the size. The thick rope holds more total force because it has more area, but the stress at break is the same.
5. σ = 1000 MPa = 1000 × 10⁶ Pa = 10⁹ Pa. A = 0.2 mm² = 0.2 × 10⁻⁶ m² = 2 × 10⁻⁷ m². F = σ × A = 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁷ = 200 N.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Compute the breaking stress for sisal from the Unit 285 data. Compare with a tabulated value for steel wire.
   **Worked Answer:** From Lesson 285: a single sisal strand broke at ~3.2 kg. Force F = 3.2 × 9.8 = 31.4 N. Diameter ~2 mm, so radius r = 1 mm = 1 × 10⁻³ m. Area A = πr² = π × (10⁻³)² = 3.14 × 10⁻⁶ m². Breaking stress σ = F/A = 31.4 / (3.14 × 10⁻⁶) = 10⁷ Pa = 10 MPa. (Published sisal fibre breaking stress is ~30–50 MPa — our single strand is lower because it is a twisted bundle of fibres, not a single fibre, and the twist reduces efficiency.) Steel wire: σ_break ≈ 1000–2000 MPa. So steel is ~100–200× stronger than sisal per unit area. This is why boda-boda brake cables are steel, not sisal.
2. **Q:** A copper wire of length 2 m and diameter 0.4 mm stretches by 1.5 mm under a 20 N load. Compute the stress, the strain, and Young's modulus.
   **Worked Answer:** Area: r = 0.2 mm = 2 × 10⁻⁴ m. A = πr² = π × (2 × 10⁻⁴)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m². Stress: σ = F/A = 20 / (1.26 × 10⁻⁷) = 1.59 × 10⁸ Pa = 159 MPa. Strain: ε = ΔL/L₀ = 1.5 × 10⁻³ / 2 = 7.5 × 10⁻⁴. Young's modulus: E = σ/ε = 1.59 × 10⁸ / 7.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.12 × 10¹¹ Pa = 212 GPa. (Published copper E ≈ 110–130 GPa — our value is high, likely because the wire was pre-stretched or the diameter measurement was imprecise. This is a good discussion point about measurement error.)
3. **Q:** A rubber band of cross-section 4 mm² and length 10 cm stretches to 30 cm under a 5 N load. Compute the strain and explain why Young's modulus for rubber is much lower than for steel.
   **Worked Answer:** Strain: ε = ΔL/L₀ = (30 − 10)/10 = 20/10 = 2.0 (the rubber stretches to 3× its original length — a strain of 200%, which is enormous compared to steel's ~0.1% at break). Stress: A = 4 mm² = 4 × 10⁻⁶ m². σ = 5 / (4 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.25 × 10⁶ Pa = 1.25 MPa. Young's modulus: E = σ/ε = 1.25 × 10⁶ / 2.0 = 6.25 × 10⁵ Pa = 0.625 MPa. Steel's E ≈ 200 GPa = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa. So steel is ~300,000× stiffer than rubber. Rubber's low modulus comes from its molecular structure — long, coiled polymer chains that uncoil under load (entropic elasticity), unlike steel's rigid atomic bonds.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 285 (Tensile Test Day) provides the data. Learners should have the breaking loads recorded. If Lesson 285 was not done, the teacher can provide sample data on the board.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the concept — a thick rope is not a stronger material, just more of it — and compute stress with simple numbers. Ages 14–17 should compute stress, strain, and Young's modulus from the data and compare with tabulated values for steel and copper.
- Local resource tips: no special materials needed. A calculator helps but pen-and-paper arithmetic works. The teacher can write the key formulas and typical values on the board: sisal ~10–50 MPa, cotton ~80 MPa, steel ~1000–2000 MPa, copper ~200 MPa (stress at break), E_steel ≈ 200 GPa, E_copper ≈ 120 GPa, E_rubber ≈ 0.01–1 MPa.
- The key insight: breaking load depends on size (cross-section), but breaking stress is a material property. This is why engineers use stress, not force, to compare materials. A 1 mm² steel wire and a 1 mm² sisal strand hold very different forces, but the comparison is fair because the area is the same.
- This lesson directly uses the data from Lesson 285. If the data is messy (it will be), discuss why: strand diameters are hard to measure, the sand-cup mass is approximate, and the strands are twisted bundles, not single fibres. Real engineering data is also messy — that is why we take averages and use safety factors.
