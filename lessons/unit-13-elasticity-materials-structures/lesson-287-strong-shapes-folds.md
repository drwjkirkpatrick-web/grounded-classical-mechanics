# Lesson 287: Strong Shapes I: Folds and Corrugation

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that a flat sheet of paper flops under load but a folded sheet holds significant weight.
2. Explain how corrugation (folds) increases stiffness without adding material — the shape does the work.
3. Identify corrugated mabati (roofing sheets) as a real-world example of shape-based strength.
4. Build and test a simple card I-beam and explain why the I-shape puts material where it matters most.

## Materials
- Several sheets of A4 paper (or exercise book pages)
- Old cardboard boxes (kopo — from the shop or market)
- A ruler — to measure fold spacing
- Books of equal size (to use as load — or a bucket of sand)
- A pair of sturdy books or bricks (to use as supports, like a bridge)
- Tape or glue (optional — to hold folds in place)
- A piece of corrugated mabati (roofing iron) — if available, for show-and-tell
- Scissors — to cut cardboard
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **corrugation** (mikunjo) — repeated folds that give a flat sheet stiffness without adding weight
- **stiffness** (ugumu) — resistance to bending; a stiff sheet does not flop under load
- **I-beam** (bamu ya I) — a beam shaped like the letter I; puts material at the top and bottom where stress is highest
- **second moment of area** (mmento wa pili) — a measure of how far the material is spread from the centre; more spread = more bending resistance
- **neutral axis** (mhimili wa kati) — the centre line of a beam where stress is zero during bending

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A flat sheet of paper cannot hold a book. How can you make the same sheet hold a book without adding any material?
2. Why is roofing mabati corrugated (wavy) instead of flat?
3. Look at a steel I-beam in a building (or a picture). Why is it shaped like an I and not a solid square?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Fold it — corrugation (accordion folds) makes the sheet stiff enough to span a gap and hold a book. The same material, arranged differently, is much stronger.
2. Corrugation gives the mabati stiffness — a flat sheet would sag under its own weight or under a person walking on the roof. The waves act like mini-beams running along the sheet.
3. The I-shape puts most of the steel at the top and bottom flanges, where the bending stress is highest. The web (middle) just holds the two flanges apart. A solid square would waste material in the middle where it does little work.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold a flat A4 sheet horizontally between two books. It flops. Ask: *how can we make this same sheet hold a book?* Fold it into accordion pleats (like a fan). Now it spans the gap and holds a book. Show a piece of corrugated mabati — ask: *why are the waves there?* Board: **shape gives strength — corrugation adds stiffness without adding material**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build and test (see Hands-On Build). They make a flat paper bridge, test it (it flops). They fold it into accordion pleats, test it (it holds books). They count how many books it holds. Then they make a card I-beam (two flanges and a web) and test it against a flat card of the same size. Results go on a class table: shape | load held | observations.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. When a beam bends, the top is compressed (pushed together) and the bottom is stretched (pulled apart). The middle (the neutral axis) does almost nothing — it is neither compressed nor stretched. So material at the top and bottom does the most work; material in the middle does the least. Corrugation pushes material away from the centre (up and down in the waves), increasing the second moment of area — a measure of how far the material is spread from the neutral axis. More spread = more bending resistance. An I-beam does the same thing deliberately: thick flanges at top and bottom, thin web in the middle. Board: **strength comes from where you put the material, not how much you use**.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *corrugation and I-beams put material where it matters — far from the centre*. Homework: find three corrugated or folded structures at home or in the market (e.g., mabati roof, cardboard box flutes, folded tin tray). Sketch each and note where the folds are.

## Hands-On Build: Paper and Card Shape Test
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- 3–4 sheets of A4 paper (or exercise book pages)
- An old cardboard box (cut into flat panels)
- Two sturdy books or bricks (to use as supports)
- Several books of equal size (to use as load)
- A ruler — to measure fold spacing
- Tape or glue (optional)
- Scissors — to cut cardboard
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Set up two supports (books or bricks) 20 cm apart. **Checkpoint:** the supports are level and sturdy.
2. Lay a flat A4 sheet across the gap. Place one book on it. Observe: it flops immediately. **Checkpoint:** the flat sheet cannot even hold one book.
3. Fold a second A4 sheet into accordion pleats (fan folds) — fold every 1.5 cm, alternating up and down. Lay it across the gap. **Checkpoint:** the folds are even and the sheet holds its shape.
4. Place books one at a time on the folded sheet. Count how many it holds before it collapses. Record the number. **Checkpoint:** the folded sheet holds many books — the same material, now shaped.
5. Cut a flat card panel (from the cardboard box) about 5 cm wide and 20 cm long. Lay it across the gap and test how many books it holds. Record. **Checkpoint:** the flat card holds some load but bends visibly.
6. Build a card I-beam: cut two strips for the flanges (top and bottom) and one strip for the web (middle). Tape or glue them into an I shape. The flanges should be horizontal, the web vertical. **Checkpoint:** the flanges are parallel and the web is centred.
7. Place the I-beam across the gap and load it with books. Count how many it holds. Compare with the flat card of the same total material. **Checkpoint:** the I-beam holds significantly more than the flat card.
8. Tabulate all results on the class table: shape | load held | observations.

### Common Mistakes
- **Uneven folds** — if the accordion pleats are uneven, the sheet buckles at the widest fold. Keep the spacing consistent (~1.5 cm).
- **Folds too wide** — if the pleats are 4 cm apart, the sheet is still floppy. Narrower folds (1–1.5 cm) give more stiffness.
- **I-beam flanges not aligned** — if the top and bottom flanges are not parallel, the beam twists under load. Check alignment before testing.
- **Web not centred** — if the web is off-centre, one flange does more work than the other. Centre the web carefully.
- **Tape at the supports** — taping the beam to the supports changes the test (it becomes a fixed beam, not a simply supported one). Do not tape at the supports.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A flat sheet of paper flops. How can you make the same sheet hold a book without adding material? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why is roofing mabati corrugated instead of flat? (mirrors pre-test)
3. When a beam bends, where is the material doing the most work — at the top and bottom, or in the middle? Why?
4. What is the neutral axis, and why does material near it contribute little to bending strength?
5. Challenge: you have a fixed amount of cardboard. Which shape holds more load — a solid square bar or an I-beam of the same total area? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Fold it into accordion pleats (corrugation). The folds push material away from the centre, increasing stiffness without adding material.
2. Corrugation gives the mabati stiffness — a flat sheet would sag under its own weight or under a person on the roof. The waves act like mini-beams along the sheet.
3. At the top and bottom. When a beam bends, the top is compressed and the bottom is stretched — that is where the forces are highest. The middle (neutral axis) has almost zero stress.
4. The neutral axis is the centre line of the beam where stress is zero during bending. Material near it is neither compressed nor stretched, so it contributes little to resisting the bend. Material far from it (at the top and bottom) does most of the work.
5. The I-beam holds more. It puts the same amount of material at the top and bottom (where stress is highest) and minimises the middle (where stress is near zero). The solid square wastes material in the middle. Both have the same total area, but the I-beam's material is distributed more effectively — it has a larger second moment of area.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Explain the second moment of area qualitatively: why does material far from the middle do the work?
   **Worked Answer:** When a beam bends, the top is compressed and the bottom is stretched. The amount of compression or stretching increases with distance from the neutral axis — the further from the centre, the more the material is deformed, and the more force it carries. The second moment of area (I) captures this: I = ∫y² dA, where y is the distance from the neutral axis. Because of the y² term, material far from the centre contributes much more than material near it. Doubling the distance from the centre quadruples the contribution. This is why corrugation works — it pushes material away from the centre (up and down in the waves), increasing I without adding area. It is also why an I-beam is efficient: the flanges (far from the centre) carry most of the load, and the web (near the centre) just holds them apart. A solid square bar wastes material near the centre where y is small and the y² contribution is tiny.
2. **Q:** A flat card 5 cm wide holds 2 books. The same card folded into an I-beam holds 8 books. The flanges are 2.5 cm from the neutral axis. Estimate the factor of improvement and explain why it is more than just the area redistribution.
   **Worked Answer:** The I-beam holds 4× the load (8/2 = 4). The improvement comes from the second moment of area. For a flat card of thickness t and width b, I_flat = bt³/12 (thin and flat — small I). For the I-beam, the flanges (area A each) are at distance h = 2.5 cm from the centre. I_beam ≈ 2 × A × h² (the flanges dominate; the web contributes little). The ratio I_beam/I_flat ≈ 2Ah² / (bt³/12). Since the same material is used, 2A + web ≈ bt (total area conserved). If the flanges take most of the material, 2A ≈ bt, so I_beam/I_flat ≈ bt × h² / (bt³/12) = 12h²/t². If t ≈ 0.3 mm (card) and h = 25 mm, then 12 × (25/0.3)² = 12 × 6944 ≈ 83,000. The theoretical improvement is enormous, but in practice the card buckles and the joints fail, so the actual improvement is ~4×. The point: shape can improve stiffness by orders of magnitude, but real structures are limited by buckling and joint strength, not just the second moment of area.
3. **Q:** A corrugated mabati roof sheet has corrugations 3 cm high and 7 cm apart. A flat sheet of the same thickness sags under its own weight. Explain why the corrugated sheet does not, and estimate the stiffness improvement.
   **Worked Answer:** The corrugations push the material 3 cm away from the neutral axis (up and down in the waves). The second moment of area per unit width increases from I_flat = t³/12 (t = sheet thickness, ~0.5 mm) to I_corrugated ≈ t × h² (h = 3 cm = 30 mm, the corrugation height). Ratio: I_corrugated/I_flat = t × h² / (t³/12) = 12h²/t² = 12 × (30/0.5)² = 12 × 3600 = 43,200. So the corrugated sheet is ~43,000× stiffer than the flat sheet of the same thickness. This is why a 0.5 mm flat sheet sags but a 0.5 mm corrugated sheet spans roof rafters at 60–90 cm spacing without sagging. The corrugations do what a much thicker flat sheet would do, but with far less material — saving weight and cost. This is why all roofing iron in Kenya is corrugated.

## Safety Notes
- Cardboard edges can be sharp — the teacher may pre-cut the panels.
- Do not stand on the paper or card bridges — they are for books, not people.
- Books falling from a collapsed bridge can land on feet — keep toes clear.
- Scissors should be used carefully — younger learners may need help with cutting.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should understand tension and compression from everyday experience (pulling vs squeezing). No prior formula is needed — this lesson introduces shape-based strength qualitatively, with the second moment of area explained intuitively.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the build and the qualitative observation (flat flops, folded holds). Ages 14–17 should explain the second moment of area and estimate the stiffness improvement from corrugation.
- Local resource tips: A4 paper, cardboard boxes, and books are available in any school. Corrugated mabati is everywhere in Kenya — learners see it daily but may not have thought about why it is corrugated. If a piece of mabati is available, pass it around and let learners feel the corrugations.
- The key insight: strength comes from where you put the material, not how much you use. This is the foundation of structural engineering — I-beams, corrugated sheets, and trusses all exploit this principle. The lesson leads into Lesson 288 (Strong Shapes II: The Arch), where the shape carries load in compression.
- The I-beam demo is powerful because it uses the same amount of cardboard as the flat card but holds much more. If the I-beam joints fail before the material does, discuss why — in real I-beams, the flanges and web are welded or rolled as one piece, so the joints are as strong as the material.
