# Lesson 288: Strong Shapes II: The Arch

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a card arch between two books and show that it carries load in compression.
2. Explain why an arch pushes outward at its base and how a keystone locks the structure.
3. Demonstrate that an eggshell dome (or a half-eggshell) can carry a surprising load due to its curved shape.
4. Identify arches in local structures — doorways, culverts, bridges — and explain why traditional stone arches need no cement.

## Materials
- Strips of cardboard (from old boxes) — about 2 cm wide and 15–20 cm long
- Two sturdy books or bricks (to serve as abutments — the supports at the arch base)
- A few small books (to load the arch)
- Several raw eggs (for the eggshell dome demo — teacher-led)
- A flat surface (table or desk)
- A piece of stone or brick with a flat top (optional — to load the eggshell)
- Pictures or local examples of arches (doorways, culverts, bridges)
- Tape (optional — to hold the card arch in place during construction)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **arch** (dira) — a curved structure that carries load by pushing downward and outward along its curve
- **compression** (banyazo) — a squeezing force; arches work entirely in compression
- **abutment** (nguzo za dira) — the supports at the base of the arch that resist the outward push
- **keystone** (jiwe la ufunguo) — the top centre stone that locks the arch together
- **thrust** (sukuma) — the outward force at the base of an arch that must be resisted by the abutments
- **dome** (kuba) — a 3D arch rotated in a circle; carries load in compression from all directions

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A flat beam bends and can snap. A curved arch over the same gap can hold more weight. Why?
2. What stops the two halves of an arch from pushing apart at the base?
3. Name one structure near your school or home that uses an arch shape.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The arch carries the load in compression — the curve channels the downward force along the arch and into the supports. Stone and brick are strong in compression, so the arch uses the material's strength. A flat beam bends, putting the bottom in tension (which stone cannot take well).
2. The abutments — the supports at the base — resist the outward thrust. If the abutments are not strong enough, the arch spreads and collapses.
3. Answers will vary: doorway arches, culverts under roads, the arch of a bridge, traditional stone arches in old buildings, the dome of a mosque.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show a picture of an old stone bridge or a local culvert with an arch shape. Ask: *why is it curved and not flat?* A flat stone slab across a gap would crack — stone is weak in tension. The arch curves the load into compression, which stone handles well. Hold up two card strips in a shallow arch between two books. Ask: *what happens if I push down on top?* The arch pushes outward at the base — the books (abutments) must hold. Board: **arch = compression all the way down; the base pushes outward**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the card arch (see Hands-On Build). They lean card strips against each other between two books to form an arch. They test how many small books it holds. They observe what happens when the abutments (the books at the base) are moved apart — the arch collapses. The teacher demonstrates the eggshell dome: stand a half-eggshell on the table, dome up, and place a flat book on it gently. The shell holds surprising weight because the curve channels the load into compression. Results go on a class table: shape | load held | mode of failure.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. An arch carries load entirely in compression. The curve channels the downward force along the arch and into the abutments. At the base, the arch pushes outward (thrust) — the abutments must resist this sideways push or the arch spreads and falls. The keystone at the top is the last piece placed — it wedges all the other stones (voussoirs) into compression, locking the arch. This is why traditional stone arches need no cement — the compression holds them together once the keystone is in. A dome is a 3D arch: rotate an arch in a circle and you get a dome, which carries load in compression from every direction. That is why an eggshell — thin and fragile — can hold a book: the curve channels the load into compression, and the shell material is strong in compression. Board: **arches and domes = compression structures; the abutments resist the outward thrust**.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *arches carry load in compression; the keystone locks it; abutments resist the outward push*. Homework: sketch three arches you can find between home and school (doorway, culvert, bridge). Mark the keystone and the direction of thrust at the base.

## Hands-On Build: Card Arch and Eggshell Dome
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- 4–6 strips of cardboard (from old boxes), each about 2 cm wide and 15–20 cm long
- Two sturdy books or bricks (to serve as abutments)
- Several small books (to load the arch)
- 2–3 raw eggs (for the eggshell dome demo — teacher-led)
- A flat, clean table
- Tape (optional — to hold the arch during construction)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Place two sturdy books (abutments) 12–15 cm apart on the table. **Checkpoint:** the abutments are stable and will not slide — if needed, tape them to the table.
2. Lean 4–6 card strips against each other between the abutments to form a shallow arch. Start from each abutment and work toward the centre — the last strip (the keystone) wedges at the top. **Checkpoint:** the arch holds its shape without tape — the compression keeps it standing.
3. Place a small book gently on top of the arch (at the keystone). Observe: the arch should hold. Add more books one at a time. Count how many it holds. **Checkpoint:** load gently — dropping a book is an impact that can collapse the arch.
4. Now move the abutments 2 cm further apart. Observe: the arch flattens, spreads, and collapses. **Checkpoint:** this shows the outward thrust — without strong abutments, the arch fails.
5. Teacher demo: carefully break an egg in half (across the equator). Rinse the halves. Place one half dome-up on the table. Place a flat, light book on it gently. Observe: the dome holds. Add a second book. **Checkpoint:** the eggshell is thin and fragile, but the dome shape channels the load into compression — the shell holds.
6. Record results on the class table: shape | load held | mode of failure (crushing, spreading, buckling).

### Common Mistakes
- **Abutments too light** — if the abutments slide, the arch spreads and collapses before any load is added. Use heavy books or bricks, or tape them down.
- **Arch too flat** — a very shallow arch has high outward thrust and little compression benefit. Make the arch rise at least 3–4 cm above the base line.
- **Keystone not centred** — if the top strip is off-centre, one side takes more load and the arch twists. Centre the keystone carefully.
- **Loading off-centre** — placing the book on one side of the arch puts asymmetric load on it and causes early collapse. Load at the centre (keystone).
- **Eggshell on an uneven surface** — the shell must sit flat. A wobble means one side takes all the load and cracks. Use a flat, clean table.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Why can an arch hold more weight than a flat beam of the same material? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is the keystone, and what does it do? (mirrors pre-test)
3. What happens to an arch if the abutments are moved apart? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Why can a thin eggshell dome hold a book without cracking?
5. Challenge: traditional stone arches need no cement. What holds them together?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The arch channels the downward load into compression along the curve and into the abutments. Stone and brick are strong in compression, so the arch uses the material's strength. A flat beam bends, putting the bottom in tension — stone is weak in tension and cracks.
2. The keystone is the top centre stone (or card strip). It is the last piece placed, and it wedges all the other stones into compression, locking the arch together.
3. The arch spreads and collapses. The abutments resist the outward thrust; without them, the arch pushes apart and falls under its own weight (or any load).
4. The dome shape channels the downward load into compression across the entire curved surface. The shell material is strong in compression, so even a thin shell can carry a significant load. The curve distributes the force, so no single point takes the full load.
5. Compression. Once the keystone is placed, every stone in the arch is pushed into compression by the load above. The compression locks the stones together — they press against each other and cannot fall. Cement is only needed to fill gaps and weatherproof; the structural integrity comes from the compression.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Trace the compression path through a stone arch. Why did traditional builders need no cement?
   **Worked Answer:** Consider a semicircular stone arch with voussoirs (wedge-shaped stones). A downward load on the keystone pushes it down. The keystone is wedge-shaped, so pushing it down pushes the adjacent stones outward and down. Each of those pushes the next stone outward and down, and so on, all the way to the abutments. At every joint, the force is transmitted as compression — the stones press against each other. The line of action of the compression force follows a curve (the "line of thrust") that stays within the arch. As long as the line of thrust stays inside the stones (does not exit the arch surface), the arch is stable. No cement is needed because the compression at each joint holds the stones in place — gravity and geometry do the work. Cement is added only to fill gaps, prevent water ingress, and protect against weathering. Traditional builders (Roman aqueducts, medieval bridges, Kenyan stone culverts) relied on this principle: cut the stones accurately so the joints are tight, and the compression does the rest. The arch stands because every stone is squeezed, not because it is glued.
2. **Q:** An eggshell dome holds 2 kg before cracking. The shell is 0.3 mm thick and the dome radius is 30 mm. Estimate the compressive stress in the shell and compare with the compressive strength of eggshell (~30 MPa).
   **Worked Answer:** Load F = 2 kg × 9.8 = 19.6 N. The dome carries the load in compression across its curved surface. Approximate the load as distributed over a ring at the base: the circumference is 2πr = 2π × 30 mm = 188 mm. The cross-sectional area of the shell ring is A = circumference × thickness = 188 mm × 0.3 mm = 56.4 mm² = 56.4 × 10⁻⁶ m². Compressive stress σ = F/A = 19.6 / (56.4 × 10⁻⁶) = 3.47 × 10⁵ Pa = 0.35 MPa. This is well below the compressive strength of eggshell (~30 MPa), so the shell has a large safety margin. The dome fails not because the material exceeds its compressive strength, but because of local buckling or stress concentration at imperfections (micro-cracks in the shell). This is why the dome can hold far more than a flat piece of the same shell — the shape distributes the load, and the material is strong in compression.
3. **Q:** A culvert under a rural road is a semicircular stone arch with a span of 1.5 m. The abutments must resist an outward thrust of 15 kN. If the abutment is a brick wall 0.5 m wide, how heavy must the wall be to resist the thrust (assuming friction coefficient μ = 0.6)?
   **Worked Answer:** The outward thrust is 15 kN. The abutment resists it by friction: F_friction = μ × N, where N is the weight of the wall. For the wall to resist: μ × N ≥ 15 kN → N ≥ 15/0.6 = 25 kN. So the wall must weigh at least 25 kN. Mass: m = W/g = 25,000/9.8 = 2550 kg. If brick density is ~1800 kg/m³, the volume needed is V = m/ρ = 2550/1800 = 1.42 m³. The wall is 0.5 m wide, so the required height (for a 1 m length of wall) is h = V/(0.5 × 1) = 2.83 m. So the abutment wall must be at least 2.8 m high and 0.5 m wide (per metre of length) to resist the thrust by friction alone. In practice, the wall is also embedded in the ground, which provides additional passive resistance, so the required height is less. This is why arch culverts have thick, heavy abutments — they must resist the outward thrust, or the arch spreads and collapses.

## Safety Notes
- The eggshell dome demo is teacher-led — raw eggs can carry salmonella. Wash hands after handling.
- Do not eat the eggs used in the demo.
- Card arches can collapse suddenly — keep fingers clear when loading.
- Books falling from a collapsed arch can land on feet — keep toes clear.
- Broken eggshells are sharp — collect and dispose of them immediately.
- If a brick is used as an abutment, ensure it does not fall on anyone's foot.
