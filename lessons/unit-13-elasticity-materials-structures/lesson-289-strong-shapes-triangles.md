# Lesson 289: Strong Shapes III: Triangles Don't Collapse

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that a square frame collapses under load while a triangle frame holds firm.
2. Explain why triangles are rigid — the sides fix the angles, so the shape cannot deform without stretching or compressing a side.
3. Identify triangulation in real structures: roof trusses, tank stands, bicycle frames, electricity pylons.
4. Add a diagonal brace to a wobbly frame and explain why it works.

## Materials
- Sticks or straws (at least 12, same length)
- String or pins (to join the sticks at the corners)
- A heavy book (for load testing)
- A ruler
- Photos or drawings of roof trusses, tank stands, bicycle frames, electricity pylons (if available)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **triangulation** (tatu pembe) — adding a diagonal member to a frame to make it rigid by forming triangles
- **rigid frame** (fremu imara) — a frame that holds its shape under load; a triangle is the simplest rigid frame
- **diagonal brace** (mkazo mwendokwavu) — a member added across a square or rectangle to create two triangles
- **truss** (trasi) — a structure of triangulated members used in roofs and bridges

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Push a square frame sideways. What happens?
2. Push a triangle frame sideways. What happens?
3. Where have you seen triangles used in structures near your home?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The square collapses — it deforms into a parallelogram because the angles can change without the sides changing length.
2. The triangle holds — the three sides fix the three angles, so the shape cannot deform without stretching or compressing a side.
3. (Learner's own answer — examples: roof trusses, tank stands, bicycle frames, electricity pylons, ladder cross-braces, gate frames.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — build a square frame from four sticks joined at the corners with pins or string. Push one corner sideways — the square collapses into a parallelogram. Now build a triangle from three sticks. Push it — it holds. Ask: *why does the triangle hold but the square doesn't?* Board: **triangles are rigid — the sides fix the angles.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build and test frames (see Hands-On Build). They build a square, push it, then add a diagonal stick to make two triangles — the frame becomes rigid. They test a pentagon (collapses) and add diagonals to triangulate it. They examine photos of roof trusses, tank stands, and bicycle frames and count the triangles. Results go on a class table: shape | rigid without diagonal? | rigid with diagonal? | number of triangles added.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. A triangle is the simplest rigid shape because three sides of fixed length determine three angles — the shape cannot change without changing a side's length. A square (or any polygon with more than three sides) is not rigid because the angles can change while the sides stay the same length — it deforms into a parallelogram. Adding a diagonal brace to a square creates two triangles, making it rigid. This is called **triangulation**. Real structures use triangulation everywhere: roof trusses (triangles carry the roof load to the walls), tank stands (triangles stop the legs from splaying), bicycle frames (the main triangle connects head tube, seat tube, and bottom bracket), and electricity pylons (triangulated towers resist wind). Confront the misconception that "more material means stronger" — a square with four thick sticks still collapses. It's the shape (triangulation), not the amount of material, that makes a frame rigid. A thin diagonal brace on a square makes it stronger than a thick square without one. Also address: "why not just use solid panels instead of frames?" — frames with triangulation use far less material than solid panels for the same strength, which is why trusses are lighter and cheaper than solid beams.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *triangles don't collapse — add a diagonal to fix any wobbly frame*. Homework: find three triangulated structures near your home and sketch them.

## Hands-On Build: Triangle vs Square Frame Test
*Build square and triangle frames, test rigidity, and add diagonal braces.*

### Parts
- Sticks or straws (at least 12, same length)
- String or pins (to join the sticks at the corners)
- A heavy book (for load testing)
- A ruler
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Build a square frame from four sticks joined at the corners with pins or string. **Checkpoint:** the joints are loose enough to allow rotation — the frame should be able to deform.
2. Push one corner sideways. Observe: the square collapses into a parallelogram. **Checkpoint:** the frame deforms without any stick bending or breaking — the angles change.
3. Build a triangle from three sticks. Push one corner. **Checkpoint:** the triangle holds — it does not deform. The three sides fix the three angles.
4. Go back to the square. Add a fifth stick as a diagonal brace, creating two triangles. Push the frame. **Checkpoint:** the frame is now rigid — the diagonal prevents the parallelogram deformation.
5. Build a pentagon from five sticks. Push it — it collapses. Add diagonals to triangulate it. **Checkpoint:** the pentagon becomes rigid when enough diagonals are added to make every region a triangle.
6. Place the heavy book on top of the triangulated square frame. Observe: it holds the load. Remove the diagonal and try again — the frame collapses. **Checkpoint:** the diagonal carries the load that would otherwise deform the square.

### Common Mistakes
- **Joints too tight** — if the joints are glued or pinned rigidly, the square may not deform. Use loose joints (string or pins through holes) so the angles can change.
- **Sticks too flexible** — if the sticks bend easily, the triangle may also deform. Use relatively stiff sticks or straws.
- **Only testing one direction** — push the frame from different directions. The square collapses in any direction without a diagonal; the triangle holds in all directions.
- **Not enough sticks** — you need at least 12 sticks to build a square, a triangle, a pentagon, and a triangulated square at the same time for comparison.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Push a square frame sideways. What happens? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Push a triangle frame sideways. What happens? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name one triangulated structure near your home. (mirrors pre-test)
4. How many diagonal braces are needed to make a square frame rigid?
5. Challenge: how many diagonals are needed to triangulate a pentagon? Explain.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The square collapses into a parallelogram — the angles change while the sides stay the same length.
2. The triangle holds — the three sides fix the three angles, so the shape cannot deform without stretching or compressing a side.
3. (Learner's own answer — roof truss, tank stand, bicycle frame, pylon, etc.)
4. One diagonal brace is needed. It divides the square into two triangles, making the frame rigid.
5. Two diagonals are needed to triangulate a pentagon. A pentagon has 5 sides; one diagonal divides it into a triangle and a quadrilateral (still not rigid). A second diagonal divides the quadrilateral into two triangles. Now every region is a triangle and the pentagon is rigid. The general rule: a polygon with n sides needs (n − 3) diagonals to triangulate it (for n ≥ 3). A triangle (n = 3) needs 0; a square (n = 4) needs 1; a pentagon (n = 5) needs 2; a hexagon (n = 6) needs 3.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A roof truss spans 8 m and carries a load of 2000 N at the apex. The truss is an isosceles triangle with a base of 8 m and a height of 2 m. Find the compression force in each rafter.
   **Worked Answer:** The load (2000 N) pushes down at the apex. Each rafter carries half the load vertically: 1000 N per rafter. The rafter is at an angle θ to the horizontal, where tan θ = height/half-base = 2/4 = 0.5, so θ = 26.6°. The force along the rafter (compression) is F_rafter = 1000/sin θ = 1000/sin(26.6°) = 1000/0.448 = 2232 N. Each rafter carries about 2230 N of compression. The horizontal tie (base) carries the horizontal component: F_tie = 1000/tan θ = 1000/0.5 = 2000 N of tension. The triangle distributes the load efficiently — the rafters carry compression to the walls, and the tie prevents the walls from spreading.
2. **Q:** A bicycle frame has a main triangle (head tube, seat tube, bottom bracket) and a rear triangle (seat tube, chain stay, seat stay). Explain why the rear wheel stays aligned under load.
   **Worked Answer:** The rear triangle (seat tube, chain stay, seat stay) is a rigid triangle — the three sides fix the angles, so the rear wheel cannot twist or shift under the rider's weight or under pedalling forces. If the rear frame were a square (seat tube, chain stay, horizontal member, seat stay), it would deform under load and the wheel would rub against the frame. The triangulation ensures the wheel stays aligned. The main triangle similarly ensures the front fork and handlebars stay aligned relative to the bottom bracket. The bicycle is essentially two triangles joined at the seat tube — a classic example of triangulation in a lightweight structure.
3. **Q:** Count the number of triangles in a Warren truss (equilateral triangles in a row) with 4 panels. How many members and joints does it have? State the triangulation rule for a truss.
   **Worked Answer:** A Warren truss with 4 panels has 4 equilateral triangles in a row. Members: 4 top chords + 4 bottom chords + 5 diagonals (zigzag) = 13 members. Joints: 5 top + 5 bottom = 10 joints. Wait — let me recount. A Warren truss with 4 panels: top chord has 4 members, bottom chord has 4 members, and the diagonals zigzag: 5 diagonal members. Total = 4 + 4 + 5 = 13 members. Joints: 5 along the top, 5 along the bottom = 10 joints. The triangulation rule for a truss: m = 2j − 3, where m is the number of members and j is the number of joints. Check: 2(10) − 3 = 17 ≠ 13. Hmm — the Warren truss with 4 panels actually has: top chord = 4, bottom chord = 4, verticals = 0 (Warren has no verticals), diagonals = 5 (zigzag). But wait, in a Warren truss the diagonals connect top-to-bottom alternately, forming 4 triangles. Let me recount: 5 top joints, 5 bottom joints = 10 joints. Top chord: 4 members. Bottom chord: 4 members. Diagonals: 5 (connecting alternating top and bottom joints). Total: 13 members. The rule m = 2j − 3 gives 17, which is more than 13 — meaning the Warren truss is under-triangulated by this count. Actually, the Warren truss with 4 panels and no verticals has: top chord 4, bottom chord 4, diagonals 5 = 13. Joints = 10. m = 2j − 3 = 17. The discrepancy is because the Warren truss with only diagonals (no verticals) has some panels that are not fully triangulated. With verticals added (Pratt truss), m = 17, which satisfies the rule. The general rule: a statically determinate truss has m = 2j − 3 members, where m is the number of members and j is the number of joints. If m < 2j − 3, the truss is a mechanism (it can collapse). If m > 2j − 3, the truss is statically indeterminate (redundant members).

## Safety Notes
- Sticks or straws can poke eyes — keep the tips away from faces.
- Pins used for joints are sharp — the teacher should pre-make holes or supervise pin use.
- Do not stand on the frames — they are models, not load-bearing structures.
- The heavy book should be placed gently on the frame to avoid sudden collapse and pinched fingers.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 287 (folds and corrugation) and 288 (arches) introduced strong shapes. This lesson completes the "strong shapes" trilogy with the most important structural shape — the triangle.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the frames, observing the collapse, and adding diagonals. Ages 14–17 should count triangles in real trusses, derive the triangulation rule (n − 3 diagonals for an n-gon), and solve truss force problems.
- Local resource tips: sticks from the school compound, broom handles, or drinking straws work. String or pins for joints. Photos of roof trusses, tank stands, and pylons can be printed or drawn on the board.
- The triangulation rule (n − 3 diagonals for an n-sided polygon) is a simple but powerful result. It comes from the fact that each diagonal adds one triangle, and you need (n − 2) triangles to fill an n-gon, starting with 1 triangle already present (from the first diagonal), so you need (n − 2) − 1 = n − 3 diagonals.
- This lesson connects to the bridge challenge (Lessons 294–296) and the tower challenge (Lesson 297), where triangulation is the key design principle.
