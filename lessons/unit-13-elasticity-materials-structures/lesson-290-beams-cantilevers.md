# Lesson 290: Beams and Cantilevers

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that a beam supported at both ends sags in the middle under load, with the top stretching (tension) and the bottom compressing (compression).
2. Show that a cantilever (fixed at one end) sags at the free end, with the top compressing and the bottom stretching.
3. Explain why an I-beam puts material at the top and bottom — where the forces are greatest.
4. Relate beam depth to stiffness: doubling the depth quarters the sag.

## Materials
- A ruler (or a thin flat stick) — the beam
- Two books (supports)
- Weights (water bottles or stones, 0.5–2 kg)
- A sponge or a thick piece of foam (marked with vertical lines) — to visualise stretching and compressing
- A piece of bamboo split lengthwise (optional, for the I-beam demo)
- Cardboard strips (for folding into I-beam or box-beam shapes)
- A ruler or tape measure (to measure sag)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **beam** (bimu) — a horizontal member supported at both ends that carries a load
- **cantilever** (kantileva) — a beam fixed at one end only, with the other end free
- **sag** (kushuka) — the downward deflection of a beam under load
- **I-beam** (bimu ya I) — a beam shaped like the letter I, with material concentrated at the top and bottom where forces are greatest
- **neutral axis** (mhimili wa kati) — the line through the middle of a beam where neither tension nor compression occurs

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Place a ruler across two books and push down in the middle. What happens to the top of the ruler?
2. What happens to the bottom of the ruler?
3. Which sags more: a thick beam or a thin beam (same material, same load)?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The top of the ruler stretches (tension) — it gets longer as the beam curves downward.
2. The bottom of the ruler compresses (compression) — it gets shorter as the beam curves downward.
3. The thin beam sags more. A thicker beam is stiffer — doubling the depth quarters the sag (the stiffness grows with the square of the depth).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — place a ruler across two books. Push down in the middle — it sags. Ask: *what is happening to the top and bottom of the ruler?* Use a marked sponge to show: the top lines stretch apart (tension), the bottom lines squeeze together (compression). Board: **top = tension, bottom = compression, middle = neutral.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs test beams and cantilevers (see Hands-On Build). They support a ruler between two books, load it with water bottles, and measure the sag. They clamp one end of a ruler to a desk edge (cantilever) and load the free end — measure the sag. They fold cardboard into an I-shape and compare its stiffness to a flat strip of the same cardboard. They try doubling the beam depth (stack two rulers) and measure the sag reduction. Results go on a class table: beam type | depth | load | sag.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. A beam supported at both ends sags in the middle. The top stretches (tension), the bottom compresses (compression), and the middle (the neutral axis) does neither. This is why an I-beam is shaped like an I — the material is concentrated at the top and bottom flanges, where the forces are greatest, and the web (the vertical part) just keeps the flanges apart. The middle (neutral axis) carries little force, so there is no need for material there — the I-shape saves weight without losing strength. A cantilever (fixed at one end) is the opposite: the top compresses and the bottom stretches, because the beam curves the other way. The stiffness of a beam grows with the square of its depth: doubling the depth makes the beam 4× stiffer (the sag is quartered). This is because the forces in the flanges are proportional to the distance from the neutral axis — a deeper beam puts the flanges farther from the neutral axis, so they resist the bending more effectively. Confront the misconception that "a thicker beam is just stronger because it has more material" — it's not the amount of material but where it is placed. An I-beam with the same amount of material as a flat bar is much stiffer because the material is at the top and bottom, far from the neutral axis. Also address: "the middle of the beam does nothing" — the web (middle) carries shear force, which prevents the top and bottom from sliding past each other. Without the web, the flanges would slide and the beam would fail. The web doesn't carry much bending force, but it is essential for shear.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *top stretches, bottom compresses — put material where the forces are*. Homework: find an I-beam or box beam in a local building or gate and sketch its cross-section.

## Hands-On Build: Beam and Cantilever Test
*Test beams and cantilevers, measure sag, and build an I-beam from cardboard.*

### Parts
- A ruler (or a thin flat stick) — the beam
- Two books (supports)
- Weights (water bottles or stones, 0.5–2 kg)
- A sponge or thick foam (marked with vertical lines) — to visualise stretching and compressing
- Cardboard strips (for folding into I-beam shapes)
- A ruler or tape measure (to measure sag)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Place the ruler across two books, 20 cm apart. Measure the height of the ruler at midspan (no load). **Checkpoint:** the ruler is level — the zero-load position is recorded.
2. Place a 0.5 kg water bottle at midspan. Measure the sag (the drop from the zero-load position). **Checkpoint:** the sag is recorded in mm.
3. Increase the load to 1 kg, then 2 kg. Measure the sag each time. **Checkpoint:** the sag grows with load — roughly proportional for small sags.
4. Mark the sponge with vertical lines on the side. Bend it into a curve (like a sagging beam). Observe: the top lines stretch apart (tension), the bottom lines squeeze together (compression). **Checkpoint:** the top is in tension, the bottom is in compression, the middle is neutral.
5. Clamp one end of the ruler to a desk edge (cantilever). Load the free end. Measure the sag. Observe the sponge: the top compresses, the bottom stretches (opposite of the simply supported beam). **Checkpoint:** the cantilever bends the other way — top compression, bottom tension.
6. Fold a cardboard strip into an I-shape (two flanges and a web). Place it across the books and load it. Compare the sag with a flat strip of the same cardboard. **Checkpoint:** the I-beam sags much less than the flat strip — the material at the top and bottom resists the bending.
7. Stack two rulers (doubling the depth) and load them. Compare the sag with a single ruler under the same load. **Checkpoint:** the sag is about 4× less — doubling the depth quarters the sag.

### Common Mistakes
- **Measuring from the wrong reference** — always measure sag from the zero-load position, not from the desk surface.
- **Overloading the ruler** — too much weight can break the ruler. Use water bottles up to 2 kg.
- **Not marking the sponge** — without vertical lines, you can't see the stretching and compressing. Use a marker to draw lines.
- **Cardboard I-beam too small** — the flanges need to be far apart for the effect to be visible. Use a tall I-shape (at least 3 cm deep).
- **Stacking rulers without fixing them** — if the rulers can slide against each other, they act as two separate beams, not one deep beam. Tape them together.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A beam supported at both ends sags. What happens to the top? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What happens to the bottom? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Which sags more: a thick beam or a thin beam? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Why is an I-beam shaped like an I? Where is the material concentrated?
5. Challenge: if doubling the beam depth quarters the sag, what happens to the sag if you triple the depth?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The top stretches (tension) — it gets longer as the beam curves downward.
2. The bottom compresses (compression) — it gets shorter as the beam curves downward.
3. The thin beam sags more. A thicker beam is stiffer — the stiffness grows with the square of the depth.
4. The material is concentrated at the top and bottom flanges, where the tension and compression forces are greatest. The web (middle) just keeps the flanges apart and carries shear. The I-shape puts material where the forces are and removes it from the neutral axis, where it would do nothing — saving weight without losing strength.
5. The stiffness grows with the square of the depth: stiffness ∝ depth². Tripling the depth gives 3² = 9× the stiffness, so the sag is reduced by a factor of 9 (the sag is 1/9 of the original). This is why a small increase in beam depth gives a large increase in stiffness — the square relationship makes depth the most efficient way to stiffen a beam.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A wooden beam (2 cm wide, 4 cm deep) sags 10 mm under a 50 N load. If you double the depth to 8 cm (same width, same load), what is the new sag?
   **Worked Answer:** The stiffness of a rectangular beam is proportional to the square of the depth: stiffness ∝ depth². Doubling the depth from 4 cm to 8 cm gives 2² = 4× the stiffness. The sag is inversely proportional to stiffness, so the new sag = 10/4 = 2.5 mm. The deeper beam sags only 2.5 mm — a quarter of the original. This is why simply making a beam deeper is far more efficient than making it wider: doubling the width only doubles the stiffness (sag halved), but doubling the depth quadruples it (sag quartered).
2. **Q:** A cantilever beam (fixed at one end, 1 m long) carries a 20 N load at the free end. The top of the beam is in compression and the bottom is in tension. Explain why the forces are reversed compared to a simply supported beam.
   **Worked Answer:** In a simply supported beam (supported at both ends, loaded in the middle), the beam curves downward at midspan: the top stretches (tension) and the bottom compresses (compression). In a cantilever (fixed at one end, loaded at the free end), the beam curves downward at the free end, but the curvature is opposite: the top of the beam is concave (compressed) and the bottom is convex (stretched). This is because the fixed end acts like a pivot, and the load at the free end bends the beam downward — the top of the beam is being squeezed together by the bending, while the bottom is being pulled apart. The neutral axis is still in the middle, carrying neither tension nor compression. The forces reverse because the bending direction reverses: the cantilever bends "the other way" compared to a simply supported beam.
3. **Q:** A shelf bracket (cantilever) extends 30 cm from the wall. The shelf carries 100 N of books distributed evenly. Where is the maximum bending moment, and what is its value?
   **Worked Answer:** For a cantilever with a uniformly distributed load (UDL), the maximum bending moment is at the fixed end (the wall). The total load is 100 N, spread over 0.3 m. The UDL is 100/0.3 = 333 N/m. The maximum bending moment for a UDL cantilever is M = wL²/2, where w is the UDL and L is the length. M = 333 × 0.3²/2 = 333 × 0.09/2 = 333 × 0.045 = 15 N·m. The maximum bending moment is 15 N·m at the wall. This is where the bracket is most likely to fail — the tension at the bottom of the bracket at the wall is greatest. This is why shelf brackets are thicker at the wall end and thinner at the free end, or have a diagonal brace from the wall down to the free end (triangulation, Lesson 289).

## Safety Notes
- Do not overload the ruler — it can snap and cause injury. Use water bottles up to 2 kg maximum.
- The cantilever clamp must be secure — if the ruler slips, the load can fall on feet.
- Cardboard edges can be sharp — smooth them with sandpaper.
- The sponge demo is safe but should not be torn — mark it gently with a marker.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 287–289 (strong shapes: folds, arches, triangles) introduced structural shapes. This lesson focuses on beams — the most common structural member in buildings and furniture.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the sponge demo (top stretches, bottom compresses) and the sag measurement. Ages 14–17 should derive the depth² relationship, solve bending moment problems, and explain the I-beam quantitatively.
- Local resource tips: a ruler is the simplest beam. A sponge or foam is available in most markets. Cardboard from boxes can be folded into I-beam shapes. Bamboo split lengthwise is a natural I-beam — the hard outer fibres are the flanges, the soft inner pith is the web.
- The depth² relationship (doubling depth = 4× stiffness) is one of the most important rules of thumb in structural engineering. It comes from the second moment of area (I ∝ depth³ for a rectangular beam, and the stiffness is EI/L³, so the sag is proportional to 1/depth³ — but for the simple rule of thumb, stiffness ∝ depth² is a close approximation for comparing beams of the same material and width).
- This lesson connects to the bridge challenge (Lessons 294–296), where beam theory helps learners design bridges that don't sag. The I-beam and box-beam shapes are directly applicable to the bridge design.
