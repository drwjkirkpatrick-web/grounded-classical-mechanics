# Lesson 291: Tension vs Compression: Reading a Structure

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3; G10-PHY-1.5  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Identify whether each member of a simple structure is in tension (T) or compression (C).
2. Use the "remove it" test: if removing a member would make the structure collapse inward, it was in compression; if it would fall apart, it was in tension.
3. Label every member of a tank stand and a roof truss as T or C.
4. Explain why ropes and cables can only carry tension, while pillars and struts can only carry compression.

## Materials
- A model tank stand (made from sticks and string, or from the previous lesson's materials)
- A model roof truss (made from sticks, or a drawing on the board)
- Coloured cards or stickers — red for Tension, blue for Compression
- A rope or string
- A stick or ruler (as a strut)
- Photos or drawings of real structures: tank stands, roof trusses, electricity pylons, gate frames
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **tension (T)** (mvutano) — a pulling force; the member is being stretched
- **compression (C)** (mbubanso) — a pushing force; the member is being squeezed
- **member** (kipengele) — a single part of a structure (a beam, strut, tie, or cable)
- **tie** (kamba) — a member designed to carry tension only (rope, cable, chain)
- **strut** (nguzo) — a member designed to carry compression only (pillar, post, brace)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A rope hangs from a ceiling with a weight on the end. Is the rope in tension or compression?
2. A pillar supports a roof. Is the pillar in tension or compression?
3. Can a rope carry compression? Why or why not?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Tension — the weight pulls the rope downward, stretching it. The rope carries the pulling force.
2. Compression — the roof pushes down on the pillar, squeezing it. The pillar carries the pushing force.
3. No — a rope (or cable or chain) can only pull, not push. If you push on a rope, it buckles (goes slack). Ropes can only carry tension. Pillars and struts can carry compression but buckle if put in tension (a tall thin pillar can't be pulled).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold a rope between two hands and pull. The rope is in tension. Now push the rope — it buckles, it can't carry compression. Hold a stick between two hands and push — the stick is in compression. Now pull the stick — it can carry tension too (but a tall thin stick would buckle if you tried to compress it along its length). Board: **T = tension (pulling), C = compression (pushing). Ropes can only do T. Pillars can only do C.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs label members of real structures (see Hands-On Build). They build a simple tank stand from sticks and string and label each member as T or C. They draw a roof truss and label each member. They use the "remove it" test: if removing a member makes the structure collapse inward (the gap gets smaller), it was in compression; if removing it makes the structure fall apart (the gap gets larger), it was in tension. They label photos of real structures. Results go on a class table: structure | member | T or C | reasoning.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Every member of a structure carries either tension or compression. The "remove it" test is a simple way to determine which: imagine removing the member. If the two joints it connects would move closer together (the structure collapses inward), the member was pushing them apart — it was in compression. If the joints would move apart (the structure falls open), the member was pulling them together — it was in tension. In a tank stand: the legs are in compression (they carry the weight of the tank down to the ground), the horizontal ties are in tension (they stop the legs from splaying outward), and the diagonal braces are in compression (they prevent the frame from deforming). In a roof truss: the rafters are in compression (they carry the roof load down to the walls), the tie beam (bottom chord) is in tension (it stops the walls from spreading), and the vertical members (king post or verticals) are in tension (they pull the rafters down to the tie). Confront the misconception that "all vertical members are in compression" — the vertical king post in a truss is in tension (it pulls the apex down to the tie beam). Also address: "all horizontal members are in tension" — the top chord of a truss under snow load is in compression (the snow pushes down, compressing the top chord). The direction (T or C) depends on the load and the geometry, not just the orientation. The "remove it" test is the reliable way to determine T or C for any member.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *remove it — if it collapses inward, it was C; if it falls open, it was T*. Homework: sketch a gate frame and label each member as T or C.

## Hands-On Build: Label a Tank Stand and a Roof Truss
*Build a model tank stand and a roof truss, and label each member as T or C.*

### Parts
- Sticks or straws (for structural members)
- String (for tension members)
- Pins or tape (for joints)
- Coloured cards or stickers — red for Tension, blue for Compression
- Photos or drawings of tank stands, roof trusses, pylons
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Build a simple tank stand: four vertical legs (sticks), connected by horizontal ties (string or sticks) near the top and bottom, and diagonal braces (sticks or string) on each side. **Checkpoint:** the stand is stable — it doesn't wobble.
2. Apply the "remove it" test to each member. For each leg: imagine removing it — the tank would fall down. The legs push the tank up, so they are in **compression (C)**. Label them blue. **Checkpoint:** all four legs are C.
3. For each horizontal tie: imagine removing it — the legs would splay outward and the stand would collapse. The ties pull the legs together, so they are in **tension (T)**. Label them red. **Checkpoint:** the horizontal ties are T (if they are string) or could be C (if they are rigid sticks under compression — but a horizontal stick between two legs under a downward load is typically in tension if the legs splay outward).
4. For each diagonal brace: imagine removing it — the frame would deform into a parallelogram (Lesson 289). The diagonal prevents this. If the diagonal is a stick, it carries **compression (C)** — it pushes the joints apart. If the diagonal is a string, it carries **tension (T)** — it pulls the joints together. **Checkpoint:** the diagonal is C if it's a stick, T if it's a string.
5. Draw a simple roof truss (triangle with a horizontal tie at the bottom and a vertical king post from the apex to the tie). Label each member. Rafters: C (they carry the roof load down to the walls). Tie beam: T (it stops the walls from spreading). King post: T (it pulls the apex down to the tie). **Checkpoint:** every member is labelled T or C.
6. Check with the "remove it" test: remove the tie beam — the walls spread and the roof collapses. The tie was pulling them together → T. Remove a rafter — the roof sags on that side. The rafter was pushing the load down → C. Remove the king post — the apex sags and the tie beam bends. The king post was pulling the apex down → T. **Checkpoint:** the "remove it" test confirms every label.

### Common Mistakes
- **Assuming all verticals are compression** — the king post (vertical) is in tension. Use the "remove it" test, not the orientation.
- **Assuming all horizontals are tension** — the top chord of a truss under snow load is in compression. Use the "remove it" test.
- **Confusing the load direction with the member force** — the load pushes down, but the tie beam (horizontal) is in tension because it resists the spreading force, not the downward force.
- **Not building the model** — drawings alone are less convincing. Build the stick-and-string model and physically remove members to see the effect.
- **Using rigid sticks for ties** — if the horizontal tie is a rigid stick, it could be in compression (if the legs are being pushed inward by an external force). But under a downward load on the tank, the legs splay outward, so the tie is in tension. Use string to make it unambiguous.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A rope hangs from a ceiling with a weight. Is the rope in T or C? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A pillar supports a roof. Is the pillar in T or C? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Can a rope carry compression? (mirrors pre-test)
4. In a roof truss, is the tie beam (bottom chord) in T or C? Use the "remove it" test.
5. Challenge: in a simple king-post truss under a downward load at the apex, label all members as T or C and justify each.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Tension (T) — the weight pulls the rope downward, stretching it.
2. Compression (C) — the roof pushes down on the pillar, squeezing it.
3. No — a rope can only pull (tension). If you push on a rope, it buckles and goes slack. Ropes cannot carry compression.
4. Tension (T) — remove the tie beam and the walls spread outward (the rafters push them apart). The tie beam pulls the walls together, so it is in tension.
5. Rafters (two sloping sides): C — they carry the roof load down to the walls. Remove a rafter and the roof sags on that side; the rafter was pushing the load down (compression). Tie beam (horizontal bottom): T — remove it and the walls spread; it pulls them together (tension). King post (vertical from apex to tie): T — remove it and the apex sags and the tie bends; it pulls the apex down to the tie (tension). Summary: rafters = C, tie = T, king post = T.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A simple truss has two rafters (each at 30° to the horizontal) and a horizontal tie beam. A load of 1000 N sits at the apex. Find the force in each rafter (compression) and in the tie beam (tension).
   **Worked Answer:** Each rafter carries half the vertical load: 500 N per rafter. The rafter force along its length: F_rafter = 500/sin(30°) = 500/0.5 = 1000 N (compression). The horizontal component of the rafter force: F_horizontal = 500/tan(30°) = 500/0.577 = 866 N. The tie beam carries this horizontal force: F_tie = 866 N (tension). Check: the rafter force (1000 N) has a vertical component of 1000 × sin(30°) = 500 N (half the load) and a horizontal component of 1000 × cos(30°) = 866 N (balanced by the tie). Summary: each rafter carries 1000 N compression, the tie carries 866 N tension.
2. **Q:** A tank stand has four legs, each 2 m tall, carrying a 5000 N tank. The horizontal ties are 1 m long. Find the compression in each leg and the tension in each tie (assume the load is evenly distributed and the legs splay at 5° from vertical).
   **Worked Answer:** Each leg carries 5000/4 = 1250 N vertically. If the legs splay at 5° from vertical, the force along the leg is F_leg = 1250/cos(5°) = 1250/0.996 = 1255 N (compression). The horizontal component (the spreading force) is F_horizontal = 1250 × tan(5°) = 1250 × 0.0875 = 109 N per leg. The tie connects two adjacent legs, so it resists the spreading force from two legs: F_tie = 2 × 109 = 218 N (tension). Summary: each leg carries 1255 N compression, each tie carries 218 N tension. The splay angle is small (5°), so the leg force is only slightly more than the vertical load, and the tie force is modest. But if the splay angle were 15°, the tie force would be 2 × 1250 × tan(15°) = 2 × 1250 × 0.268 = 670 N — much larger. This is why keeping the legs nearly vertical (small splay angle) reduces the tie force and makes the stand more efficient.
3. **Q:** In a Pratt truss (diagonals slope toward the centre) under a central point load, which diagonals are in tension and which are in compression?
   **Worked Answer:** In a Pratt truss under a central downward load, the diagonals closest to the supports (outer panels) are in tension, and the diagonals near the centre are in compression. This is because the shear force is largest near the supports and smallest at the centre. The diagonals carry the shear: in the outer panels, the shear pulls the diagonal (tension); near the centre, the shear pushes the diagonal (compression). The Pratt truss is designed so that the diagonals (which are lighter and can only carry tension efficiently) are in tension in the high-shear regions, while the verticals (which are shorter and can carry compression without buckling) handle the compression. This is why the Pratt truss is efficient — it puts the right members in the right roles.

## Safety Notes
- Sticks and straws can poke eyes — keep tips away from faces.
- Pins are sharp — the teacher should pre-make holes or supervise.
- Do not load the model structures with heavy weights — they are models, not load-bearing.
- Coloured cards should not be placed in mouths.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 287–290 (strong shapes and beams) introduced structural concepts. This lesson teaches learners to "read" a structure — to identify the force in each member.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the "remove it" test and labelling members of a tank stand. Ages 14–17 should solve truss force problems and analyse Pratt/Warren truss configurations.
- Local resource tips: sticks, string, and pins are sufficient. Photos of tank stands, roof trusses, and pylons can be printed or drawn. The school's own water tank stand (if it has one) is a real example.
- The "remove it" test is the most powerful tool for teaching T vs C. It works for any structure and any member. The key insight: if the joints move closer together when the member is removed, it was in compression (it was pushing them apart). If they move apart, it was in tension (it was pulling them together).
- This lesson connects to the bridge challenge (Lessons 294–296), where learners must identify which members of their bridge are in T and C to predict the failure point.
