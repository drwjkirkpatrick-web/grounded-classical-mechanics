# Lesson 293: Balance Acts: Loads on Heads, Bodas and Carts

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.5, JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain why a load carried on the head is more stable than the same load carried in the hands — the CG sits directly above the support base (the feet).
2. Identify the correct loading position on a mkokoteni (handcart) — heavy goods over the axle, light goods at the ends — and justify it using axle moments.
3. Describe why a high load on a boda-boda carrier raises the centre of gravity and makes the bike unstable in a turn.
4. State three safe loading rules for head carrying, mkokoteni, and boda carriers.

## Materials
- A sufuria or a small bucket (to carry on the head — fill with books or sand)
- A sturdy stick or broom handle (to model a mkokoteni axle)
- Two bags of sand or books of different weights (a heavy one ~5 kg and a light one ~1 kg)
- A wooden plank or long ruler (to model the mkokoteni bed)
- A spring scale or hanging scale (if available — otherwise estimate by feel)
- A measuring tape or ruler
- A piece of string and a small weight (to make a plumb line)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **centre of gravity** (kituo cha uzito) — the single point where all the weight of an object (or a load-plus-carrier system) appears to act
- **base of support** (msingi wa tegemezi) — the area under an object (or person) that contacts the ground; wider = more stable
- **axle moment** (memento wa tasi) — the turning effect of a load about the wheel axle; a load behind the axle lifts the handle, a load in front pushes it down
- **stable loading** (upakiaji imara) — placing heavy goods low and centred over the axle so the CG stays within the base of support
- **overload** (mzigo kupita kiasi) — a load that exceeds the safe capacity of the carrier, raising the CG and stressing the structure

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Why is it easier to balance a bucket of water on your head than to carry the same bucket in one hand at arm's length?
2. A mkokoteni has its wheels at the centre. If you put all the heavy goods at the back (behind the axle), what happens to the handles when you lift?
3. A boda-boda rider stacks three sacks of maize high on the rear carrier. Why does the bike become hard to control in a corner?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. On the head, the bucket's weight acts straight down through the neck and spine — the CG is directly above the base of support (the feet). The carrier does not need to lean or apply a counter-torque. In the hand at arm's length, the weight creates a large moment about the shoulder (force × arm length), so the carrier must lean and strain to hold it.
2. The heavy goods behind the axle create a moment that lifts the handles. The pusher must press down hard to keep the handles on the ground, which is tiring and dangerous — the cart can tip backward.
3. The high sacks raise the combined CG of the bike, rider, and load. In a corner, the CG shifts outward (centripetal effect). If the CG is high, the tipping moment is large and the bike can roll over. A low load keeps the CG near the ground and the bike stable.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — invite a learner who can carry a sufuria on the head to demonstrate (or the teacher does it). Place the sufuria on the head — it balances. Then hold the same sufuria in one hand at arm's length — it is hard. Ask: *why is the same load easy on the head and hard in the hand?* Board: **CG over the base of support = stable; CG far from the support = a big moment you must fight**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs investigate loading on a model mkokoteni (see Hands-On Build). They balance a plank on a stick (the axle) and place heavy and light loads at different positions. They feel the handle force: heavy behind the axle lifts the handles; heavy in front pushes them down; heavy over the axle is balanced. They also test a plumb line from the CG of a loaded system and see whether it falls inside or outside the base of support. Results go on a class table: load position | handle feel (up, down, balanced) | plumb line inside base?
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Stability depends on whether the CG of the whole system (carrier + load) sits above the base of support. On the head: the load's CG is directly above the feet — stable, no counter-torque needed. In the hand at arm's length: the load's CG is far from the shoulder — a large moment (F × d) that the arm and back must resist. On a mkokoteni: the axle is the pivot. A load behind the axle creates a moment that lifts the handles (the cart tips backward). A load in front pushes the handles down. A load over the axle creates zero net moment — the handles are balanced. Rule: **heavy goods low and over the axle; light goods at the ends**. On a boda: a high load raises the CG. In a corner, the centripetal force acts at the CG, and the higher the CG, the larger the tipping moment (M = F × h, where h is the height of the CG). A low load keeps h small and the bike stable. Confront the misconception that "strong people can carry any load any way" — even a strong person wastes energy fighting moments that correct loading eliminates.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *put the CG over the support, and the load over the axle*. Homework: observe a mkokoteni at the market or a boda carrying goods. Sketch the load position and note whether the CG is over the axle (mkokoteni) or low (boda). Write one safety recommendation.

## Hands-On Build: Model Mkokoteni Loading Test
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- A wooden plank or long ruler (~60–80 cm, to model the mkokoteni bed)
- A sturdy stick or broom handle (to model the axle — placed under the plank at the midpoint)
- Two bags of sand or books: one heavy (~5 kg) and one light (~1 kg)
- A piece of string and a small weight (to make a plumb line)
- A measuring tape or ruler
- Two sturdy books or bricks (to prop up the plank ends if needed)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Place the stick (axle) on the table. Lay the plank across it at the midpoint, like a seesaw. **Checkpoint:** the plank balances on the axle — if it tips, slide it until it is level.
2. Place the heavy bag at the back end of the plank (behind the axle). Observe: the back end drops, the front (handle) end rises. Press down on the handle end — feel the upward force. **Checkpoint:** the learner can feel the handles lifting.
3. Move the heavy bag to the front end (in front of the axle). Observe: the front drops, the back rises. Lift the handle end — feel the downward force. **Checkpoint:** the handles are heavy and hard to lift.
4. Move the heavy bag to the centre, directly over the axle. Observe: the plank is balanced. The handles feel neutral — neither lifting nor pressing. **Checkpoint:** the load over the axle is the balanced position.
5. Tie the plumb line to the centre of the plank (the approximate CG of the system). Move the heavy load to different positions and observe where the plumb line falls. **Checkpoint:** when the plumb line falls outside the axle (the base of support), the plank tips; when it falls over the axle, the plank is stable.
6. Now stack the heavy bag on top of the light bag (high load) over the axle. Gently push the top of the stack sideways. Observe: the high stack tips easily. Then place both bags flat and low over the axle. Push again — much harder to tip. **Checkpoint:** low load = stable; high load = easy to tip.
7. Record all observations on the class table: load position | handle feel | plumb line position | stability.

### Common Mistakes
- **Axle not at the midpoint** — if the stick is off-centre, the plank has a built-in bias. Measure and centre the axle before testing.
- **Plank too short** — a short plank makes the moments small and hard to feel. Use at least 60 cm.
- **Learner pushes too hard when testing tipping** — a gentle sideways push is enough. A hard push tips any stack and teaches nothing.
- **Confusing weight with moment** — the same weight creates different handle forces depending on its distance from the axle. Emphasise: it is not just how heavy, but where.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Why is a bucket balanced on the head more stable than the same bucket held in the hand at arm's length? (mirrors pre-test)
2. On a mkokoteni, where should the heaviest goods go — over the axle, at the front, or at the back? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why does a high load on a boda-boda carrier make the bike unstable in a corner? (mirrors pre-test)
4. State one safe loading rule for head carrying and one for mkokoteni loading.
5. Challenge: A mkokoteni has 40 kg of maize and 10 kg of empty sacks. Where do you place each, and why?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. On the head, the load's CG is directly above the feet (the base of support), so no counter-torque is needed. In the hand at arm's length, the load's CG is far from the shoulder, creating a large moment (F × d) that the arm and back must resist — unstable and tiring.
2. Over the axle. A load over the axle creates zero net moment about the pivot, so the handles are balanced. A load behind the axle lifts the handles; a load in front pushes them down. Heavy over the axle = easiest and safest to push.
3. A high load raises the combined CG of the bike, rider, and load. In a corner, the centripetal force acts at the CG, and the tipping moment is M = F × h. A higher CG (larger h) means a larger tipping moment, so the bike rolls over more easily. A low load keeps h small and the bike stable.
4. Head carrying: keep the load centred on the head and close to the body — the CG stays over the feet. Mkokoteni: place heavy goods low and over the axle, light goods at the ends — the CG stays within the base of support and the handles are balanced.
5. The 40 kg of maize goes low and directly over the axle — it is the heaviest, so it must be at the pivot to keep the handles balanced. The 10 kg of empty sacks goes at the ends (front and back) — they are light, so their moment about the axle is small and they do not upset the balance. This keeps the combined CG over the axle and low to the ground.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A mkokoteni has an axle at the centre of a 1.5 m bed. The pusher holds the handles at the back end. A 60 kg sack is placed 0.4 m behind the axle. Calculate the upward force on the handles and the downward force on the front end. (The mkokoteni itself has a mass of 20 kg, CG at the axle.)
   **Worked Answer:** The sack's weight: W = 60 × 9.8 = 588 N, acting 0.4 m behind the axle. This creates a moment about the axle: M = 588 × 0.4 = 235.2 N·m (clockwise, lifting the front). The handle is at the back end, 0.75 m behind the axle (half of 1.5 m). To balance the moment, the handle force F_handle must create an equal and opposite moment: F_handle × 0.75 = 235.2 → F_handle = 314 N upward (the pusher must press down with 314 N to keep the handles down — or equivalently, the handles lift with 314 N). The front end (0.75 m in front of the axle) must support the remaining load: the total downward force is 588 N (sack) + 196 N (cart) = 784 N. The axle supports most of this. Taking moments about the axle: the front wheel force F_front × 0.75 = 235.2 (from the sack) → F_front = 314 N downward on the front end. The axle carries the rest: 784 − 314 = 470 N. So the handles feel ~314 N upward lift and the front end carries ~314 N. This is why the pusher struggles — the load is behind the axle.
2. **Q:** Design the loading plan for a mkokoteni carrying 100 kg of mixed goods: 50 kg of cement, 30 kg of water containers, and 20 kg of empty sacks. The bed is 1.5 m long, axle at the centre. Justify by CG and axle moments.
   **Worked Answer:** The 50 kg of cement is the heaviest — place it flat and directly over the axle (at position 0 m from the axle). Its moment about the axle is zero: M = 50 × 9.8 × 0 = 0. The 30 kg of water containers goes just behind the axle, say 0.2 m back: M = 30 × 9.8 × 0.2 = 58.8 N·m (clockwise, lifting the front). The 20 kg of empty sacks goes at the front, 0.5 m ahead of the axle: M = 20 × 9.8 × 0.5 = 98 N·m (counter-clockwise, pressing the front down). Net moment: 98 − 58.8 = 39.2 N·m counter-clockwise — the front is slightly heavy, which is safe (the pusher can feel the handles and adjust). The combined CG: taking moments about the axle, Σ(m × d) = 50 × 0 + 30 × (−0.2) + 20 × (+0.5) = 0 − 6 + 10 = +4 kg·m. Total mass = 100 kg. CG position = 4/100 = 0.04 m in front of the axle — almost centred. The CG is very close to the axle, so the handles are nearly balanced. If we want exactly zero moment, move the water containers to 0.33 m behind the axle: M = 30 × 9.8 × 0.33 = 97 N·m, which balances the 98 N·m from the sacks. But the slight front-heavy bias is safer — it keeps the front down and prevents backward tipping.
3. **Q:** A boda-boda carries a passenger and 25 kg of goods on the rear carrier. The goods are stacked 80 cm above the road. The combined mass of bike + rider + load is 200 kg, and the CG is at 55 cm height. In a turn at 8 m/s with a radius of 10 m, calculate the tipping moment and state whether the bike is stable. (The wheelbase is 1.3 m, track width 0.6 m.)
   **Worked Answer:** The centripetal force in the turn: F = mv²/r = 200 × 8²/10 = 200 × 64/10 = 1280 N. This force acts horizontally at the CG, which is at h = 0.55 m. The tipping moment: M_tip = F × h = 1280 × 0.55 = 704 N·m. The restoring moment comes from the weight acting at half the track width: M_restore = W × (track/2) = (200 × 9.8) × 0.3 = 1960 × 0.3 = 588 N·m. Since M_tip (704) > M_restore (588), the bike tips over. The high load (80 cm) raised the CG to 55 cm, making the tipping moment exceed the restoring moment. If the load were secured low (say CG drops to 40 cm): M_tip = 1280 × 0.4 = 512 N·m < 588 N·m — the bike would be stable. Conclusion: the bike is unstable with the high load; the rider must slow down or secure the load lower.

## Safety Notes
- Do not carry heavy loads on the head during the lesson — a small sufuria with a few books is enough to demonstrate.
- The model mkokoteni plank can tip and pinch fingers — keep hands clear of the ends when placing loads.
- Do not stand on the plank or stick — they are models, not real structures.
- If testing with a real mkokoteni outside the classroom, ensure the path is clear and the load is secured.
- Sand bags can spill — fill them over a tray or outside.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 292 (Centre of Gravity and Stability) introduced the CG concept and the stability rule (CG over the base of support). This lesson applies it to three Kenyan loading contexts: head carrying, mkokoteni, and boda-boda.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the feel of the handles (up, down, balanced) and the qualitative rule (heavy over the axle, load low). Ages 14–17 should compute moments about the axle and the tipping moment in a corner, as in the advanced questions.
- Local resource tips: the mkokoteni is ubiquitous in Kenyan markets and towns — every learner has seen one. Head carrying is common in rural areas and markets. Boda-boda carriers are seen daily. The plank-and-stick model is a cheap, effective stand-in for a real mkokoteni.
- The key insight: stability is about where the CG sits relative to the support. Head carrying works because the CG is over the feet. Mkokoteni loading works when the heavy goods are over the axle (zero moment). Boda stability depends on keeping the load low (small tipping moment). These are all the same physics — moments and CG — applied to different carriers.
- If a learner can demonstrate head carrying, let them — it is a powerful visual. But do not pressure anyone; the teacher can demonstrate with a small load.
- This lesson connects to the bridge challenge (Lessons 294–296): a bridge must also keep its load within its support base, and the failure modes (tipping, bending) are related to the same moment reasoning.
