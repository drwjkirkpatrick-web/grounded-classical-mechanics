# Lesson 294: Bridge Challenge I: Design Brief

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, G10-PHY-1.5  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** bridge

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Read a design brief: span 50 cm between two desks, hold 1 kg at midspan, using only sticks, straws, string, and glue.
2. Sketch a bridge design with labelled members, identifying which members are in tension (T) and which are in compression (C).
3. Triangulate the design — use triangles, not squares — and explain why triangles resist collapse while squares hinge.
4. Predict the member most likely to fail first and justify the prediction from the load path.

## Materials
- Popsicle sticks or collected dry sticks (about 20–30 per group)
- Drinking straws (a packet — cheap at any dukia)
- String or thin sisal twine (for tying joints)
- White glue (PVA) or wood glue — or flour-and-water paste as a substitute
- A ruler or tape measure — to measure the 50 cm span
- Two desks or tables (to serve as the supports — 50 cm apart)
- A 1 kg mass (a 1 kg packet of sugar, maize flour, or a bag of sand)
- Graph paper or squared exercise book pages (for sketches)
- Pencils and erasers
- Scissors — to cut sticks and straws
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **design brief** (maelezo ya muundo) — a written specification of what the structure must do: span, load, materials, constraints
- **span** (muda) — the distance between the two supports that the bridge must cross
- **midspan** (katikati ya muda) — the centre of the bridge, where the load is applied; the point of maximum bending
- **triangulation** (pembe tatu) — using triangles in a structure so that it cannot collapse without stretching or compressing a member
- **tension member** (kamba ya kuvuta) — a structural part pulled along its length; strings and thin sticks work well in tension
- **compression member** (kamba ya kubana) — a structural part pushed along its length; thick sticks work well in compression but slender ones buckle

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Why is a triangle a stronger shape than a square for a bridge frame?
2. A load sits at the centre of a bridge. Is the bottom chord (the bottom horizontal member) in tension or compression?
3. Name one material from the list (sticks, straws, string, glue) that is best used in tension, and one best used in compression.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A triangle cannot change shape without stretching or compressing one of its sides — it is rigid. A square can hinge at its corners and collapse into a parallelogram without any member changing length. Triangulation locks the shape.
2. The bottom chord is in tension — the load pushes down at midspan, the bottom of the bridge pulls apart (stretches) as the bridge bends. The top chord is in compression (pushed together).
3. String is best in tension (it pulls but cannot push). Thick sticks are best in compression (they push but buckle if too slender). Straws are weak in compression (they buckle easily) but can work in tension if kept straight.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — place a single stick across the 50 cm gap. Place the 1 kg mass on it. It bends and may break. Ask: *how can we make a bridge from the same sticks that holds this load?* Show a picture of a truss bridge (or sketch one on the board). Point out the triangles. Ask: *why triangles?* Board: **triangles are rigid — they cannot collapse without stretching or breaking a member**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs read the design brief and sketch their bridge (see Hands-On Build). Each group draws a side view of their bridge on graph paper, labels the span (50 cm), the midspan load point, and each member as T (tension) or C (compression). They identify the member they think will fail first and circle it. They list the materials they plan to use and estimate the number of sticks/straws. The teacher circulates and checks that every group has a triangulated design (no unreinforced squares). **Checkpoint:** every group has a labelled sketch with T/C annotations and a circled failure prediction.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The load at midspan pushes down. The top chord is compressed (pushed together); the bottom chord is tensioned (pulled apart). The diagonal members carry the shear — some in tension, some in compression depending on their direction. A Pratt truss has diagonals in tension and verticals in compression; a Howe truss is the reverse. The key rules: (1) triangulate — no unreinforced squares; (2) put thick sticks where compression is high (top chord, verticals); (3) put string or thin members where tension is high (bottom chord, tension diagonals); (4) joints must be strong — a bridge fails at a weak joint before it fails in a member. Confront the misconception that "more material = stronger" — a poorly triangulated bridge with 40 sticks is weaker than a well-triangulated one with 20. Efficiency (strength per unit weight) is the goal, not raw material.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *triangulate, label T/C, predict the failure*. Homework: refine the sketch if needed and bring it to the next lesson (Lesson 295: Build). Each group must arrive with a final labelled drawing.

## Hands-On Build: Bridge Design Sketch
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Graph paper or squared exercise book pages (2–3 sheets per group)
- Pencils and erasers
- A ruler or tape measure
- A reference sketch of a truss bridge (the teacher draws one on the board, or a printed image)
- The design brief (written on the board):
  - **Span:** 50 cm between two desks
  - **Load:** 1 kg at midspan
  - **Materials:** sticks, straws, string, glue only
  - **Constraint:** the bridge must not touch the desks except at the supports
  - **Goal:** maximise strength-to-weight ratio (tested in Lesson 296)

### Workflow
1. Read the design brief aloud as a class. Confirm the span (measure 50 cm between two desks) and the load (hold up the 1 kg mass). **Checkpoint:** every learner understands the four constraints (span, load, materials, support).
2. Sketch a side view of the bridge on graph paper. Start with two horizontal lines (top and bottom chords) spanning 50 cm. Add vertical and diagonal members to form triangles. **Checkpoint:** the design is fully triangulated — no unreinforced squares or rectangles.
3. Label each member: T for tension, C for compression. Rule: the bottom chord at midspan is T (pulled apart by the load); the top chord at midspan is C (pushed together); diagonals depend on their slope. **Checkpoint:** every member has a T or C label.
4. Circle the member you predict will fail first. Justify: it is either the most loaded compression member (buckling) or the weakest joint (pulling apart). **Checkpoint:** each group has a circled failure prediction with a reason.
5. Count the materials: how many sticks, straws, lengths of string, and how much glue? Write the list beside the sketch. **Checkpoint:** the materials list is realistic — the group can obtain the items.
6. Present the sketch to the teacher for approval. The teacher checks: (a) span is 50 cm, (b) design is triangulated, (c) T/C labels are correct, (d) failure prediction is reasonable. **Checkpoint:** the sketch is approved and ready for Lesson 295.

### Common Mistakes
- **Unreinforced squares** — a square frame collapses into a parallelogram. Every square must have a diagonal to triangulate it. If the sketch has a square with no diagonal, add one.
- **Bottom chord labelled C** — the bottom chord at midspan is in tension (the load pulls it apart). If a group labels it C, correct them: the top is C, the bottom is T.
- **Too many members** — a bridge with 50 sticks is heavy and inefficient. Aim for a clean truss with 15–25 members. More material does not mean stronger — it means heavier.
- **No failure prediction** — a design without a predicted failure point shows the group has not thought about the load path. Every group must circle a member and justify it.
- **Ignoring joint strength** — the joints (where sticks meet) are usually the weakest point. The sketch should show how joints will be made (glued, tied, or lashed with string).

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Why is a triangle stronger than a square in a bridge frame? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A load sits at midspan. Is the top chord in tension or compression? Is the bottom chord in tension or compression? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name one material from the brief that is best in tension and one best in compression. (mirrors pre-test)
4. Sketch a simple triangulated truss (at least 3 triangles) and label two members as T or C.
5. Challenge: you have 20 sticks. Is it better to build a solid beam (stacked sticks) or a triangulated truss? Explain using strength-to-weight reasoning.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A triangle is rigid — it cannot change shape without stretching or compressing a side. A square can hinge at its corners and collapse into a parallelogram. Triangulation locks the shape so the structure holds its form under load.
2. Top chord: compression (the load pushes down, compressing the top). Bottom chord: tension (the load pulls the bottom apart as the bridge bends).
3. String is best in tension (it pulls but cannot push). Thick sticks are best in compression (they push but buckle if too slender). Straws buckle easily in compression and are better used in tension or as lightweight filler.
4. (Learner's sketch — check that the truss has at least 3 triangles and that the T/C labels are correct: bottom chord = T, top chord = C, diagonals depend on slope. A correct example: a Warren truss with alternating diagonals — diagonals sloping down toward midspan are in tension; those sloping up are in compression.)
5. The triangulated truss is better. A solid beam of 20 sticks is heavy — all the material in the middle does little work (the neutral axis). A truss of 20 sticks arranged in triangles puts every member in either tension or compression, with no wasted material. The truss is lighter (less material in the middle) and stronger (triangulation prevents collapse). Strength-to-weight ratio = load held ÷ bridge weight. The truss holds more and weighs less, so its ratio is much higher. This is why real bridges are trusses, not solid beams.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Draw a Pratt truss (diagonals slope down toward midspan from the top) and a Howe truss (diagonals slope up toward midspan from the bottom). For a load at midspan, identify which diagonals are in tension and which are in compression in each truss. Which design is better if your compression members are weak (thin sticks)?
   **Worked Answer:** In a Pratt truss, the diagonals slope down toward midspan from the top chord. Under a midspan load, the shear is carried by the diagonals in tension and the verticals in compression. So the diagonals are T and the verticals are C. In a Howe truss, the diagonals slope up toward midspan from the bottom chord. Under the same load, the diagonals are in compression and the verticals are in tension. So the diagonals are C and the verticals are T. If the compression members are weak (thin sticks that buckle), the Pratt truss is better — it puts the diagonals (the longer members) in tension, where thin sticks and even string can work, and reserves compression for the short verticals, which are less likely to buckle. The Howe truss puts the long diagonals in compression, where thin sticks buckle easily. So with weak compression members, choose Pratt.
2. **Q:** Predict the failing member and its T/C nature for your bridge design. Justify from the load path.
   **Worked Answer:** (Learner's answer depends on their design — check the reasoning.) A correct answer follows this pattern: "The load at midspan pushes down. The load travels down the vertical member nearest midspan (compression), then splits into the two bottom chords (tension) and the two diagonals (tension in a Pratt, compression in a Howe). The top chord is in compression throughout. The member most likely to fail is [the top chord near midspan / the longest compression diagonal / the joint at the bottom chord midspan] because [it carries the highest compressive force and is most likely to buckle / the joint is glued and glue is weaker in tension than wood is in compression]. The failure mode is [buckling for a compression member / tearing for a tension joint]." The key is that the prediction is specific, the T/C label is correct, and the justification traces the load path from midspan to the supports.
3. **Q:** Estimate your bridge's strength-to-weight ratio. If the bridge weighs 50 g and holds 1 kg, what is the ratio? If you could halve the weight while keeping the same strength, how much does the ratio improve?
   **Worked Answer:** Strength-to-weight ratio = max load ÷ bridge weight. If the bridge weighs 50 g = 0.05 kg and holds 1 kg, the ratio = 1/0.05 = 20:1. If you halve the weight to 25 g = 0.025 kg while keeping the same 1 kg strength, the ratio = 1/0.025 = 40:1 — double. This is why efficiency matters: a lighter bridge that holds the same load has a much better ratio. In the competition (Lesson 296), the champion is the bridge with the highest ratio, not the one that holds the most absolute load. A 30 g bridge that holds 0.8 kg (ratio 27:1) beats a 100 g bridge that holds 2 kg (ratio 20:1). The lesson: use the minimum material needed, placed where it does the most work (far from the neutral axis, in triangulated members).

## Safety Notes
- Do not test the bridge in this lesson — the test is in Lesson 296. Sketching only.
- Scissors should be used carefully — younger learners may need help cutting sticks.
- White glue is non-toxic but should not be eaten or put near the eyes. Wash hands after use.
- If using collected sticks from outside, check for splinters and insects.
- Do not lean on the desks used as supports — they can tip if pushed.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 287–289 (Strong Shapes: folds, arches, triangles) and Lesson 291 (Tension vs Compression) provide the structural concepts. This lesson applies them to a design challenge.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on sketching a triangulated bridge and labelling T/C with the teacher's help. Ages 14–17 should compare truss types (Pratt vs Howe), predict the failing member from the load path, and estimate the strength-to-weight ratio.
- Local resource tips: popsicle sticks are available at shops, but collected dry sticks work just as well and are free. Straws are cheap at any dukia. String or sisal twine is widely available. White glue (PVA) is common; flour-and-water paste is a free substitute but dries slower. Graph paper may be scarce — squared exercise book pages work.
- This is the first of three bridge lessons (294 Design, 295 Build, 296 Test). The design sketch is the deliverable for this lesson. Every group must arrive at Lesson 295 with an approved, labelled sketch.
- The design brief should be written on the board before the lesson starts. Read it aloud as a class and confirm every group understands the four constraints.
- The key insight: a good bridge is designed before it is built. The sketch forces learners to think about the load path, T/C members, and failure points before they touch the materials. Groups that skip the sketch and build by trial and error usually build heavy, weak bridges.
- The strength-to-weight ratio is the competition metric in Lesson 296. Introduce it now so groups design for efficiency, not just strength.
