# Lesson 295: Bridge Challenge II: Build

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** bridge

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Construct a bridge from the approved design sketch (Lesson 294) using sticks, straws, string, and glue.
2. Log every design deviation from the original sketch with a reason — and justify each change using physics (buckling, tension, joint strength).
3. Weigh the materials used and record the total bridge mass for the strength-to-weight competition in Lesson 296.
4. Identify and reinforce the weakest joint before testing.

## Materials
- The approved design sketch from Lesson 294 (each group brings theirs)
- Popsicle sticks or collected dry sticks (as estimated in the sketch)
- Drinking straws (as needed)
- String or thin sisal twine (for lashing joints)
- White glue (PVA) or wood glue — or flour-and-water paste
- A ruler or tape measure — to verify the 50 cm span
- Two desks or tables (to check the fit — 50 cm apart)
- A kitchen scale or hanging scale (to weigh the finished bridge)
- Scissors — to cut sticks and straws
- Small clamps, clothes pegs, or rubber bands (to hold glued joints while they dry)
- A build log sheet (a blank page in the notebook, headed: "Build Log — Bridge Challenge")
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **build log** (kumbukumbu ya ujenzi) — a written record of every change made during construction, with the reason for each change
- **design deviation** (mabadiliko ya muundo) — any difference between the approved sketch and the built bridge; must be logged with a physics justification
- **joint** (kiungo) — the point where two or more members meet; usually the weakest part of a stick bridge
- **buckling** (kunyofya) — when a compression member bends sideways under load before it breaks; prevented by making members shorter or thicker
- **efficiency** (ufaifu) — strength per unit weight; a light bridge that holds a lot is efficient

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Why is a glued joint usually the first thing to fail in a stick bridge?
2. If a compression member is buckling, what two things can you do to fix it?
3. Your bridge weighs 60 g. Why does a lighter bridge (40 g) that holds the same load score better in the competition?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Glue is weaker than wood in tension — the glue bond area is small, and the glue itself has a lower shear strength than the stick. When the load pulls on a joint, the glue peels or shears before the stick breaks. Lashing with string adds strength.
2. Make it shorter (buckling force is proportional to 1/length² — halving the length quadruples the buckling resistance) or make it thicker (buckling force is proportional to width³ — doubling the thickness gives 8× the resistance). Adding a lateral brace also prevents sideways buckling.
3. The competition metric is strength-to-weight ratio = max load ÷ bridge weight. A 40 g bridge holding 1 kg has a ratio of 1000/40 = 25:1. A 60 g bridge holding the same 1 kg has a ratio of 1000/60 = 16.7:1. The lighter bridge wins because it uses less material to achieve the same strength — it is more efficient.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — each group places their approved sketch (from Lesson 294) on the desk. The teacher checks that every group has a sketch. Ask: *who thinks their bridge will hold the 1 kg load?* Take a show of hands. Then ask: *what is the first thing that will break?* Board: **joints fail first — reinforce them**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups build their bridges from the sketch (see Hands-On Build). They cut sticks to length, glue and lash joints, and assemble the truss. Every time they deviate from the sketch (add a member, remove a member, change a length, reinforce a joint), they write an entry in the build log: what changed, why, and the physics reason. The teacher circulates and checks that: (a) the span is 50 cm, (b) the design is triangulated, (c) joints are glued AND lashed, (d) the build log is being kept. **Checkpoint:** the bridge matches the sketch (or deviations are logged) and every joint is reinforced.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The build phase reveals problems the sketch did not anticipate: a stick is too long and buckles, a joint is weak because the glue surface is small, a diagonal is in the wrong direction. Each deviation is a physics lesson. The build log captures the reasoning. Key rules: (1) joints are the weakest point — always lash with string in addition to glue; (2) compression members should be short and thick to avoid buckling; (3) tension members can be thin (even string) because they cannot buckle; (4) weigh the bridge as soon as it is done — the mass goes on the class table for the competition. Confront the misconception that "deviations are failures" — a logged deviation with a good physics reason is engineering, not a mistake. Real engineers change designs during construction all the time; what matters is that the reason is recorded and justified.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *build to the sketch, log every change, weigh the bridge*. Homework: write one paragraph summarising the most important deviation your group made and the physics behind it. Bring the finished bridge to Lesson 296 (Test and Autopsy).

## Hands-On Build: Construct the Bridge
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- The approved design sketch from Lesson 294
- Popsicle sticks or collected dry sticks (as estimated)
- Drinking straws (as needed)
- String or thin sisal twine (for lashing joints)
- White glue (PVA) or wood glue (or flour-and-water paste)
- A ruler or tape measure
- Two desks or tables (50 cm apart — to check the fit)
- A kitchen scale or hanging scale (to weigh the finished bridge)
- Scissors
- Small clamps, clothes pegs, or rubber bands (to hold glued joints while they dry)
- A build log sheet (notebook page headed "Build Log — Bridge Challenge")
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Lay out the materials and the sketch. Confirm the span: measure 50 cm between the two desks. **Checkpoint:** the span is exactly 50 cm and the desks are stable.
2. Cut the sticks to the lengths shown on the sketch. Label each stick with its role (top chord, bottom chord, diagonal, vertical) using a pencil mark. **Checkpoint:** every stick is cut to the right length and labelled.
3. Build one truss (one side of the bridge) flat on the desk. Glue each joint, then lash it with string wrapped tightly around the connection. Use a clothes peg or rubber band to hold the joint while the glue sets. **Checkpoint:** every joint is both glued and lashed — no joint relies on glue alone.
4. Check the truss against the sketch. If a member is too long, too short, or in the wrong place, adjust it and write a build log entry: what changed, why, and the physics reason. **Checkpoint:** the first truss matches the sketch or deviations are logged.
5. Build the second truss (the other side), identical to the first. If the second truss differs from the first, log the change. **Checkpoint:** both trusses are identical in shape and size.
6. Connect the two trusses with cross-members (sticks across the top and bottom) to form a 3D bridge. The cross-members prevent the trusses from twisting sideways. **Checkpoint:** the bridge is rigid in 3D — it does not twist when picked up.
7. Place the bridge on the two desks, 50 cm apart. Check the fit: it should rest on both supports without falling through. **Checkpoint:** the bridge spans the gap and sits level on the supports.
8. Weigh the finished bridge on the kitchen scale. Record the mass on the class table: group | bridge mass (g) | predicted failure member. **Checkpoint:** the mass is recorded for the strength-to-weight competition.
9. Identify the weakest joint (the one most likely to fail under load). Reinforce it with an extra lashing or a small glue fillet. Log the reinforcement. **Checkpoint:** the weakest joint is reinforced and logged.

### Common Mistakes
- **Glue-only joints** — glue alone is weak in tension and shear. Always lash joints with string. The string holds the joint even if the glue cracks.
- **Compression members too long** — a long thin stick in compression buckles sideways. If a compression member is longer than ~8 cm, add a lateral brace or use a doubled (two sticks glued side by side) member.
- **No cross-members** — two trusses without cross-members twist and collapse sideways. Always connect the trusses with top and bottom cross-members.
- **Not keeping the build log** — every deviation must be logged. A bridge with no log suggests the group built by trial and error, not by design. The log is part of the deliverable.
- **Building without checking the span** — a bridge that is 45 cm or 55 cm does not fit the 50 cm gap. Measure the span before cutting and again before finishing.
- **Rushing the glue** — PVA glue takes 15–20 minutes to set. Use clothes pegs or rubber bands to hold joints and let them dry while building other parts. Do not load or move the bridge until the glue has set.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Why is a glued joint usually the first thing to fail in a stick bridge? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If a compression member is buckling, what two things can you do to fix it? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Your bridge weighs 60 g and holds 1 kg. A rival bridge weighs 40 g and holds the same 1 kg. Which wins the competition and why? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Name one deviation your group made from the original sketch and the physics reason for it.
5. Challenge: your bridge has a long diagonal in compression that buckles during a dry test. You cannot shorten it (the span is fixed). What do you do?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Glue is weaker than wood in tension and shear. The glue bond area is small, and the glue's shear strength is lower than the stick's compressive strength. When the load pulls on a joint, the glue peels or shears before the stick breaks. Lashing with string adds a mechanical bond that holds even if the glue fails.
2. (a) Make it shorter — buckling force is proportional to 1/length², so halving the length quadruples the buckling resistance. (b) Make it thicker — buckling force is proportional to the width cubed, so doubling the thickness gives 8× the resistance. Alternatively, add a lateral brace to prevent sideways buckling.
3. The rival bridge wins. Strength-to-weight ratio = max load ÷ bridge weight. Your bridge: 1000/60 = 16.7:1. Rival: 1000/40 = 25:1. The rival uses less material for the same strength — it is more efficient.
4. (Learner's own answer — check that the deviation is described and the physics reason is correct. Example: "We added a second diagonal to the second panel because the first diagonal buckled when we pressed it. The second diagonal shares the compressive load, halving the force on each and preventing buckling. Logged as: added diagonal, reason: buckling prevention, physics: load sharing.")
5. Double the member (glue two sticks side by side) — this increases the thickness, and since buckling force scales with width³, doubling the thickness gives ~8× the buckling resistance without changing the length. Alternatively, add a lateral brace (a small stick glued perpendicular to the diagonal at its midpoint) to prevent sideways buckling. Both methods increase the buckling resistance without shortening the member. Log the change.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Keep a build log entry for every design deviation your group made. For each entry, state: (a) what changed, (b) why, (c) the physics justification. Write at least three entries.
   **Worked Answer:** (Learner's own entries — check for physics reasoning. Example entries:)
   - Entry 1: "Changed the top chord from a single stick to a doubled stick (two glued side by side). Why: the single stick buckled when we pressed it in a dry test. Physics: the top chord is in compression. Buckling force F_cr ∝ EI/L², where E is the stiffness, I is the second moment of area, and L is the length. Doubling the thickness quadruples I (I ∝ width³ for a rectangular section), so the buckling resistance increases by ~4×. The doubled chord now resists buckling."
   - Entry 2: "Added a string tie across the bottom chord at midspan. Why: the bottom chord joints were pulling apart when we tensioned the bridge. Physics: the bottom chord is in tension. The glue joints are weak in tension (peel mode). The string tie acts as a tension member that holds the two halves of the bottom chord together, bypassing the glue. The string is strong in tension and cannot buckle, so it is ideal for this role."
   - Entry 3: "Removed two redundant vertical members near the ends. Why: the load path does not pass through them — the shear at the ends is low, and the diagonals carry it. Physics: the members near the supports carry little load (the bending moment is zero at the supports). Removing them saves weight without reducing strength, improving the strength-to-weight ratio. This is why real truss bridges have fewer members near the supports."
2. **Q:** Your bridge has a joint where three sticks meet at an angle. The glue keeps failing. Describe two ways to strengthen the joint without adding new members.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Lash the joint with string: wrap string tightly around all three sticks at the junction, tying them together mechanically. The string holds the sticks in compression against each other, so even if the glue peels, the string keeps the joint together. This is the most effective fix. (b) Add a glue fillet: build up a thick bead of glue in the angle between the sticks, filling the gap. A fillet increases the glue surface area and changes the failure mode from peel (weak) to shear (stronger). The fillet should be at least as thick as the stick. For maximum strength, do both: lash first, then add the fillet. The string provides the mechanical bond; the fillet provides the adhesive bond. Together they are much stronger than either alone.
3. **Q:** Your finished bridge weighs 45 g. You estimate it will hold 1.2 kg before failing. Calculate the strength-to-weight ratio. Then state one change that would improve the ratio without reducing the strength.
   **Worked Answer:** Ratio = max load ÷ bridge weight = 1200 g ÷ 45 g = 26.7:1. To improve the ratio without reducing strength: remove redundant members near the supports (where the bending moment is near zero, members carry little load). If removing two sticks saves 4 g (new weight 41 g) and the bridge still holds 1.2 kg, the new ratio = 1200/41 = 29.3:1 — a 10% improvement. Another option: replace a heavy compression member with a lighter one (a single stick instead of a doubled stick) if the single stick does not buckle. If the single stick holds, the weight drops by ~3 g (new weight 42 g), ratio = 1200/42 = 28.6:1. The key: every gram saved without losing strength improves the ratio. This is why efficient design matters more than raw strength in the competition.

## Safety Notes
- Scissors and craft knives (if used) can cut fingers — the teacher may pre-cut sticks for younger learners.
- White glue is non-toxic but should not be eaten. Wash hands after building.
- Do not test the bridge in this lesson — testing is in Lesson 296. A premature test can break the bridge before the official competition.
- Collected sticks from outside may have splinters — sand rough edges or wear gloves.
- Do not lean on the desks used as supports — they can tip.
- Glue on clothing is hard to remove — wear an apron or old clothes.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 294 (Bridge Challenge I: Design Brief) — every group must arrive with an approved, labelled sketch. Groups without a sketch should be given 5 minutes to produce one before building.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the bridge to the sketch and lashing joints. Ages 14–17 should keep a detailed build log with physics justifications for every deviation, and weigh the bridge for the competition.
- Local resource tips: popsicle sticks are available at shops, but collected dry sticks (allsorted by thickness) work well and are free. Straws are cheap. String or sisal twine is widely available. White glue (PVA) is common; flour-and-water paste is a free substitute but dries slower and is weaker — use it only if PVA is unavailable. Clothes pegs or rubber bands hold glued joints while drying.
- This is the second of three bridge lessons (294 Design, 295 Build, 296 Test). The deliverable is a finished, weighed bridge and a build log. Both are brought to Lesson 296 for testing.
- The build log is a critical deliverable. It teaches learners that engineering is iterative — designs change during construction, and the reasoning must be recorded. A bridge with no log is incomplete.
- The key insight: joints fail first. A bridge with strong joints and a mediocre truss will outperform a bridge with a brilliant truss and weak joints. Lashing with string is the single most effective reinforcement.
- If time is short, groups can build one truss in class and the second at home (or in a second session). But both trusses must be identical — measure carefully.
- The strength-to-weight ratio is the competition metric. Weigh every bridge and record the mass on the class table. In Lesson 296, the max load will be divided by this mass to find the champion.
