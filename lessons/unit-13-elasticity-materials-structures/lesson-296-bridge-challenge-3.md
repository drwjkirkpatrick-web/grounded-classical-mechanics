# Lesson 296: Bridge Challenge III: Test and Autopsy

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** bridge

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Load a bridge to destruction using a sand bucket and record the maximum load and the failure mode.
2. Classify the failure mode as buckling (compression), tearing (tension), or joint failure (glue/lash) and relate it to the predicted failure member from Lesson 294.
3. Compute the strength-to-weight ratio for each bridge and crown the champion.
4. Write one design rule derived from the class's failure data — a general principle for building stronger, lighter bridges.

## Materials
- The finished bridges from Lesson 295 (each group brings theirs)
- The build logs from Lesson 295
- A sturdy bucket or debe (to hang from the bridge at midspan)
- Dry sand (to add as load in measured cups — one 500 ml cup ≈ 0.8 kg)
- A measuring cup or empty 500 ml water bottle (to measure sand)
- A kitchen scale or hanging scale (to weigh the bridge and the sand bucket)
- Two desks or tables (50 cm apart — the supports)
- A piece of string or thin rope (to hang the bucket from the bridge midspan)
- A tray or newspaper (to catch spilled sand when the bridge fails)
- The class data table from Lesson 295 (group | bridge mass | predicted failure member)
- A large sheet of paper or the board (for the class results table)
- Safety goggles or sunglasses (to protect eyes from snapping sticks)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **failure mode** (njia ya kuvunjika) — how the bridge breaks: buckling (compression member bends sideways), tearing (tension member pulls apart), or joint failure (glue or lashing gives way)
- **destruction test** (jaribio la kuharibu) — loading a structure until it fails to find its maximum capacity
- **strength-to-weight ratio** (uwiano wa nguvu kwa uzito) — max load divided by bridge weight; the competition metric
- **autopsy** (uchunguzi wa maiti) — examining the broken bridge to determine which member failed first and why
- **design rule** (kanuni ya muundo) — a general principle derived from testing, e.g., "lash every joint" or "keep compression members short"

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A bridge fails when a top chord member bends sideways and collapses. What is this failure mode called?
2. Your bridge weighs 50 g and held 1.5 kg of sand. What is its strength-to-weight ratio?
3. Why is it useful to predict the failure member before testing (as you did in Lesson 294)?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Buckling — a compression member bends sideways under load before it breaks. The top chord is in compression, so it buckles when the compressive force exceeds its critical buckling load.
2. Ratio = max load ÷ bridge weight = 1500 g ÷ 50 g = 30:1. The bridge holds 30 times its own weight.
3. Predicting the failure member forces you to trace the load path before testing. If the prediction is correct, you understand the structure. If it is wrong, you learn where your analysis was off — and that is the most valuable lesson, because it reveals a misconception about how loads travel through the truss.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — place a bridge on the two desks, 50 cm apart. Hang the empty bucket from the midspan with string. Ask: *how much sand will this bridge hold before it breaks?* Take guesses. Then ask: *which member will fail first?* Remind the class of their predictions from Lesson 294. Board: **Test to destruction — record the max load and the failure mode**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups test their bridges one at a time (see Hands-On Build). The bucket is hung from midspan, sand is added cup by cup, and the load is recorded. When the bridge fails, the test stops and the failure mode is examined: which member broke, was it buckling (compression), tearing (tension), or joint failure (glue/lash)? The result is compared to the predicted failure member from Lesson 294. Results go on the class table: group | bridge mass (g) | max load (g) | ratio | predicted failure | actual failure | match? **Checkpoint:** every bridge is tested, the max load and failure mode are recorded, and the prediction is compared to the actual failure.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The class table reveals patterns. Typical results: (a) bridges with lashed joints fail in a member (buckling or tearing), not at the joint — lashing works; (b) bridges with glue-only joints fail at the joint — the glue peels; (c) long compression members buckle; (d) triangulated bridges hold more than untriangulated ones; (e) lighter bridges with good geometry have better ratios. Crown the strength-to-weight champion. Confront the misconception that "the strongest bridge is the one that holds the most" — the champion is the one with the best ratio, not the most absolute load. A 30 g bridge that holds 0.9 kg (ratio 30:1) beats a 100 g bridge that holds 2 kg (ratio 20:1). Also address: "if the prediction was wrong, the test was a failure" — no, a wrong prediction is the most valuable result because it reveals a gap in the load-path analysis. The autopsy is where the real learning happens.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *test to destruction, classify the failure, compute the ratio*. Homework: write one design rule derived from the class data (e.g., "lash every joint because glue-only joints failed in 4 of 6 bridges"). Bring the rule to the next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Bridge Destruction Test and Autopsy
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- The finished bridges from Lesson 295
- A sturdy bucket or debe (to hang from midspan)
- Dry sand (to add as load)
- A measuring cup or empty 500 ml water bottle (one cup ≈ 0.8 kg)
- A piece of string or thin rope (to hang the bucket)
- Two desks or tables (50 cm apart)
- A tray or newspaper (to catch spilled sand)
- A kitchen scale or hanging scale (to weigh sand if measuring by cup is not precise enough)
- Safety goggles or sunglasses
- The class results table (on the board or a large sheet)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Set up the two desks 50 cm apart. Place a tray or newspaper under the gap to catch sand. **Checkpoint:** the span is 50 cm and the catch tray is in place.
2. Weigh the bridge and record the mass on the class table. **Checkpoint:** the bridge mass is recorded.
3. Place the bridge on the two desks. Tie the string around the midspan of the bridge and hang the empty bucket from it. **Checkpoint:** the bucket hangs freely from midspan and does not touch the ground.
4. Weigh the empty bucket and record it (or note its mass — typically 0.3–0.5 kg for a debe). **Checkpoint:** the bucket mass is known.
5. Add sand one cup at a time. After each cup, pause for 3 seconds to let the bridge settle. Count the cups. **Checkpoint:** the load is increased gradually — do not dump sand all at once.
6. Continue adding sand until the bridge fails (a member buckles, tears, or a joint gives way). Stop immediately. **Checkpoint:** the failure is observed and the load at failure is recorded.
7. Record the max load: (number of cups × 0.8 kg) + bucket mass. Write it on the class table. **Checkpoint:** the max load is calculated and recorded.
8. Examine the broken bridge (autopsy). Identify the first member that failed: was it a compression member that buckled (bent sideways), a tension member that tore (pulled apart), or a joint that gave way (glue peeled or lashing slipped)? **Checkpoint:** the failure mode is classified and recorded.
9. Compare the actual failure to the predicted failure member from Lesson 294. Was the prediction correct? If not, what failed instead and why? **Checkpoint:** the prediction is compared to the actual failure and the result is recorded.
10. Compute the strength-to-weight ratio: max load (g) ÷ bridge mass (g). Write it on the class table. **Checkpoint:** the ratio is computed for every bridge.
11. Repeat for each group. At the end, identify the champion (highest ratio) and the bridge that held the most absolute load. Note if they are different. **Checkpoint:** the champion is crowned and the results are complete.

### Common Mistakes
- **Dumping sand too fast** — a sudden load shocks the bridge and can cause a different failure mode than a gradual load. Add sand one cup at a time and pause.
- **Not catching the sand** — when the bridge fails, the bucket falls and sand spills everywhere. Put a tray or newspaper under the gap before testing.
- **Not recording the bucket mass** — the bucket itself is part of the load. If you forget it, the max load is underestimated by 0.3–0.5 kg.
- **Confusing absolute load with ratio** — the champion is the bridge with the best strength-to-weight ratio, not the one that holds the most sand. A light bridge with a good ratio is the better design.
- **Not doing the autopsy** — just recording the max load misses the point. The failure mode (buckling, tearing, joint) tells you what to fix next time. Always examine the broken bridge.
- **Testing on a wet or sticky desk** — the bridge must rest freely on the supports. If it sticks, the test is not fair. Wipe the desk edges dry.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A bridge fails when a top chord member bends sideways and collapses. What is this failure mode? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Your bridge weighs 50 g and held 1.5 kg. What is its strength-to-weight ratio? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why is it useful to predict the failure member before testing? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your bridge was predicted to fail in the bottom chord (tension) but actually failed at a glued joint. What does this tell you, and what would you change?
5. Challenge: write one design rule derived from the class data that would make a bridge stronger or lighter.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Buckling — a compression member bends sideways under load. The top chord is in compression, so it buckles when the force exceeds its critical buckling load.
2. Ratio = 1500 g ÷ 50 g = 30:1. The bridge holds 30 times its own weight.
3. Predicting the failure member forces you to trace the load path before testing. If the prediction is correct, your analysis is sound. If it is wrong, you learn where your load-path reasoning was off — and that reveals a misconception, which is the most valuable lesson from the test.
4. The joint was weaker than the bottom chord. The prediction assumed the chord would tear before the joint failed, but the glue bond was the weak point. The fix: lash the joint with string (mechanical bond) in addition to glue (chemical bond). The lashing holds even if the glue peels. Next time, design the joints to be stronger than the members — a joint should never be the weak link.
5. (Learner's own rule — check that it is derived from the class data and is general. Examples: "Lash every joint — in our class, 4 of 6 bridges failed at glued joints, and the 2 that failed in a member had lashed joints." "Keep compression members under 8 cm — the two bridges with long compression diagonals buckled; the ones with short or braced diagonals held more." "Use string for tension members — string is lighter than sticks and cannot buckle, so it saves weight and improves the ratio." The rule should be specific, data-driven, and generalisable.)

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Compute each bridge's strength-to-weight ratio from the class data. Identify the champion. Then analyse the champion's geometry: what made it the most efficient?
   **Worked Answer:** (Learner's answer depends on class data — check the calculation and analysis.) Example: if the champion weighed 35 g and held 1.4 kg, the ratio = 1400/35 = 40:1. The analysis: the champion used a Pratt truss (diagonals in tension, verticals in compression). The tension diagonals were string (light, no buckling risk). The compression verticals were short (under 6 cm, so no buckling). The top chord was doubled (two sticks glued side by side) to resist compression. The bottom chord was a single stick (tension — no buckling risk, so one stick was enough). Every joint was lashed with string. The bridge had no redundant members — the two sticks near the supports were removed (low bending moment there). The result: a light bridge (35 g) with no wasted material, every member in the right role, and joints stronger than the members. The geometry put the right material in the right place — string in tension, thick sticks in compression, short compression members, and lashed joints.
2. **Q:** Two bridges held the same load (1.2 kg). Bridge A weighed 40 g; Bridge B weighed 80 g. Compute both ratios. Then explain why Bridge B is heavier — what redundant or misplaced material might it have?
   **Worked Answer:** Bridge A: ratio = 1200/40 = 30:1. Bridge B: ratio = 1200/80 = 15:1. Bridge A is twice as efficient. Bridge B is heavier because it likely has: (a) redundant members near the supports, where the bending moment is near zero — these carry little load but add weight; (b) doubled sticks where a single stick would suffice (e.g., a doubled bottom chord, when the bottom chord is in tension and a single stick is strong enough); (c) thick sticks used for tension members, where string would be lighter and equally strong; (d) excess glue (heavy globs at joints instead of thin fillets); (e) cross-members that are too thick or too many — the bridge only needs enough to prevent twisting, not a solid deck. Bridge B's designer used more material than necessary because they did not analyse the load path — they added material "just in case." Bridge A's designer put every member where it does work and used the minimum material. The lesson: efficiency comes from analysis, not from adding material.
3. **Q:** Write the design rule that explains the champion's success. State it as a general principle that applies to any bridge, not just this one.
   **Worked Answer:** The design rule: "Put the right material in the right place — string for tension, thick sticks for compression, short compression members, and lashed joints. Remove any member that carries little load." This is general because it follows from the physics: (1) tension members cannot buckle, so they can be thin (string is ideal — light and strong in tension); (2) compression members buckle, so they must be thick and short (the buckling force scales with width³ and 1/length² — double the width for 8× the resistance, halve the length for 4×); (3) joints are the weakest point, so lash them with string (mechanical bond) in addition to glue (chemical bond); (4) members near the supports carry little load (the bending moment is zero at the supports), so removing them saves weight without reducing strength. This rule applies to any truss bridge, from a stick model to a steel railway bridge: use the minimum material, placed where the physics demands it, with joints stronger than the members. The champion followed this rule; the others did not.

## Safety Notes
- When the bridge fails, the bucket of sand falls. Keep feet and hands clear of the area under the bridge.
- Wear safety goggles or sunglasses — snapping sticks can send splinters flying.
- Do not stand on the desks or lean over the bridge during testing.
- Sand is heavy — a full bucket can weigh 2–3 kg. Do not try to catch it when it falls; let it drop onto the tray.
- Clean up spilled sand promptly — it is a slipping hazard.
- Do not test bridges near the edge of the desk — the falling bucket can pull the bridge off the support and damage the desk edge.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 294 (Design Brief) and 295 (Build) — every group must arrive with a finished, weighed bridge and a build log. Groups without a bridge should observe and record data from other groups' tests.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on loading the bridge, observing the failure, and classifying it as "bent" (buckling), "pulled apart" (tearing), or "joint broke." Ages 14–17 should compute the ratio, compare predicted vs actual failure, and derive a design rule from the class data.
- Local resource tips: a debe or small bucket is available in any home. Dry sand is free (from a riverbed or building site). A 500 ml water bottle serves as a measuring cup. A kitchen scale is ideal but not essential — counting cups of sand (each ≈ 0.8 kg) is accurate enough for the competition.
- This is the third and final bridge lesson (294 Design, 295 Build, 296 Test). The deliverables are: (a) the class results table (group | mass | max load | ratio | predicted failure | actual failure), (b) the crowned champion, and (c) one design rule per learner derived from the data.
- The destruction test is the highlight of the unit. The moment of failure is dramatic and memorable — learners see the physics in action. The autopsy (examining the broken bridge) is where the real learning happens: comparing the prediction to the actual failure reveals whether the load-path analysis was correct.
- The key insight: the strength-to-weight ratio is the true measure of structural efficiency. The champion is not the bridge that holds the most sand — it is the bridge that holds the most sand per gram of its own weight. This is why real engineers care about efficiency, not just strength: a lighter bridge (or building, or aircraft) costs less, uses less material, and is more sustainable.
- If a bridge holds more than 3 kg of sand, stop the test and declare it "passed" — the bucket may be too heavy to hang safely. Record the load as ">3 kg" and note that the bridge exceeded the test capacity.
- The design rules derived from the class data are the culmination of the bridge project. Collect them and display them in the classroom. They are genuine engineering principles, discovered by testing — not memorised from a textbook.
