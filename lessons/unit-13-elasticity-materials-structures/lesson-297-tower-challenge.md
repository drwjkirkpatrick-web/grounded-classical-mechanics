# Lesson 297: Tower Challenge: Tallest Stable Structure

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.5, G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** tower

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a free-standing tower from paper or drinking straws that is both tall and stable.
2. Explain why a wide base improves stability against overturning and why a tall narrow tower topples easily.
3. Test the tower in a fan breeze for 10 seconds and identify whether it fails by toppling, buckling, or base sliding.
4. Decide whether guy ropes (tension ties to the ground) should be allowed and justify the choice with a stability argument.

## Materials
- 20–30 sheets of A4 paper (or ~50 drinking straws)
- A small desk fan (or a large piece of cardboard to flap and make wind)
- A tape measure or ruler marked in centimetres
- Masking tape or cellotape
- Scissors
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- String or sisal twine (for optional guy ropes)
- Small weights (stones or coins) to tape at the base as ballast
- A flat floor area or long table
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **stability** (utulivu) — the ability of a structure to stay upright when pushed by wind or load
- **overturning moment** (mmentare wa kugeuza) — the wind force × height; the higher the push, the more it tips the tower
- **restoring moment** (mmentare wa kurudisha) — the weight × base width; a wide, heavy base resists tipping
- **base width** (upana wa msingi) — the distance across the bottom of the tower; wider = more stable
- **guy rope** (kamba ya usaidizi) — a tension rope angled from the tower to the ground that stops it from tipping
- **buckling** (kujikunja) — a thin column bending sideways and collapsing under load before the material breaks

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A tall thin tower and a short wide tower are both pushed by the same wind. Which one tips over more easily? Why?
2. Why do mobile-phone masts and radio towers have steel cables running from the top to the ground?
3. If you want your paper tower to stand in a fan breeze, what two things can you change about its base?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The tall thin tower tips more easily. The wind force acts higher up, so the overturning moment (force × height) is larger. The narrow base gives a small restoring moment (weight × base width), so it cannot resist the tip.
2. The cables are guy ropes — they hold the mast in tension and stop it from tipping in the wind. Without them, a tall thin mast would overturn because its base is narrow.
3. Make the base wider (more restoring moment) and make the base heavier (more weight to resist tipping).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show a picture of a tall radio mast with guy ropes and a short wide water tank stand. Ask: *which one would survive a strong wind, and why?* Take answers. Push a tall thin water bottle with one finger — it tips easily. Push a short wide sufuria — it barely moves. Board: **stability = restoring moment vs overturning moment; wide, heavy base wins**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the tower (see Hands-On Build). They roll or fold paper into columns, tape them into a tower, and measure the height. They aim for the tallest tower that stands for 10 seconds in the fan breeze. They try a narrow base, then a wide base, then add ballast. Some pairs try guy ropes. Results go on a class table: height | base width | ballast | guy ropes? | survived 10 s? | failure mode.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Wind pushes the tower sideways. The overturning moment = wind force × height to the centre of pressure. The restoring moment = weight × half the base width. If the overturning moment exceeds the restoring moment, the tower tips. A tall tower has a large overturning moment; a narrow, light base has a small restoring moment — so it falls. Two fixes: widen the base (more restoring moment) and add weight at the bottom (more restoring moment). Guy ropes add an external restoring force — they pull the tower back down — which is why real masts use them. A thin paper column can also buckle (bend sideways) before it tips — making the column slightly thicker or bracing it with diagonal strips prevents buckling. Board: **tall + narrow = toppling; wide + heavy base = stable; guy ropes = external support**.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *stability is a balance of moments — the wind tries to tip, the base resists*. Homework: find three tall structures near your home (mast, water tank, tree, flagpole). For each, note the base width and whether it has guy ropes or bracing. Write one sentence on why it stands.

## Hands-On Build: Paper/Straw Wind Tower
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- 20–30 sheets of A4 paper (or ~50 drinking straws)
- Masking tape or cellotape
- Scissors
- A small desk fan (or a large piece of cardboard to flap)
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- String or sisal twine (for optional guy ropes)
- Small stones or coins (for ballast)
- A tape measure or ruler
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Roll 3–4 sheets of A4 paper into tight columns (or tape straws into bundles). These are the legs. **Checkpoint:** each column is the same length and thickness.
2. Tape the columns together at the top to form a tower frame. Stand it on the floor. Measure the height and the base width. **Checkpoint:** the tower stands on its own for at least 5 seconds before the fan.
3. Set the fan to low speed about 1 metre away. Turn it on for 10 seconds. Observe: does the tower topple, buckle, or slide? Record the result. **Checkpoint:** the fan distance and speed are the same for every test so results are comparable.
4. Now rebuild with a wider base — splay the legs outward at the bottom so the base width is at least double. Keep the height the same if possible. Test again in the fan for 10 seconds. **Checkpoint:** the wider base should make the tower more stable.
5. Add ballast — tape stones or coins to the bottom of the legs. Test again. **Checkpoint:** added weight at the base increases the restoring moment.
6. Optional: attach guy ropes — tie string from near the top of the tower to weights on the floor, angled outward at three or four points. Test in the fan. **Checkpoint:** the guy ropes should hold the tower even if the base is narrow.
7. Record all results on the class table: height | base width | ballast (g or stones) | guy ropes? | survived 10 s? | failure mode (toppled, buckled, slid).

### Common Mistakes
- **Tape joints too weak** — the tower sags at the joints before the wind even starts. Reinforce every joint with extra tape.
- **Base not flat** — if one leg is shorter, the tower wobbles and tips even without wind. Check that all legs touch the floor.
- **Fan too close or too strong** — a fan 30 cm away on high speed blows everything down and teaches nothing. Keep the fan 1 metre away on low speed; increase only to test improvements.
- **Ballast at the top** — taping weights high up raises the centre of mass and makes the tower less stable. Always put ballast at the bottom.
- **Guy ropes too loose** — loose ropes do nothing. They must be taut and anchored to a weight on the floor.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A tall thin tower and a short wide tower are pushed by the same wind. Which tips more easily? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why does widening the base make a tower more stable?
3. What is a guy rope, and how does it help a tall mast stay upright?
4. Your tower buckled (bent sideways) before it tipped. What could you change to stop the buckling?
5. Challenge: your tower survived the fan but slid sideways along the floor. What caused the sliding, and how do you stop it?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The tall thin tower tips more easily. The wind force acts higher up, giving a larger overturning moment (force × height). The narrow base gives a small restoring moment (weight × base width), so it cannot resist the tip.
2. Widening the base increases the restoring moment — the weight acts over a larger lever arm, so more wind force is needed to tip the tower. A wide base also lowers the centre of mass relative to the base, further improving stability.
3. A guy rope is a tension cable angled from the tower to an anchor on the ground. It pulls the tower back toward the ground when the wind pushes it, adding an external restoring force. This lets a tall, narrow mast stay upright without a massive base.
4. Buckling means the column is too thin and bends sideways. Fix it by making the column thicker (more paper or straws), adding diagonal bracing between the legs, or shortening the tower to reduce the load on the column.
5. The wind force overcame the friction between the base and the floor. Stop it by adding weight at the base (more friction), roughening the bottom of the legs, or taping the base to the floor (a fixed base).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Estimate the overturning moment from wind at 5 m/s on your tower face, and size the base by moments.
   **Worked Answer:** The wind pressure on a flat face is approximately p ≈ ½ρv², where ρ ≈ 1.2 kg/m³ (air density) and v = 5 m/s. So p ≈ 0.5 × 1.2 × 25 = 15 Pa. If the tower face is 0.3 m wide and 0.6 m tall, the face area A = 0.18 m². The wind force F = p × A = 15 × 0.18 = 2.7 N. The force acts at the centre of pressure, roughly half the height: h = 0.3 m. Overturning moment M_over = F × h = 2.7 × 0.3 = 0.81 N·m. The restoring moment is M_restore = W × (b/2), where W is the tower weight and b is the base width. If the tower weighs 0.5 kg, W = 0.5 × 9.8 = 4.9 N. For stability, M_restore ≥ M_over: 4.9 × (b/2) ≥ 0.81 → b/2 ≥ 0.165 → b ≥ 0.33 m. So the base must be at least 33 cm wide to resist a 5 m/s wind. If the actual base is 20 cm, the tower will tip. Widening to 35 cm or adding ballast (to increase W) makes it stable.
2. **Q:** Your tower is 60 cm tall with a 20 cm base and weighs 0.4 kg. The fan produces a wind force of 2 N acting at 30 cm height. Will it tip? If not, what is the safety factor?
   **Worked Answer:** Overturning moment M_over = F × h = 2 × 0.30 = 0.60 N·m. Restoring moment M_restore = W × (b/2) = (0.4 × 9.8) × 0.10 = 0.392 N·m. Since M_over (0.60) > M_restore (0.392), the tower will tip. Safety factor = M_restore / M_over = 0.392 / 0.60 = 0.65 — less than 1, so it fails. To make it stable, either widen the base: need b such that 3.92 × (b/2) ≥ 0.60 → b ≥ 0.306 m (31 cm), or add ballast: need W such that W × 0.10 ≥ 0.60 → W ≥ 6.0 N → m ≥ 0.61 kg (add ~210 g at the base).
3. **Q:** A real radio mast is 20 m tall with a 2 m square base and weighs 5,000 kg. The wind force at storm speed is 1,500 N acting at 10 m height. Will it tip? Should guy ropes be added?
   **Worked Answer:** M_over = F × h = 1,500 × 10 = 15,000 N·m. M_restore = W × (b/2) = (5,000 × 9.8) × 1.0 = 49,000 N·m. Safety factor = 49,000 / 15,000 = 3.3 — the mast is stable by itself. But real masts face gusts, turbulence, and fatigue, so engineers add guy ropes for a safety factor of 2–3 above the static calculation. Guy ropes also stop buckling of the thin mast column by providing lateral support at intervals up the height. So even though the static calculation says it is stable, guy ropes are added for dynamic loads and column stability.

## Safety Notes
- The fan should be on low speed and at least 1 metre away — a high-speed fan can blow paper into faces and eyes.
- Do not stand on chairs or tables to build tall towers — build on the floor.
- Scissors should be used carefully — younger learners may need help with cutting.
- Falling towers can startle — keep the area clear of breakable objects.
- Do not climb on or lean on the towers — they are paper, not load-bearing.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should understand moments (force × distance) from earlier units, and tension vs compression from Unit 13 lessons on strong shapes. This lesson applies moments to a real stability problem.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the build, the qualitative observation (wide base = stable, tall narrow = tippy), and recording whether the tower survived 10 seconds. Ages 14–17 should estimate the overturning and restoring moments and size the base or ballast needed for a given wind.
- Local resource tips: A4 paper, straws, tape, string, and stones are available anywhere. A desk fan is common in offices and staffrooms; if unavailable, a large piece of cardboard flapped steadily works well. The fan distance and speed must be kept constant for fair comparison.
- The debate on guy ropes is the key teaching moment. Some learners will argue guy ropes are "cheating" because they make the challenge too easy. The physics answer: guy ropes are a legitimate engineering solution — every tall radio mast uses them. The challenge is to understand why they work (external restoring force) and when they are needed (tall, narrow structures). Let pairs choose: with or without guy ropes, but record which and compare.
- This lesson is the culmination of the tower project. Learners apply shape (columns, bracing), material (paper stiffness), and stability (base width, ballast, guy ropes) in one build. The failure modes (toppling, buckling, sliding) are all real engineering failures — discuss each one.
- If time is short, the fan test can be done as a class demonstration with one or two towers rather than every pair. But the build should be done by every pair — the hands-on experience is the point.
