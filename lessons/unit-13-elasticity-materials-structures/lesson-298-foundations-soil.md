# Lesson 298: Foundations and Soil: The Ground Is a Material Too

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain why a building must spread its weight over the soil beneath it and what happens if the soil cannot bear the load.
2. Distinguish compacted soil from loose soil by a simple bearing test using a weighted shoe box.
3. Describe why walls crack unevenly when one part of the foundation settles more than another.
4. Compare mud, stone, and concrete footings and explain why stone and concrete are used for permanent buildings.

## Materials
- A sturdy shoe box (or a flat plastic tray)
- Dry sand (about 2 kg) — to model loose soil
- The same sand, wetted and compacted by hand — to model compacted soil
- A few bricks or heavy books (to load the box)
- A piece of flat stone or a tile (to model a stone footing)
- A lump of dry clay or a dried mud block (to model a mud footing)
- A ruler or tape measure
- A flat surface (table or ground)
- Pictures of cracked walls (differential settlement) — if available
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **foundation** (msingi) — the part of a building that transfers its weight to the ground
- **bearing capacity** (uwezo wa kubeba) — how much load the soil can take before it gives way
- **settlement** (kushuka) — the slow sinking of a building into the soil under its own weight
- **differential settlement** (kushuka tofauti) — when one side sinks more than the other, causing cracks
- **footing** (mguu wa msingi) — the widened base of a foundation that spreads the load over more soil
- **compaction** (kubana) — pressing soil tightly so it bears more load; loose soil settles, compacted soil does not

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Why does a heavy sufuria of water not sink into a hard floor but does sink into soft mud?
2. A house wall cracks on one side but not the other. What likely caused the uneven cracking?
3. Why do builders dig a trench and fill it with stones or concrete before building the wall above?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The hard floor has high bearing capacity — it resists the load. Soft mud has low bearing capacity — it deforms and lets the sufuria sink. The same weight, different ground, different result.
2. Differential settlement — one side of the foundation sank more than the other because the soil under it was softer or less compacted. The wall twists and cracks at the weak point.
3. The trench and stones (or concrete) form a footing — a widened base that spreads the wall's weight over more soil, reducing the pressure on the ground so it does not settle or crack.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — place a brick on loose dry sand and watch it sink slightly. Then compact the sand with your hand or a flat stone and place the same brick on top — it barely sinks. Ask: *why does the same brick behave differently?* The ground is a material, and its bearing capacity changes with compaction. Board: **the ground is a material — loose soil settles, compacted soil bears**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs do the bearing test (see Hands-On Build). They fill a shoe box with loose dry sand, place a flat stone on the sand, and load it with bricks one at a time. They measure how much the stone sinks. Then they wet and compact the sand, repeat the test, and compare. They also test a dried mud block as a footing vs a stone as a footing on the same sand. Results go on a class table: soil type | footing material | load at failure | settlement (mm).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Every building pushes down on the ground. The ground must push back (bearing capacity). If the soil is loose, it compresses and the building sinks (settlement). If one side sinks more than the other, the wall twists and cracks (differential settlement) — this is the most common cause of cracked walls in Kenyan rural houses. A footing (stone, concrete, or compacted murram) spreads the load over a larger area, reducing the pressure on the soil (pressure = force ÷ area). Wider footing = less pressure = less settlement. Compacted soil bears more than loose soil because the grains are packed tightly and cannot rearrange. Mud footings soften in rain and settle; stone and concrete do not. Board: **pressure = force ÷ area; wider footing = less pressure; compacted soil = more bearing**.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the ground is a material — spread the load, compact the soil, and the building stands*. Homework: find a cracked wall near your home or school. Sketch the crack and write one sentence on whether you think it was caused by differential settlement (one side sinking more than the other).

## Hands-On Build: Shoe-Box Bearing Test
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- A sturdy shoe box or flat tray
- About 2 kg of dry sand
- Water (a small amount, to compact the sand)
- A flat stone or tile (to model a stone footing)
- A dried mud block (to model a mud footing)
- 3–4 bricks or heavy books (to load)
- A ruler or tape measure
- A flat table or ground surface
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Fill the shoe box with dry loose sand to about 3 cm depth. Level the surface. **Checkpoint:** the sand is loose and dry — do not pack it yet.
2. Place the flat stone on the sand. Measure the height of the stone above the box rim. **Checkpoint:** record this starting height — it is the zero-load reference.
3. Place one brick on the stone. Wait 5 seconds. Measure how much the stone has sunk. Record the settlement. **Checkpoint:** place the brick gently — dropping it is an impact, not a static load.
4. Add a second brick, then a third. Measure the settlement after each. Record the total load and settlement. **Checkpoint:** the stone sinks visibly into the loose sand.
5. Now remove everything. Wet the sand slightly and compact it by pressing hard with the flat stone or your fist for 1 minute. Re-level the surface. **Checkpoint:** the sand is now firm and does not give when pressed with a finger.
6. Repeat the test: place the stone, load with bricks one at a time, measure settlement. **Checkpoint:** the compacted sand should show much less settlement than the loose sand for the same load.
7. Now test a dried mud block as the footing on loose sand. Load it and observe: does it crack, soften, or sink? Compare with the stone footing. **Checkpoint:** the mud block may crack or soften — note the failure mode.
8. Record all results on the class table: soil type | footing material | load at failure (bricks) | settlement (mm or cm).

### Common Mistakes
- **Sand too shallow** — if the sand is less than 2 cm deep, the stone hits the bottom of the box and the test measures the box, not the soil. Use at least 3 cm of sand.
- **Wetting the sand too much** — the sand should be damp, not a puddle. Too much water turns it to slurry, which is a different test (liquefaction).
- **Loading too fast** — dropping bricks adds impact load. Place them gently and wait 5 seconds for the soil to settle before measuring.
- **Not measuring from the same reference** — measure the stone height from the same point on the box rim each time, or the settlement readings are not comparable.
- **Mud block not dry** — a wet mud block is not a footing; it is mud. Dry the block in the sun for a day before the lesson.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Why does a heavy load sink into soft mud but not into a hard floor? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is differential settlement, and why does it crack walls? (mirrors pre-test)
3. How does a footing (widened foundation base) reduce the pressure on the soil?
4. Why is compacted soil a better foundation material than loose soil?
5. Challenge: a mud footing and a stone footing are placed on the same sand and loaded equally. Which settles more, and why?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The hard floor has high bearing capacity — it resists the load without deforming. Soft mud has low bearing capacity — it deforms and lets the load sink. The weight is the same; the ground's ability to bear it differs.
2. Differential settlement is when one side of a foundation sinks more than the other, usually because the soil under one side is softer or less compacted. The wall twists because one side drops, and the twisting force cracks the masonry at the weak point.
3. A footing widens the base of the foundation, spreading the same force over a larger area. Since pressure = force ÷ area, a larger area means less pressure on the soil, so the soil settles less.
4. Compacted soil has grains packed tightly together — they cannot easily rearrange or compress further, so it bears more load before settling. Loose soil has air gaps between grains; under load, the grains rearrange and the soil compresses, causing settlement.
5. The mud footing settles more. Mud is softer than stone and, if it gets wet, it softens further and deforms under load. Stone is hard, stiff, and does not soften in water, so it transfers the load to the sand with minimal settlement of its own.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Compute the safe footing width: a house wall carries 20,000 N per metre on soil with a bearing capacity of 100 kPa. How wide must the footing be?
   **Worked Answer:** Bearing capacity is the maximum pressure the soil can take: q = 100 kPa = 100,000 N/m². The wall load per metre is F = 20,000 N per metre. The footing must spread this load so the pressure on the soil does not exceed the bearing capacity: pressure = force ÷ area. For a 1 m length of wall, the footing area is width × 1 m. So p = F / (w × 1) = 20,000 / w. For safety: p ≤ q → 20,000 / w ≤ 100,000 → w ≥ 20,000 / 100,000 = 0.20 m. So the footing must be at least 20 cm wide. In practice, engineers add a safety factor of 2–3, so the footing would be 40–60 cm wide for a permanent building.
2. **Q:** A wall weighs 30,000 N per metre. The soil bearing capacity is 80 kPa. The builder uses a 25 cm wide footing. Is it safe? If not, what happens?
   **Worked Answer:** Pressure on the soil: p = F / A = 30,000 / (0.25 × 1) = 120,000 N/m² = 120 kPa. The bearing capacity is 80 kPa. Since 120 kPa > 80 kPa, the footing is not safe — the soil is overloaded. The soil will compress and the wall will settle. If the settlement is uneven (one side on softer soil), the wall will crack (differential settlement). To make it safe, widen the footing: w ≥ F / q = 30,000 / 80,000 = 0.375 m = 37.5 cm. So the footing must be at least 38 cm wide, or the soil must be compacted to raise its bearing capacity.
3. **Q:** A rural house has a mud footing 30 cm wide on loose soil (bearing capacity 50 kPa). The wall load is 15,000 N per metre. After one rainy season, the wall cracks. Explain the failure and propose two fixes.
   **Worked Answer:** Pressure on the soil: p = 15,000 / (0.30 × 1) = 50,000 N/m² = 50 kPa. This is exactly at the bearing capacity — there is no safety margin. In the rainy season, the mud footing softens (water reduces its stiffness) and the loose soil weakens further (water fills the air gaps, reducing effective stress). The bearing capacity drops below 50 kPa, so the soil settles. If one side of the house is on slightly softer soil, that side sinks more — differential settlement — and the wall cracks. Fix 1: replace the mud footing with a stone or concrete footing (does not soften in water) and widen it to 40 cm (p = 15,000 / 0.40 = 37.5 kPa, well below 50 kPa). Fix 2: compact the soil before building (raising bearing capacity to 80–100 kPa) and add a layer of murram or gravel under the footing to improve drainage so the soil does not weaken in rain.

## Safety Notes
- Do not overload the shoe box — a broken box spills sand everywhere and can cut fingers on sharp plastic edges.
- Bricks can fall during loading — keep toes clear and place bricks gently.
- Wet sand can be slippery — clean up spills immediately.
- Do not eat the sand or mud — they may contain bacteria or parasites.
- The mud block should be dry and handled gently — broken pieces can be sharp.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should understand pressure (force ÷ area) from earlier units. This lesson applies pressure to soil and foundations. No geotechnical engineering background is needed — the concepts are introduced through the hands-on test.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the build, the qualitative observation (loose sand sinks, compacted sand holds), and the idea that the ground is a material. Ages 14–17 should compute footing widths from bearing capacity and wall load, and explain differential settlement quantitatively.
- Local resource tips: sand, stones, bricks, and mud are all free or very cheap in Kenya. The shoe box can be any flat container. If no bricks are available, books or a bucket of water work as load. Pictures of cracked walls are common in any rural area — learners see them daily.
- The key insight: the ground is a material with a bearing capacity, just like steel has a tensile strength. Buildings fail not because the wall is weak but because the soil under it gives way. This is why foundations matter — they are the hidden structure that everything else depends on.
- The mud vs stone comparison is culturally relevant: many rural Kenyan houses use mud footings and crack after a few rainy seasons. The lesson explains why — mud softens in water and the soil settles. Stone and concrete footings are the upgrade, and the physics explains the cost.
- This lesson connects to Lesson 297 (Tower Challenge): the tower's base is a foundation, and the ballast is like a footing spreading the load. It also connects to Lesson 299 (Materials and Climate): water is the enemy of soil and mud foundations, which is why climate matters for material choice.
