# Lesson 299: Materials and Climate: Thatch vs Mabati and Maintenance

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, G10-PHY-4.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Compare thatch and mabati as roofing materials in terms of insulation, durability, cost, and maintenance.
2. Explain why thatch keeps a house cooler in hot weather and mabati makes it hotter, using the idea of thermal insulation.
3. Describe how rust, rot, and termites attack different materials and why maintenance schedules are applied physics.
4. Build a 30-year cost and comfort comparison between a thatch roof and a mabati roof using local prices.

## Materials
- A piece of corrugated mabati (roofing iron) — for show-and-tell
- A bundle of dry grass or palm leaves (thatch material) — if available
- A thermometer (if available) — for an optional temperature demo
- Pictures of rusted mabati, rotting thatch, and termite-damaged wood
- A board or large paper for the cost comparison table
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **insulation** (isolesheni) — a material's ability to block heat flow; thatch insulates, mabati does not
- **thermal conductivity** (conduktiviti) — how fast heat passes through a material; metal conducts heat fast, grass slow
- **rust** (kutu) — iron oxide formed when steel reacts with water and oxygen; weakens mabati over time
- **rot** (kuoza) — decay of organic material (grass, wood) by fungi and moisture; weakens thatch
- **termite** (mchwa) — insects that eat wood and thatch; a structural threat in tropical climates
- **lifecycle cost** (gharama ya maisha) — the total cost of a material over its whole life, including replacement and maintenance

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. On a hot sunny day, which roof makes the house inside hotter — thatch or mabati? Why?
2. What happens to a mabati roof after many rainy seasons if it is not painted or maintained?
3. Why does a thatch roof need to be replaced every few years while a mabati roof lasts much longer?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Mabati makes the house hotter. Metal is a good thermal conductor — heat from the sun passes straight through to the inside. Thatch is a thick layer of dry grass, which is a poor conductor (a good insulator), so it blocks the heat.
2. It rusts. Steel reacts with water and oxygen to form iron oxide (rust). The rust weakens the sheet, creates holes, and eventually the roof leaks and must be replaced.
3. Thatch is organic material — grass rots when it gets wet, and termites eat it. So it decays and must be replaced every 3–7 years. Mabati is metal — it does not rot or get eaten, so it lasts 15–30 years (with anti-rust paint).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a piece of mabati and a bundle of dry grass. Ask: *if you build a house with each of these as a roof, what is different about living under it?* Take answers. On a hot day, the mabati house is like an oven; the thatch house is cool. On a rainy day, the thatch leaks after a few years; the mabati leaks after 15–20 years (if rusted). Board: **thatch insulates but rots; mabati lasts but conducts heat**.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — the class builds a comparison table on the board (no build, but structured discussion and data collection). Learners contribute what they know from home: how often the thatch roof is replaced, how much it costs, how hot the mabati house gets, when the mabati was last painted, whether termites have attacked any wooden structures. The teacher guides the table: Property | Thatch | Mabati. Rows: insulation, durability, cost per cycle, replacement frequency, maintenance, fire risk, comfort in heat, comfort in rain, environmental impact. If a thermometer is available, the teacher places one under a piece of mabati in the sun and one under a thick bundle of grass, and reads the temperatures after 5 minutes.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Thatch is a thick layer of dry grass full of air pockets — air is a poor conductor, so heat from the sun does not pass through easily. This makes thatch a good insulator: the house stays cool in the heat and warm at night. But grass is organic — fungi break it down when it is wet, and termites eat the fibres. So thatch rots and must be replaced every 3–7 years. Mabati is steel — a metal, and metals are good conductors. Heat from the sun passes straight through, making the house hot. But metal does not rot or get eaten, so mabati lasts 15–30 years. The enemy of mabati is rust: iron + water + oxygen → iron oxide. Anti-rust paint (or galvanising — a zinc coating) slows the rust. Maintenance is applied physics: painting mabati slows rust (barrier to water and oxygen); re-thatching replaces rotted material; treating wood with used oil deters termites. Board: **thatch = insulation + rot; mabati = conductivity + rust; maintenance fights the failure mode**.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *every material has a climate it suits and a failure mode it fights — maintenance is applied physics*. Homework: find a roof near your home (thatch, mabati, or tile). Write down the material, its age if you can ask, and one sign of wear (rust, rot, missing patches). Write one sentence on what maintenance would fix it.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. On a hot sunny day, which roof keeps the house cooler — thatch or mabati? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is rust, and what causes it on a mabati roof?
3. Why does a thatch roof need replacing more often than a mabati roof?
4. Name one maintenance action for each roof type and the failure mode it fights.
5. Challenge: a homeowner wants a cool house in a hot area but also wants a roof that lasts 20 years. What would you recommend, and what is the trade-off?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Thatch keeps the house cooler. Grass is a poor thermal conductor (a good insulator) — the thick layer blocks heat from the sun. Mabati is metal, a good conductor, so heat passes through easily and the house gets hot.
2. Rust is iron oxide, formed when the steel in mabati reacts with water (rain) and oxygen (air). It weakens the sheet, creates holes, and eventually causes leaks.
3. Thatch is organic (grass) — it rots when wet (fungi break down the fibres) and termites eat it. So it decays every 3–7 years. Mabati is metal — it does not rot or get eaten, so it lasts 15–30 years with anti-rust protection.
4. Mabati: paint with anti-rust paint (fights rust by blocking water and oxygen). Thatch: re-thatch every 3–7 years (fights rot by replacing decayed grass). Also: treat wooden roof structures with used oil (fights termites).
5. Recommendation: use mabati for durability (20+ years) but add a ceiling (mabati or plywood with an air gap below the roof) to insulate against heat. The trade-off is cost — a ceiling adds expense, but it gives the durability of mabati with some of the insulation of thatch. Alternatively, use a thick thatch over a mabati frame (rare but combines both), though this is expensive and high-maintenance.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Lifecycle analysis: thatch replaced every 5 years vs mabati replaced once — compare the 30-year cost and comfort with numbers.
   **Worked Answer:** Assume a rural house roof area of 50 m². Thatch: costs ~KSh 3,000 per replacement (materials and labour, local rates). Replaced every 5 years → 6 replacements in 30 years. Total thatch cost = 6 × 3,000 = KSh 18,000. Mabati: costs ~KSh 20,000 for 50 m² of gauge-30 sheets (materials + fixings). Lasts 30 years with one repaint at year 15 (~KSh 2,000). Total mabati cost = 20,000 + 2,000 = KSh 22,000. So over 30 years, thatch is cheaper (KSh 18,000 vs KSh 22,000). But comfort differs: thatch keeps the house ~5–8 °C cooler in hot weather (insulation), while mabati can raise indoor temperature by 5–10 °C above ambient on a sunny day. If the homeowner values comfort (cool house), thatch wins. If they value low maintenance (no re-thatching every 5 years), mabati wins. The true lifecycle cost should include the cost of discomfort: if a mabati house needs a fan or more ventilation, that adds energy cost. Over 30 years, the total cost may be similar, but the thatch house is more comfortable and the mabati house is more durable. The best solution is often mabati with an insulated ceiling — higher upfront cost but low maintenance and good comfort.
2. **Q:** Explain why galvanising (zinc coating) slows rust on mabati, and estimate how much longer a galvanised sheet lasts vs an uncoated one.
   **Worked Answer:** Rust requires iron, water, and oxygen. Galvanising coats the steel with a layer of zinc. Zinc acts as a barrier — it blocks water and oxygen from reaching the iron. But zinc also acts as a sacrificial anode: zinc is more reactive than iron, so even if the coating is scratched, the zinc corrodes first (preferentially) and protects the iron beneath. A typical uncoated steel sheet in a Kenyan climate (two rainy seasons per year) rusts through in 5–10 years. A galvanised sheet (zinc coating ~120 g/m²) lasts 20–30 years before the zinc is consumed and the steel begins to rust. So galvanising extends the life by roughly 3–4×. This is why almost all mabati sold in Kenya is galvanised — the extra cost (maybe KSh 50 per sheet) is far cheaper than replacing the roof every 5 years.
3. **Q:** A thatch roof is 25 cm thick. The outside surface is 40 °C in the sun and the inside surface is 28 °C. Estimate the heat flow per square metre and compare with a mabati roof of the same area (inside surface ~38 °C).
   **Worked Answer:** Heat flow through a material is Q = k × A × ΔT / d, where k is thermal conductivity, A is area, ΔT is the temperature difference, and d is thickness. For thatch: k ≈ 0.08 W/(m·K) (dry grass, similar to fibreglass), A = 1 m², ΔT = 40 − 28 = 12 °C, d = 0.25 m. Q_thatch = 0.08 × 1 × 12 / 0.25 = 3.84 W per m². For mabati: k ≈ 50 W/(m·K) (steel), d ≈ 0.0005 m (0.5 mm), ΔT = 40 − 38 = 2 °C (the inside surface is almost as hot as the outside because metal conducts heat fast). Q_mabati = 50 × 1 × 2 / 0.0005 = 200,000 W per m² — but this is the heat through the metal itself; in reality, the inside surface radiates and convects heat to the room. The practical comparison: the thatch roof lets in ~4 W/m² of heat, while the mabati roof's inside surface is at 38 °C and radiates heat to the room at a rate of ~100–200 W/m² (by radiation and convection). So the mabati roof delivers roughly 30–50× more heat to the living space than the thatch roof. This is why a mabati house is an oven on a sunny day and a thatch house is cool.

## Safety Notes
- This is a discussion and observation lesson — no build, so the main safety concern is handling the mabati sample (sharp edges). The teacher should pre-tape the edges.
- Dry grass (thatch sample) can be a fire hazard — keep it away from any open flame or candle.
- If a thermometer is used in the sun demo, do not leave it in direct sunlight longer than needed — it can overheat and break.
- Do not eat the thatch material — it may harbour insects or mould.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should understand thermal conductivity (heat passing through a material) from earlier units. This lesson applies it to roofing materials. No advanced thermodynamics is needed — the comparison is qualitative for ages 10–13 and semi-quantitative for ages 14–17.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the comparison table (thatch is cool but rots; mabati is hot but lasts) and naming maintenance actions. Ages 14–17 should do the lifecycle cost calculation and the heat flow comparison.
- Local resource tips: mabati and thatch are everywhere in Kenya. Pictures of rusted mabati and rotting thatch are easy to find or take. Local prices for mabati and thatch replacement vary — use the prices the learners report from home for the cost table, not textbook figures.
- The key insight: material choice is a trade-off between properties (insulation vs durability) and climate (hot, wet, termite-prone). There is no "best" material — only the best material for a given climate, budget, and use. This is engineering judgement, not just physics.
- The maintenance angle is important: many learners see maintenance as a chore, not physics. Reframe it — painting mabati is applying a barrier to a chemical reaction (rust); re-thatching is replacing a material that has exceeded its service life. Maintenance is applied physics and chemistry.
- This lesson connects to Lesson 298 (Foundations and Soil): water is the enemy of both soil and thatch. It also connects to the Unit Review (Lesson 300): the materials quiz stations will test whether learners can match materials to climates and failure modes.
- If time allows, the temperature demo (thermometer under mabati vs under thatch in the sun) is a powerful visual — learners see the 10–15 °C difference directly. If no thermometer is available, learners can place their hand under each material in the sun and feel the difference qualitatively.
