# Lesson 300: Unit Review: Structure Expo

**Unit:** 13 — Elasticity, Materials & Structures  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3, G10-PHY-1.5  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Present their bridge or tower build to peers, explaining the forces it carries and the failure mode they discovered.
2. Rotate through materials quiz stations and answer questions on strength, stiffness, shape, soil, and climate.
3. Complete a unit quiz covering the key concepts of Unit 13: tensile strength, stress and strain, strong shapes, foundations, and material choice.
4. Write an expo caption for their build that names the forces carried, the failure mode found, and one improvement they would make.

## Materials
- Learners' bridge and tower builds from earlier lessons (brought to class or set up in the room)
- Tables or desks arranged as quiz stations (4–5 stations)
- Printed or board-written quiz questions at each station
- Answer sheets (one per learner) — or notebooks
- A timer or clock for station rotation
- Pictures or samples from the unit: mabati, sisal, cardboard I-beam, card arch, stone footing, thatch
- A board or flip chart for the expo captions
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **exposition** (maonyesho) — a public display where builders show and explain their work
- **failure mode** (namna ya kushindwa) — the way a structure fails: toppling, buckling, snapping, settling, rusting, rotting
- **force path** (njia ya nguvu) — the route a load takes through a structure from entry to the ground
- **retrospective** (tathmini ya nyuma) — looking back at a build to identify what worked, what failed, and what to improve

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Name two strong shapes you learned about in this unit and one structure that uses each.
2. What is the difference between strength and stiffness? Give one example of a material that is strong but not stiff.
3. Why does a building need a foundation wider than its walls?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Any two: corrugation (mabati roof, cardboard flutes); I-beam (steel beams in buildings); arch (stone culverts, doorways); dome (eggshell, mosque dome). Accept any correct shape with a correct example.
2. Strength is the force a material can take before breaking. Stiffness is how much it resists bending. Sisal rope is strong (holds a lot of weight) but not stiff (it sags and stretches). Steel is both strong and stiff.
3. A wider foundation spreads the wall's weight over more soil, reducing the pressure (force ÷ area) so the soil does not settle or crack the wall.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the teacher frames the lesson: *today is the Structure Expo — you are the engineers, and your builds are on display*. Explain the two parts: (a) a walk-around where each pair shows their bridge or tower and tells the story of the forces and the failure; (b) quiz stations where learners rotate and answer questions on the unit. Board the expo question: *what forces does your build carry, how did it fail, and what would you change?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — quiz station rotation. The teacher sets up 4–5 stations around the room, each with 2–3 questions on a unit topic: Station 1 (Materials and Properties), Station 2 (Stress, Strain and Tensile Testing), Station 3 (Strong Shapes: Folds, Arches, Domes), Station 4 (Foundations and Soil), Station 5 (Materials and Climate). Learners spend 3 minutes at each station, writing answers on their answer sheets. The teacher circulates to help and to check understanding. **Checkpoint:** every learner has answered at least one question at every station before the rotation ends.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — the class gathers at the builds. Two or three pairs present their bridge or tower to the group: they name the forces it carries (tension in the deck, compression in the arch, overturning moment on the tower), describe how it failed (buckled, snapped, toppled, settled), and state one improvement (wider base, thicker column, stronger joints, better material). The teacher consolidates the unit's big ideas on the board: *strength = material × cross-section; shape gives strength (corrugation, I-beam, arch, dome); stability = restoring moment vs overturning moment; foundations spread the load; material choice depends on climate and failure mode*.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer the unit quiz; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *every structure is a story of forces, materials, and failure — the engineer's job is to choose the right material and shape for the load and the climate*. Homework: write the expo caption for your build (see Advanced Questions) — forces carried, failure mode found, one improvement. Bring it next lesson for the display board.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Name two strong shapes from this unit and one structure that uses each. (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is the difference between strength and stiffness? Give one example of a material that is strong but not stiff. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A corrugated mabati roof sheet is stiffer than a flat sheet of the same thickness. Why?
4. A house wall cracks on one side after a rainy season. What is the likely cause, and how would a wider footing help?
5. Challenge: you are advising a farmer who wants to build a grain store in a hot, dry, termite-prone area. Choose a roofing material and a foundation type, and justify each choice with one property.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Any two: corrugation (mabati); I-beam (steel beam); arch (stone culvert); dome (mosque, eggshell). Accept any correct shape with a correct example.
2. Strength is the force a material takes before breaking. Stiffness is how much it resists bending. Sisal rope is strong (holds weight) but not stiff (it sags and stretches). Steel is both. Chalk is stiff but not strong (snaps easily).
3. Corrugation pushes the material away from the neutral axis (up and down in the waves), increasing the second moment of area. A larger second moment of area means more bending resistance — the sheet is stiffer without adding material.
4. Differential settlement — one side of the foundation sank more than the other because the soil was softer or less compacted, and the rain weakened it further. A wider footing spreads the wall's weight over more soil, reducing the pressure (force ÷ area) so each side settles less and the settlement is more even, preventing the crack.
5. Roofing: mabati (galvanised) — it is durable, does not rot or get eaten by termites, and in a dry area the heat is less of a problem than in a wet area. (Thatch would rot and attract termites; in a dry area it is also a fire risk.) Foundation: stone or concrete footing on compacted soil — stone does not soften in water, and compaction raises the bearing capacity. (A mud footing would attract termites and soften in any rain.) Justification: the chosen materials resist the climate's failure modes — termites (mabati, stone), heat (mabati is acceptable in dry areas), and soil settlement (compacted base + stone footing).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Write the expo caption for your build: forces carried, failure mode found, one improvement.
   **Worked Answer:** (Example for a paper tower that buckled in the fan test.) *Forces carried: the tower legs carried the weight of the tower in compression. The wind exerted a sideways force at the centre of pressure, creating an overturning moment about the base. The base resisted with a restoring moment (weight × half the base width). Failure mode: the tower buckled — the front leg bent sideways at mid-height before the base tipped, because the column was too thin for its length. Improvement: add diagonal bracing between the legs at mid-height to stop the column from buckling, and widen the base from 15 cm to 25 cm to increase the restoring moment. Expected result: the braced tower should survive the 10-second fan test without buckling or tipping.* (Accept any caption that correctly names the forces, the failure mode, and a specific, physics-based improvement.)
2. **Q:** A bridge built in this unit carried 2 kg before the deck snapped at a joint. The joint was taped. Estimate the stress at the joint and propose a stronger joint design.
   **Worked Answer:** If the deck is a paper strip 2 cm wide and the load is 2 kg (F = 2 × 9.8 = 19.6 N), the tensile stress in the deck at the joint is σ = F / A. If the paper is 0.2 mm thick, A = 0.02 m × 0.0002 m = 4 × 10⁻⁶ m². σ = 19.6 / (4 × 10⁻⁶) = 4.9 × 10⁶ Pa = 4.9 MPa. Paper has a tensile strength of ~20–30 MPa, so the material itself did not fail — the joint did (tape peels at a lower force than the paper tears). A stronger joint: overlap the paper strips by at least 3 cm and tape both sides (double-sided lap joint), or fold the ends into a hook and interlock them (a mechanical joint that does not rely on tape adhesive). The best improvement is to avoid the joint entirely — use a single continuous strip for the deck, or use a gusset (a small triangle of card glued over the joint on both sides) to spread the load. The lesson: joints are usually the weak point, not the material.
3. **Q:** Looking back at the whole unit, rank these five concepts in order of importance for a rural Kenyan builder and justify your ranking: (a) material choice, (b) shape, (c) foundation, (d) maintenance, (e) cross-section.
   **Worked Answer:** (Accept any justified ranking — the point is the reasoning, not the order. A strong answer:) 1. Foundation — without a stable base, nothing else matters; differential settlement cracks the whole building (Lesson 298). 2. Material choice — the material must resist the local failure mode: rot, rust, termites, or heat (Lesson 299). 3. Shape — corrugation, arches, and I-beams give strength without adding material; this is the cheapest way to improve a structure (Lessons 287–288). 4. Cross-section — more material shares the load; a thicker column or rope holds more (Lessons 285–286). 5. Maintenance — important for longevity, but if the first four are right, maintenance is minimal; if they are wrong, no amount of maintenance saves the structure. The ranking reflects a build order: you start from the ground up (foundation), choose the material for the climate, use shape to maximise strength, size the cross-section for the load, and maintain against the failure mode. A builder who skips the foundation will see the wall crack no matter how good the material or shape is.

## Safety Notes
- This is a review lesson with no build, so the main safety concern is moving around the room during station rotation — no running.
- Builds on display (bridges, towers) may have sharp wire or tape edges — do not touch other groups' builds without permission.
- If builds are tested during the expo (e.g., loading a bridge), follow the safety notes from the original build lesson: keep toes clear of falling loads, do not stand on structures, and wear goggles if wire is involved.
- Quiz stations with material samples (mabati, sisal) should have sharp edges taped — the teacher pre-checks all samples.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson reviews the entire unit. Learners should have completed the builds (bridge, tower) and the hands-on tests (tensile test, shape test, arch, foundations, materials survey). If some learners missed earlier lessons, pair them with learners who attended so they can learn from the builds on display.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the expo presentation (naming the forces and the failure mode) and the quiz stations with simpler questions (match the material to the use, name the strong shape). Ages 14–17 should write the full expo caption (forces, failure, improvement) and answer the quantitative quiz questions (stress, footing width, lifecycle cost).
- Local resource tips: the builds are the resource — learners bring their own work. Quiz stations need only printed questions and answer sheets. If no printer is available, write questions on the board or on large paper at each station. Material samples (mabati, sisal, stone, cardboard) can be reused from earlier lessons.
- The expo format is powerful because it makes learners the experts. They present their build, explain what happened, and take questions from peers. This is assessment by presentation — the teacher listens and notes who understands the forces and who is guessing. It is also more memorable than a written test alone.
- The quiz stations should cover all five major topics of the unit: (1) Materials and Properties (Lesson 277), (2) Tensile Testing and Stress-Strain (Lessons 285–286), (3) Strong Shapes (Lessons 287–288), (4) Foundations and Soil (Lesson 298), (5) Materials and Climate (Lesson 299). Three minutes per station × 5 stations = 15 minutes, which fits the Explore block.
- The unit quiz (Post-Test) should be short — 5 questions covering the big ideas. The challenge question (grain store design) is a synthesis task that tests whether learners can combine material choice, foundation design, and climate knowledge — the core skill of the unit.
- This is the final lesson of Unit 13. The expo captions (homework) can be displayed on a class board as a record of the unit. If the school has a science fair or parents' day, the builds and captions make an excellent display — they show real engineering thinking with local materials.
