# Lesson 301: Wind Is Moving Air: Where It Comes From

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-4.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that wind is air moving horizontally from high-pressure to low-pressure regions, driven by uneven solar heating of the Earth's surface.
2. Describe how convection currents form when land and water heat at different rates, producing sea breezes and land breezes.
3. Name three major Kenyan wind systems — the Lake Turkana jet, the Ngong Hills winds, and the coastal monsoons — and locate them on a map.
4. Predict which way a sea breeze blows (daytime) and explain why it reverses at night.

## Materials
- A map of Kenya showing Lake Turkana, the Ngong Hills near Nairobi, and the Indian Ocean coast (printed or drawn on board)
- Candle in a shallow box with a clear plastic or glass side (teacher convection demo)
- A strip of tissue paper or thin paper, about 2 cm × 10 cm
- Incense stick or a piece of smouldering grass (to make smoke visible)
- Chalk and board
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **wind** (upepo) — air moving horizontally across the Earth's surface
- **convection** (mtiririko wa joto) — warm fluid rises and cooler fluid sinks, setting up a circulating current
- **pressure** (shinikizo) — the push of air on a surface, measured in pascals
- **sea breeze** (upepo wa bahari) — wind blowing from the sea to the land during the day
- **land breeze** (upepo wa nchi kavu) — wind blowing from the land to the sea at night
- **monsoon** (musimu) — seasonal wind that reverses direction between summer and winter

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What is wind, in one sentence?
2. Why does air over hot ground rise?
3. Name one place in Kenya that is known for strong winds.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Wind is air moving horizontally from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure.
2. Heating makes the air expand and become less dense, so it floats upward (convection); cooler air flows in to replace it.
3. Any of: Lake Turkana, Ngong Hills, the Indian Ocean coast (Mombasa/Malindi), Marsabit, or the Kerio Valley. Accept any well-known windy location in Kenya.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — ask learners: *When you stand outside on a hot afternoon near a large body of water, which way does the breeze come from? Has anyone visited the coast and felt the afternoon sea breeze?* Let two or three learners share their experience. Write the words "wind" and "convection" on the board as the day's key ideas.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teacher demonstrates convection with the candle-in-a-box setup. Light the candle, hold the tissue strip above the opening, and let learners observe the strip flutter upward. Then introduce a little smoke from the incense near the base of the box so learners can see the smoke rise in the warm column and sink at the cooler sides. Ask groups of 3–4 to draw what they see and label "warm air rises" and "cool air sinks." Next, show the map of Kenya and point to Lake Turkana, the Ngong Hills, and the coast. Ask: *Why would wind be strong in these places?* Let groups discuss for 3 minutes and write one sentence per group.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board: draw a cross-section of the coast showing land and sea. Mark the sun heating the land faster than the water. Show warm air rising over land, cool air flowing in from the sea — this is the sea breeze (daytime). Then draw the night version: land cools faster, the sea is now warmer, air rises over the sea, and the breeze reverses — this is the land breeze. Confront the misconception that *wind is caused by the Earth spinning* — the Earth's rotation does help deflect wind (Coriolis effect), but the primary driver is uneven solar heating and convection. Briefly mention the Lake Turkana jet (channelled wind through the valley), the Ngong Hills (wind accelerated over the ridge), and the coastal monsoons (seasonal reversal driven by land–sea temperature contrast).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *wind is moving air, and it moves because the Sun heats the Earth unevenly.* Homework: stand outside your home at midday and again after sunset. Note the wind direction each time (use a wet finger or a tissue on a string). Write two sentences: one for the daytime observation and one for the evening.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What is wind, in one sentence? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why does air over hot ground rise? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name one place in Kenya that is known for strong winds. (mirrors pre-test)
4. Draw a simple diagram of a daytime sea breeze. Label the land, the sea, and the direction the wind blows.
5. Challenge: why does the sea breeze reverse direction at night?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Wind is air moving horizontally from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure.
2. Heating makes the air expand and become less dense, so it floats upward; cooler air flows in to replace it.
3. Any of: Lake Turkana, Ngong Hills, the Indian Ocean coast, Marsabit, Kerio Valley. Accept any well-known windy location in Kenya.
4. The diagram should show the Sun heating the land, warm air rising over the land, cool air flowing from the sea toward the land at ground level, and the return flow aloft from land to sea. The wind arrow at the surface points from sea to land.
5. At night the land cools faster than the sea. The sea is now relatively warmer, so air rises over the sea and the surface breeze flows from the land toward the sea — the reverse of the daytime sea breeze.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** The specific heat capacity of water is about 4180 J/(kg·°C) and that of dry land (rock/soil) is about 800 J/(kg·°C). Explain in two sentences why a large body of water heats up more slowly than the adjacent land during the day, and why this difference drives a sea breeze.
   **Worked Answer:** Water has a much higher specific heat capacity than rock or soil, so the same solar energy input raises the temperature of water far less than it raises the temperature of the land. The land surface therefore becomes warmer than the sea surface during the day, the air above the land heats and rises, and cooler air flows in from the sea to replace it — producing the sea breeze.
2. **Q:** The Lake Turkana wind corridor has average wind speeds of about 11 m/s at 50 m height, among the highest in Africa. Explain why a valley or gap between mountains can accelerate wind, and predict what happens to the wind speed if the gap narrows.
   **Worked Answer:** When a large-scale air mass is forced through a valley or gap between ridges, the same volume of air must pass through a smaller cross-sectional area. By continuity (A₁v₁ = A₂v₂), if the area A decreases, the speed v must increase. The Lake Turkana corridor channels regional winds between the highlands, so the wind accelerates as the gap narrows. A narrower gap would further increase the wind speed, up to the limit where turbulence and friction with the valley walls begin to dissipate the extra energy.
3. **Q:** The coastal monsoons reverse between the two halves of the year: the Northeast monsoon (Kaskazi) blows from December to March and the Southeast monsoon (Kusi) blows from April to September. Explain why a monsoon reverses seasonally, using the land–sea temperature contrast.
   **Worked Answer:** A monsoon is driven by the same land–sea heating contrast as a sea breeze, but on a continental and seasonal scale. In the northern hemisphere summer (around June–August), the Asian landmass heats strongly and air rises over the land, drawing moist air in from the Indian Ocean — this is the Southeast monsoon (Kusi) along the East African coast. In the northern hemisphere winter (around December–February), the land cools relative to the ocean, the pressure pattern reverses, and air flows from the land toward the ocean — the Northeast monsoon (Kaskazi). The reversal is driven by the seasonal switch in which surface (land or sea) is warmer, exactly as the sea breeze reverses between day and night but on a yearly cycle.

## Safety Notes
- The candle-in-a-box demonstration involves an open flame. Keep the candle on a stable surface away from curtains, paper, or any flammable material. The teacher handles the candle; learners observe from their seats.
- Have a cup of water or a damp cloth ready to extinguish the candle. Never leave a lit candle unattended.
- The incense or smouldering grass produces smoke — do the demo in a ventilated room or near an open window. Learners with asthma should sit further from the smoke source.
- Do not let learners touch the hot wax or the candle glass after the demo.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that hot air rises (Unit 7, pressure and fluids) and that the Sun is the primary energy source for weather. No calculation is required in this lesson.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the convection demo, the map work, and the day/night sea breeze diagram. Ages 14–17 tackle the specific heat capacity argument, the continuity equation for the Turkana jet, and the seasonal monsoon reversal in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: if a candle is not available, a simple alternative is a jar of hot water coloured with food dye placed in a clear container of cold water — the warm coloured water rises visibly. A hand-drawn map on the board is sufficient if a printed map is unavailable.
- The map work is important: learners should leave the lesson able to point to Lake Turkana, the Ngong Hills, and the coast on a map of Kenya. These locations anchor the whole unit — the windmill and anemometer builds in Lessons 302–313 depend on understanding where wind comes from and why some sites are windier than others.
- Avoid introducing the Coriolis effect in detail — it is mentioned only to dismiss the misconception that rotation alone causes wind. The focus is convection and pressure differences.
