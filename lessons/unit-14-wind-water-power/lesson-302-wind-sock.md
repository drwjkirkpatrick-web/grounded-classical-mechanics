# Lesson 302: Build: A Wind Sock and the Beaufort Scale

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-4.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** wind-sock

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a wind sock from a plastic bag, a wire ring, and a pole, and install it so it pivots to show wind direction.
2. Read the wind sock to determine the direction the wind is coming from and going to.
3. Estimate wind strength using a simplified Beaufort scale picture card (calm to gale).
4. Record at least one observation of wind direction and estimated Beaufort number on a log sheet.

## Materials
- One large plastic bag (shopping bag or rubbish bag) per group
- A wire ring or stiff wire bent into a circle about 15–20 cm diameter (a coat hanger works)
- A pole or stick about 1–1.5 m long (broom handle, bamboo, or straight branch)
- Adhesive tape or string for fastening
- Scissors (teacher-supervised)
- A printed or hand-drawn Beaufort scale picture card (0 = smoke rises vertically, 1 = smoke drifts, 2 = leaves rustle, 3 = small flags flutter, 4 = small branches move, 5 = small trees sway, 6 = large branches move)
- Compass or phone compass app
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **wind sock** (soksi ya upepo) — a fabric or plastic tube mounted on a pole that shows wind direction and gives a rough estimate of wind strength
- **wind direction** (mwelekeo wa upepo) — the compass direction the wind is coming *from*
- **Beaufort scale** (kiwango cha Beaufort) — a numbered scale from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane) that estimates wind strength from visible effects
- **prevailing wind** (upepo unaopenda) — the direction the wind most often blows from in a place
- **compass** (dira) — an instrument that shows north, south, east, and west

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A wind sock points with the open end facing the wind. Which way is the wind blowing *to*?
2. Write down one visible sign that tells you the wind is Beaufort 3 or stronger.
3. Why is it useful to know the prevailing wind direction before building a windmill?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The wind blows *into* the open end and *out* through the narrower end, so the sock points in the direction the wind is going *to*. The wind is coming from the opposite direction (the open end faces the source).
2. Any visible sign at Beaufort 3 or above: small flags flutter, leaves are in constant motion, small branches move, small trees sway, or large branches move. Accept any correct sign from the scale.
3. A windmill must face the prevailing wind to capture the most energy. If you build it facing the wrong direction, it will spend much of the time idle or working poorly.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the class a picture or sketch of a wind sock at an airport or a wind farm. Ask: *What does this tell a pilot or an engineer?* Board the answers: direction and rough strength. Tell learners that today they will build their own wind sock and learn to read wind strength without any instrument — just by looking.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners build the wind sock. Cut the plastic bag into a tube about 30 cm long, open at both ends. Tape one end around the wire ring so it stays open. Tie or tape the ring to the top of the pole so the sock can pivot freely. Take the wind socks outside. Each group plants the pole in the ground or holds it upright. They use the compass to find north, then read the direction the sock points and record the Beaufort number from the picture card. They rotate the sock to confirm it always swings back to the same direction. Each group records three readings over 5 minutes.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — back in class, consolidate on the board. Draw a compass rose and mark each group's wind direction. Confront the misconception that *the sock points where the wind comes from* — it actually points where the wind goes *to*; the open mouth faces the source. Show the Beaufort scale card and match each number to the visible signs the groups observed. Ask: *If we wanted to build a windmill here, which way should it face?* The answer is the prevailing direction — the one most readings showed.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a wind sock shows direction and a rough strength; the Beaufort scale turns what you see into a number.* Homework: keep a one-week log of wind direction and Beaufort number at the same time each day (for example, at 4 pm). Bring the log to the next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Wind Sock
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- One large plastic bag per group
- Wire ring or bent coat hanger (15–20 cm diameter)
- Pole or stick 1–1.5 m long
- Adhesive tape or string
- Scissors (teacher-supervised)
- Compass or phone compass app
- Beaufort scale picture card

### Workflow
1. Cut the plastic bag into a tube about 30 cm long, open at both ends. **Checkpoint:** the tube is open at both ends and wide enough to let air pass through.
2. Tape one end of the tube around the wire ring so the opening stays round and stiff. **Checkpoint:** the ring holds the mouth open and the plastic is secure — it should not slip off when held up.
3. Tie or tape the ring to the top of the pole. The sock must be able to pivot and swing freely — do not fix it rigidly in one direction. **Checkpoint:** when you blow on the open end, the sock swings around to point away from you.
4. Take the wind sock outside. Plant the pole in soft ground or have one learner hold it upright. Use the compass to find north. **Checkpoint:** everyone in the group knows which way is north.
5. Watch the sock for 30 seconds. Record the compass direction the sock points *to* (the narrow end). The wind is coming from the opposite direction. Record the Beaufort number by comparing the visible signs (leaves, flags, branches) to the picture card. **Checkpoint:** both direction and Beaufort number are written down with the time.
6. Repeat the reading every 2 minutes for three readings total. Note whether the direction changes between readings. **Checkpoint:** three readings are logged with time, direction, and Beaufort number.

### Common Mistakes
- **Fixing the sock rigidly to the pole** — the sock must pivot freely. If it is taped tight, it cannot swing to show the wind direction. Test by blowing on it before going outside.
- **Reading the wrong end** — the narrow end points where the wind goes *to*; the open mouth faces where the wind comes *from*. If you say "the wind is coming from the east" when the sock points east, you have it backwards.
- **Not using the compass** — guessing the direction by the sun or by a landmark is unreliable. Always check north with the compass first.
- **Placing the sock behind a wall or tree** — buildings and trees block and redirect the wind. Place the sock in the open, away from obstacles, for a true reading.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A wind sock points with the open end facing the wind. Which way is the wind blowing *to*? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Write down one visible sign that tells you the wind is Beaufort 3 or stronger. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why is it useful to know the prevailing wind direction before building a windmill? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your wind sock points north. Where is the wind coming from, and where is it going?
5. Challenge: over five readings your sock points north three times and east twice. What is the prevailing wind direction, and how would you describe its reliability?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The wind blows into the open end and out through the narrow end, so the sock points in the direction the wind is going *to*.
2. Any correct sign at Beaufort 3 or above: small flags flutter, leaves in constant motion, small branches move, small trees sway, large branches move. Accept any correct sign.
3. A windmill must face the prevailing wind to capture the most energy. Facing the wrong direction means it works poorly or not at all for much of the time.
4. The wind is going *to* the north and coming *from* the south. The open mouth of the sock faces south.
5. The prevailing wind direction is *from the south* (since the sock points north most often). It is moderately reliable — 3 out of 5 readings agree, but 2 out of 5 differ, so the wind is somewhat variable at this site. A windmill here should face south but should ideally be able to yaw (turn) to follow shifts.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** Over a week you log the following wind directions (the direction the wind comes *from*): N, N, NE, N, S, N, NE, N, S, N, E, N, NE, N. Represent these as a simple vector tally and determine the prevailing direction. What fraction of readings agrees with the prevailing direction?
   **Worked Answer:** Count each direction: N = 8, NE = 3, S = 2, E = 1. The prevailing direction is N (from the north), with 8 out of 14 readings. Fraction = 8/14 = 4/7 ≈ 0.57, so about 57% of readings agree. A simple vector tally treats each reading as a unit vector in the direction the wind comes from; the resultant points predominantly from the north, confirming N as prevailing.
2. **Q:** A wind sock at an airfield is 60 cm long and inflates fully at a wind speed of about 5 m/s (Beaufort 3–4). If the sock is only half inflated, estimate the wind speed and state what Beaufort number you would record.
   **Worked Answer:** The inflation of a wind sock is roughly proportional to wind speed up to full inflation. If the sock is half inflated, the wind speed is approximately half of the full-inflation speed: 5 m/s × 0.5 = 2.5 m/s. At 2.5 m/s the visible signs are leaves rustling and a light flag just stirring — this is Beaufort 2 (light breeze, 1.6–3.3 m/s). Record Beaufort 2.
3. **Q:** Explain why a wind sock is more useful than a fixed flag for determining wind direction at a windmill site, and describe one limitation of the wind sock for measuring wind strength.
   **Worked Answer:** A wind sock pivots freely and aligns itself with the wind, so it shows the direction directly without the observer having to interpret the angle of a flag relative to its pole. A fixed flag can wrap around its pole in light or variable wind, giving no reading at all. One limitation of the wind sock for strength is that it only gives a rough, qualitative estimate — it cannot distinguish between, say, 4 m/s and 6 m/s unless it has been calibrated against a known speed. For precise strength, an anemometer (Lesson 303) is needed.

## Safety Notes
- Scissors are used to cut the plastic bag. Teacher supervises cutting; learners pass scissors handle-first. No running with scissors.
- The wire ring may have sharp ends after cutting. Bend the ends inward or cover them with tape before handling.
- When planting the pole in the ground, check for buried cables or pipes. Do not push the pole hard into hard ground — use a twisting motion or choose soft soil.
- Do not stand the wind sock near power lines or on a rooftop. Keep the pole below head height when carrying it.
- On a very windy day (Beaufort 6 or above), do not hold the pole overhead — a gust could pull it from your hand. Plant it in the ground instead.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have completed Lesson 301 (wind is moving air driven by uneven heating). The concept of wind direction (where the wind comes *from*) is the key idea to reinforce.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the build, reading the sock, and matching visible signs to the Beaufort card. Ages 14–17 do the vector tally, the proportionality argument for sock inflation, and the comparison with a fixed flag in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: a coat hanger or any stiff wire can form the ring. A broom handle, bamboo stick, or straight branch serves as the pole. Any lightweight plastic bag works — the lighter the better, so the sock responds to light winds. The Beaufort card can be hand-drawn on the board if prints are unavailable.
- The one-week homework log feeds directly into Lesson 303 (anemometer build) and the windmill site selection in later lessons. Encourage learners to choose a consistent time of day and the same location for each reading.
- If the wind is calm on the day of the lesson, the sock will hang limp. Use this as a teaching moment: Beaufort 0 means no measurable wind, and even a good windmill cannot produce power in still air. Reschedule the outdoor readings for a windier day if possible.
