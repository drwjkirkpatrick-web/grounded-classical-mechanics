# Lesson 303: Build: A Cup Anemometer

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-4.1; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** anemometer

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a cup anemometer from bottle tops, crossed sticks, and a pencil axle that rotates freely in the wind.
2. Count the number of rotations in a fixed time (30 seconds) and use a reference chart to estimate wind speed.
3. Explain why cup anemometers rotate faster in stronger wind and why one cup on each arm faces the opposite way to the other.
4. Calibrate the anemometer against a known speed and produce a simple turns-to-km/h conversion card.

## Materials
- Four plastic bottle tops of the same size (soda bottle caps work well)
- Two thin sticks or dowels of equal length, about 20–30 cm each (kebab sticks, thin bamboo, or straight wire)
- One pencil with a sharp point
- One straight pin or thin nail to serve as the axle
- A small block of wood or cork as a base (to anchor the pencil)
- Adhesive tape or glue
- A stopwatch (phone or wristwatch)
- A printed or hand-drawn reference chart (turns in 30 s → estimated km/h)
- A phone with slow-motion video (optional, for counting fast rotations)
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **anemometer** (kipimo cha upepo) — an instrument that measures wind speed
- **rotation** (mzunguko) — one complete turn of the cups around the axle
- **axle** (mhimili) — the central pin or shaft about which the cups rotate
- **calibration** (kalibrari) — comparing an instrument's readings to a known standard so you can convert its output to real units
- **rpm** (mzunguko kwa dakika) — rotations per minute, a unit of rotational speed

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What does an anemometer measure?
2. Why must one cup on each arm face the opposite direction to the other?
3. If your anemometer does 60 turns in 30 seconds, what is its rotation rate in turns per minute?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. An anemometer measures wind speed.
2. The wind pushes the open face of one cup forward while it pushes the curved (back) side of the opposite cup, which has much less drag. This imbalance creates a torque that keeps the arms rotating in one direction. If both cups faced the same way, the wind would push both equally and there would be no net rotation.
3. 60 turns in 30 seconds = 120 turns in 60 seconds = 120 rpm.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a bottle top and ask: *Can this rubbish tell us how fast the wind is blowing?* Show a picture or sketch of a real cup anemometer (three or four cups on arms). Tell learners that today they will build one from bottle tops and sticks and use it to put a number on the wind.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners build the anemometer. Cross the two sticks at right angles and tape them together at the centre. Tape one bottle top to each end of the crossed sticks, making sure each cup faces the opposite direction to its partner on the same arm (two cups face clockwise, two face anticlockwise — but arranged so the wind catches them in sequence). Push the pin through the exact centre of the crossed sticks and into the pencil tip so the assembly spins freely. Anchor the pencil in the wood block or cork base. Take the anemometers outside. One learner holds the anemometer at arm's length facing the wind, another runs the stopwatch for 30 seconds, and a third counts the turns (using a phone in slow-motion if the wind is strong). Record the turn count. Repeat twice.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — back in class, consolidate on the board. Write each group's turn count on the board and convert to rpm (turns in 30 s × 2). Use the reference chart to estimate wind speed in km/h. Confront the misconception that *more turns always means a better anemometer* — the number of turns depends on the wind speed, the cup size, the arm length, and friction at the axle. A well-built anemometer has low friction and gives repeatable readings; a sticky axle under-reads. Explain calibration: if you know the real wind speed (from a reference chart or a vehicle test), you can build a turns-to-km/h card for your specific anemometer.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *an anemometer turns wind speed into a count; calibration turns the count into km/h.* Homework: with a parent or teacher, sit in a parked car with the anemometer held out the window on a calm day. Have the driver move at 10 km/h, then 20 km/h, then 30 km/h (in a safe, flat area). Count the turns in 30 seconds at each speed and write them down. This is your calibration data for the next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Cup Anemometer
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Four identical plastic bottle tops
- Two thin sticks or dowels, 20–30 cm each
- One pencil with a sharp point
- One straight pin or thin nail (axle)
- Small wood block or cork (base)
- Adhesive tape or glue
- Stopwatch
- Reference chart (turns in 30 s → km/h)
- Phone with slow-motion video (optional)

### Workflow
1. Cross the two sticks at right angles and tape or glue them together at the centre so they form a plus (+) shape. **Checkpoint:** the crossed sticks are symmetrical — all four arms are the same length from the centre.
2. Tape one bottle top to the end of each arm. Arrange the cups so that each cup faces the opposite direction to its partner on the same stick — the wind should catch the open face of one cup and the curved back of the other. **Checkpoint:** when you blow on one side, the assembly rotates smoothly in one direction; blowing on the opposite side rotates it the same way.
3. Push the pin through the exact centre of the crossed sticks, then push it into the pencil tip so the cup assembly can spin freely on the pencil. **Checkpoint:** spin the cups by hand — they should rotate several times before stopping. If they stop immediately, the pin is too tight or the sticks rub on the pencil. Adjust until it spins freely.
4. Anchor the pencil point-down in the wood block or cork so the anemometer stands upright. **Checkpoint:** the anemometer stands on its own and the cups are at least 30 cm above the base.
5. Take the anemometer outside and face it into the wind. One learner holds it steady at arm's length, another starts the stopwatch for 30 seconds, and a third counts the turns. If the wind is strong and the cups spin too fast to count by eye, record a slow-motion video on a phone and count the turns by replaying. **Checkpoint:** the turn count for 30 seconds is written down with the time and date.
6. Repeat the measurement twice more. If the readings differ a lot, check for a sticky axle or an obstruction. **Checkpoint:** three turn counts are recorded and the readings are reasonably repeatable (within about 10% of each other).
7. Convert the average turn count to rpm (× 2) and use the reference chart to estimate the wind speed in km/h. **Checkpoint:** the estimated wind speed is written on the log sheet.

### Common Mistakes
- **Cups all facing the same way** — if all four cups face the same direction, the wind pushes all of them equally and there is no net torque. The anemometer will not spin. Each pair of cups on the same stick must face opposite directions.
- **Pin too tight or off-centre** — if the pin is not through the exact centre of the crossed sticks, the assembly wobbles and rubs. If it is too tight, friction stops the cups quickly. Reposition the pin at the true centre and loosen it until the cups spin freely.
- **Sticks too heavy or unbalanced** — if one arm is longer or heavier, the anemometer vibrates and gives inconsistent readings. Cut the sticks to equal length and attach the cups at the same distance from the centre on each arm.
- **Holding the anemometer behind your body** — your body blocks the wind. Hold it upwind or to the side at arm's length so the cups catch the full wind.
- **Counting half-turns as full turns** — decide in advance what counts as one full turn (the same cup returning to the top position). Count consistently across all three trials.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What does an anemometer measure? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why must one cup on each arm face the opposite direction to the other? (mirrors pre-test)
3. If your anemometer does 60 turns in 30 seconds, what is its rotation rate in rpm? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your anemometer spins freely by hand but does not turn in a light wind. Name one likely cause and one fix.
5. Challenge: you calibrate your anemometer in a car and get 45 turns in 30 s at 20 km/h. Estimate the wind speed if the same anemometer reads 90 turns in 30 s outdoors.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. An anemometer measures wind speed.
2. The wind pushes the open face of one cup (high drag) and the curved back of the opposite cup (low drag). This imbalance creates a torque that keeps the arms rotating in one direction. If both cups faced the same way, the wind would push both equally and there would be no net rotation.
3. 60 turns in 30 s × 2 = 120 rpm.
4. Likely cause: the axle pin is too tight or the cups are too small for a light wind to overcome friction. Fix: loosen the pin so the assembly spins more freely, or use larger cups (more drag area) to catch more wind.
5. If 45 turns in 30 s corresponds to 20 km/h, then 90 turns in 30 s is double the rotation rate. Assuming a roughly linear relationship between turns and wind speed (valid at low speeds), the wind speed is approximately 2 × 20 = 40 km/h. Note: in reality the relationship is not perfectly linear at higher speeds due to drag and turbulence, but the linear estimate is a reasonable first approximation.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** You calibrate your anemometer by holding it out the window of a parked car driven at steady speeds on a calm day. You record: 10 km/h → 22 turns in 30 s; 20 km/h → 44 turns in 30 s; 30 km/h → 66 turns in 30 s. Plot the relationship and write the calibration equation. If the anemometer reads 55 turns in 30 s outdoors, what is the estimated wind speed?
   **Worked Answer:** The data are linear: 10 km/h → 22 turns, 20 km/h → 44 turns, 30 km/h → 66 turns. The slope is 44/20 = 2.2 turns per (km/h) in 30 s, or equivalently turns = 2.2 × v. Rearranging: v = turns / 2.2. At 55 turns: v = 55 / 2.2 = 25 km/h. The calibration equation is v (km/h) = turns_in_30s / 2.2, or turns_in_30s = 2.2 × v.
2. **Q:** The linear calibration above assumes the cup speed equals the wind speed. In reality, a cup anemometer runs at a fraction of the wind speed (the "anemometer factor," typically about 0.3–0.4 for simple cup anemometers). Explain why the cups cannot keep up with the wind, and why this does not affect the calibration as long as the factor is constant.
   **Worked Answer:** The wind pushes the open face of one cup forward but also pushes the curved back of the opposite cup (which is moving against the wind) backward. The net torque is the difference between the drag on the open cup and the drag on the closed cup, so the cups accelerate until the net torque balances the friction and air drag on the moving cups. At this equilibrium the cup-tip speed is a fixed fraction of the wind speed — the anemometer factor. The cups cannot reach the full wind speed because the opposing cup always creates drag. This does not affect the calibration because the factor is constant: if the cups always run at, say, 0.35 × wind speed, then doubling the wind speed doubles the cup speed, and the turn count is still proportional to the wind speed. The calibration captures this proportionality, so the conversion from turns to km/h remains valid.
3. **Q:** You want to measure the wind speed at a potential windmill site. You take readings at 9 am, 12 noon, and 3 pm for five days and get average turn counts of 30, 75, and 60 turns in 30 s respectively. Using your calibration (turns = 2.2 × v), estimate the wind speed at each time. When is the site windiest, and what does this tell you about when a windmill here would produce the most power?
   **Worked Answer:** Using v = turns / 2.2: at 9 am, v = 30 / 2.2 ≈ 13.6 km/h; at 12 noon, v = 75 / 2.2 ≈ 34.1 km/h; at 3 pm, v = 60 / 2.2 ≈ 27.3 km/h. The site is windiest at midday (about 34 km/h) and least windy in the morning (about 14 km/h). A windmill here would produce the most power around midday when the wind is strongest. Since wind power is proportional to v³, the power at midday is (34.1/13.6)³ ≈ 15.7 times the morning power — a huge difference. This tells us the windmill should be sized and operated to capture the midday peak, and that morning output will be very low.

## Safety Notes
- The pin or thin nail used as the axle is sharp. Teacher supervises the pinning step. Learners push the pin through the sticks, not toward their hand. Store pins in a container when not in use.
- When calibrating with a vehicle (homework), the learner must be a passenger inside the car with the window down, not outside or leaning out. The driver must drive at a steady, safe speed in a flat, clear area with no traffic. The anemometer is held out the window only far enough to catch clean air — not into the path of other vehicles or obstacles.
- Do not hold the anemometer near power lines, fans with exposed blades, or machinery with moving parts that could catch the sticks.
- Bottle tops taped to sticks can fly off if the wind is strong. Check that all cups are securely taped before going outside. If a cup detaches, stop and reattach it.
- The wood block or cork base must be stable so the anemometer does not tip over. If using a cork, ensure the pencil is firmly embedded so it does not fall on a learner's foot.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have completed Lesson 301 (wind is moving air) and Lesson 302 (wind sock and Beaufort scale). The concept of rotation and counting rotations is introduced here; no prior formulae are needed.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the build, counting turns, and using the reference chart. Ages 14–17 do the full calibration, the linear equation, the anemometer factor explanation, and the v³ power argument in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: bottle tops are freely available from used soda or water bottles. Kebab sticks, thin bamboo, or straight wire all work for the arms. A pencil and a straight pin are the only specialised items. The reference chart can be hand-drawn on the board: for a typical bottle-top anemometer with 25 cm arms, roughly 20 turns in 30 s ≈ 10 km/h, 40 turns ≈ 20 km/h, 60 turns ≈ 30 km/h (these are approximate and should be calibrated for each build).
- The homework calibration (counting turns at known vehicle speeds) is the foundation for the turns-to-km/h card used in Lesson 313 (windmill test day) and Lesson 315 (data and graphs). Encourage learners to do this safely with a parent or teacher driving. If a car is not available, a simpler calibration is to compare the anemometer's turn count to the Beaufort estimate from Lesson 302 on a windy day.
- The anemometer built here is reused throughout the windmill project (Lessons 311–316). Store it carefully — label each group's anemometer so the calibration card matches the right instrument.
- If the wind is very light on the lesson day, the anemometer may not turn. Use a fan on low speed indoors as a fallback, or reschedule for a windier day. The build and calibration steps can still be completed indoors.
