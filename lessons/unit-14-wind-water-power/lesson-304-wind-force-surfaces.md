# Lesson 304: Wind Force on Surfaces

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe how wind exerts a force (push) on a surface and how that force changes when the surface area increases.
2. Measure and compare the wind force on a card held at different angles to the wind (flat, angled, edge-on) using a spring balance.
3. State that wind force grows with the square of wind speed — doubling the speed roughly quadruples the force.
4. Explain how sail and blade angle affect the force captured, relating this to the design of windmill blades.

## Materials
- Stiff cards of two sizes (for example, 10 cm × 10 cm and 10 cm × 20 cm) per group
- A spring balance (0–5 N range) or a simple elastic band scale made from a rubber band and a ruler
- A fan (table fan) or access to a windy outdoor area
- A protractor or a hand-drawn angle card (0°, 30°, 45°, 90°)
- A ruler or tape measure
- Sticky tape or a clip to attach the card to the spring balance hook
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **drag** (kokotzo) — the force of the wind pushing against a surface
- **surface area** (eneo la uso) — the area of the object exposed to the wind, in square metres
- **angle of attack** (pembe ya shambulio) — the angle between the surface and the oncoming wind
- **force** (nguvu) — a push or pull, measured in newtons (N)
- **speed squared** (kasi ya mraba) — the wind speed multiplied by itself (v²); force is proportional to v²

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you double the area of a card facing the wind, what happens to the wind force on it?
2. If you double the wind speed, does the force double, triple, or quadruple?
3. Hold a card edge-on (thin side) to the wind versus flat-on. Which feels more force, and why?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The force roughly doubles — wind force is proportional to the exposed area. Twice the area means twice the push.
2. The force quadruples — wind force is proportional to the speed squared (v²). Doubling v gives (2v)² = 4v², so four times the force.
3. The flat-on card feels much more force because the wind hits a large area. The edge-on card presents only its thin edge to the wind, so the exposed area (and therefore the force) is much smaller.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold a small card and a large card in front of the fan (or in the wind). Ask: *Which one is harder to hold?* Let a learner try both and report. Then hold a card flat to the wind and then tilt it to edge-on. Ask: *What changed?* Board the two key ideas: force grows with area, and force depends on the angle.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners set up the spring balance. They clip or tape the 10 cm × 10 cm card to the hook. They hold the card flat-on (90°) to the fan at a fixed distance (for example, 30 cm from the fan) and read the force on the spring balance. Then they tilt the card to 45°, then to edge-on (0°), reading the force each time. They repeat with the 10 cm × 20 cm card (double the area) at 90°. They record all readings in a table: card size | angle | force (N). If outside, they use the wind and repeat on a gust versus a lull to feel the effect of speed.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Write the readings in a table. Compare flat-on vs edge-on: force drops sharply as the angle decreases because the effective area shrinks. Compare small card vs large card at 90°: force roughly doubles when area doubles. Confront the misconception that *force just doubles when wind speed doubles* — write F ∝ v² on the board and show that doubling speed gives 4× the force. Explain that a windmill blade is like the card: it is set at an angle (angle of attack) to catch the wind. Too flat (edge-on) and it catches nothing; too square (flat-on) and it just stalls. The best angle is somewhere in between — about 5–15° for a real blade — where the wind flows over the blade and creates lift, not just drag.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *wind force grows with area and with speed squared; blade angle controls how much force you capture.* Homework: look at a billboard or a signboard near your home. Estimate its area in square metres (length × height). Write down: if a storm wind of 30 m/s hit it, how many times stronger would the force be compared to a 15 m/s breeze? (Hint: use v².)

## Hands-On Build: Wind Force on Cards
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Stiff cards: one 10 cm × 10 cm and one 10 cm × 20 cm per group
- Spring balance (0–5 N) or rubber band + ruler
- Fan or windy outdoor area
- Protractor or hand-drawn angle card (0°, 45°, 90°)
- Tape or clip to attach card to spring balance hook
- Ruler or tape measure

### Workflow
1. Clip or tape the 10 cm × 10 cm card to the spring balance hook so the card is held firmly. **Checkpoint:** the card is securely attached and does not slip or bend excessively when the wind hits it.
2. Place the fan at a fixed position. Hold the card flat-on (90° to the wind) at exactly 30 cm from the fan grille. Read and record the force on the spring balance. **Checkpoint:** the distance is measured from the fan grille to the card; the card is perpendicular to the airflow.
3. Tilt the card to 45° using the protractor or angle card. Hold it at the same 30 cm distance. Read and record the force. **Checkpoint:** the angle is measured between the card face and the wind direction; the distance is unchanged.
4. Tilt the card to edge-on (0°, thin side facing the wind). Read and record the force. **Checkpoint:** the card is truly edge-on — only the thin edge faces the wind.
5. Replace the small card with the 10 cm × 20 cm card. Hold it flat-on (90°) at the same 30 cm distance. Read and record the force. **Checkpoint:** the large card is at the same distance and angle as the small card in step 2.
6. Record all readings in a table: card size | angle | force (N). Compare: flat vs 45° vs edge-on for the small card; small flat vs large flat at 90°. **Checkpoint:** all five readings are recorded with units (N) in the table.

### Common Mistakes
- **Moving the card closer or farther between angles** — the distance from the fan changes the wind speed at the card. Measure from the fan grille to the card and keep it the same for every reading.
- **Measuring the angle from the wrong reference** — the angle of attack is between the card *face* and the wind direction, not between the card edge and the wind. At 90° the card is flat-on (maximum force); at 0° it is edge-on (minimum force).
- **The spring balance is not zeroed** — check that the spring balance reads zero before attaching the card. If it reads above zero with nothing attached, subtract the zero offset from every reading.
- **The card bends in the wind** — a floppy card changes its effective angle. Use stiff card or tape a stick along one edge for rigidity.
- **Comparing different fan speeds** — if using a fan with multiple speed settings, keep the speed the same for all readings. If outside, take all readings in the same wind condition (same gust or lull) or note that the wind changed between readings.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. If you double the area of a card facing the wind, what happens to the wind force on it? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If you double the wind speed, does the force double, triple, or quadruple? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Hold a card edge-on to the wind versus flat-on. Which feels more force, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
4. You measure 2 N on a card at 90°. What force would you expect at 45° — more, less, or the same? Explain why.
5. Challenge: a windmill blade is set at 0° (edge-on). What happens to the force and the rotation, and how would you fix it?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The force roughly doubles — wind force is proportional to the exposed area. Twice the area means twice the push.
2. The force quadruples — wind force is proportional to v². Doubling v gives (2v)² = 4v², so four times the force.
3. The flat-on card feels much more force because the wind hits a large area. The edge-on card presents only its thin edge, so the exposed area and the force are much smaller.
4. Less than 2 N. At 45° the effective area presented to the wind is reduced (the card is partly angled away), so the force is less than at 90°. In practice it is roughly 50–70% of the flat-on value, so about 1–1.4 N, depending on the exact geometry.
5. At 0° (edge-on) the blade presents only its thin edge to the wind, so the force is nearly zero and the rotor does not turn. To fix it, increase the angle of attack so the blade faces the wind at a small angle (about 5–15° for a lift-type blade). This allows the wind to flow over the blade surface and create a force that turns the rotor.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** Use the drag formula F ≈ ½ρAv² to estimate the wind force on a 1 m² signboard facing the wind at 15 m/s. Take the density of air ρ = 1.2 kg/m³ and the drag coefficient C_d = 1.2 for a flat plate. Express the answer in newtons and in kilograms-force (1 kgf ≈ 9.8 N).
   **Worked Answer:** F = ½ × ρ × A × v² × C_d = 0.5 × 1.2 × 1 × (15)² × 1.2 = 0.5 × 1.2 × 1 × 225 × 1.2 = 0.5 × 1.2 × 225 × 1.2 = 0.5 × 324 = 162 N. In kgf: 162 / 9.8 ≈ 16.5 kgf. So the wind pushes on the signboard with about 162 N, equivalent to the weight of a 16.5 kg bag of maize.
2. **Q:** If the wind speed doubles from 15 m/s to 30 m/s, what is the new force on the same 1 m² signboard? Why do billboards fail in storms? Use the formula to justify your answer.
   **Worked Answer:** Since F ∝ v², doubling the speed quadruples the force: F_new = 162 × (30/15)² = 162 × 4 = 648 N ≈ 66 kgf. At 30 m/s the signboard feels about 648 N — the weight of a 66 kg person. Billboards fail in storms because the force grows as the square of the wind speed: a storm at 30 m/s produces four times the force of a 15 m/s breeze on the same board. If the supports were designed for moderate winds, the storm force can exceed the breaking strength of the bolts, poles, or welds, causing the board to tear off or the pole to buckle.
3. **Q:** A windmill blade has a swept area of 0.5 m² and operates at a wind speed of 6 m/s. The blade is set at an angle of attack of 10° rather than 90°, so it captures only about 30% of the drag force a flat plate would feel. Estimate the force on the blade using F ≈ ½ρAv² with ρ = 1.2 kg/m³ and C_d = 1.2, then apply the 30% factor. If the wind speed drops to 3 m/s, what is the new force, and what does this tell you about why windmills need a minimum (cut-in) wind speed?
   **Worked Answer:** First compute the flat-plate force at 6 m/s: F_flat = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.5 × (6)² × 1.2 = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.5 × 36 × 1.2 = 0.5 × 0.5 × 36 × 1.44 = 0.25 × 36 × 1.44 = 12.96 N. Apply the 30% factor for the blade angle: F_blade = 0.30 × 12.96 ≈ 3.9 N. At 3 m/s: F_flat = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.5 × (3)² × 1.2 = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.5 × 9 × 1.2 = 0.25 × 9 × 1.44 = 3.24 N. Apply 30%: F_blade = 0.30 × 3.24 ≈ 0.97 N. So the force drops from about 3.9 N to about 1.0 N — a factor of 4, because halving the wind speed gives ¼ the force. At 3 m/s the blade force (≈ 1 N) may not be enough to overcome bearing friction and the load of the pump, so the rotor stalls. This is why windmills have a cut-in speed: below about 3–4 m/s the available force is too small to overcome friction and start the rotor turning.

## Safety Notes
- The fan has rotating blades. Keep fingers, cards, and loose clothing away from the fan grille. Do not insert anything through the grille. Turn the fan off before attaching or removing cards.
- The spring balance hook can scratch. Attach the card carefully and do not pull the hook toward your face.
- If working outside on a windy day, stand firmly — a strong gust can push the card and the learner off balance. Do not hold the card overhead in strong wind.
- Cards held in strong wind can snap or fly off if the tape fails. Check all attachments before starting. Stand to the side of the card, not behind it, so a flying card does not hit you.
- Do not use the fan near water. Keep the fan on a dry, stable surface.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have completed Lessons 301–303 (wind origin, wind sock, anemometer). The concept of force and newtons is from Unit 2 (forces in everyday life); area and simple proportions are from mathematics.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the build, feeling the push, and comparing flat vs edge-on and small vs large. Ages 14–17 do the full drag formula calculation, the v² scaling, and the cut-in speed argument in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: a spring balance may not be available in every school. A simple substitute is a rubber band stretched between a fixed point and the card — measure the stretch distance with a ruler and compare readings. A table fan is ideal for indoor work; if no fan is available, do the activity outside on a windy day and compare readings across gusts and lulls. Stiff cards from old boxes or cardboard packaging work well.
- The angle-of-attack concept introduced here is the foundation for the windmill blade design in Lessons 309–312. Do not go deep into lift vs drag — the key takeaway for younger learners is that the blade must be angled (not flat, not edge-on) to catch the wind and turn.
- The v² relationship is the most important quantitative idea in this lesson. Reinforce it with the billboard homework: a 30 m/s storm wind gives 4× the force of a 15 m/s breeze (since (30/15)² = 4). This is why billboards and roofs fail in storms and why windmill towers must be engineered for the worst-case wind, not the average.
- If a spring balance is unavailable and the rubber band substitute is used, note that the rubber band itself is non-linear (it stretches more per newton at higher loads). The comparisons (more vs less, double vs single) still hold qualitatively, but absolute force values should not be quoted. For the Advanced Questions, use the formula directly rather than measured values.
