# Lesson 305: Windmills of East Africa: Kijito and Kamkwamba

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-4.1; G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe two real wind-power stories from East Africa: the Kijito wind pumps of Kenya and William Kamkwamba's windmill in Malawi.
2. Explain what makes a good wind-pump site (steady wind, open exposure, access for maintenance).
3. Compare a wind pump with a solar pump for a borehole, considering energy-source pattern vs water-demand pattern.
4. Identify the mechanical chain: wind → rotor → crank → pump rod → water.

## Materials
- Story cards or printed photos (Kijito pump, Kamkwamba windmill — teacher prepares)
- Map of Kenya showing windy regions (Turkana, Ngong, Marsabit)
- A world map or globe (for Malawi)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **wind pump** (pampu ya upepo) — a windmill that drives a water pump instead of making electricity
- **wind site** (eneo la upepo) — a location with steady, strong wind; ideally open, elevated, and free of trees or buildings
- **exposure** (mwonekano) — how open the site is to the wind; no hills, trees, or buildings blocking the prevailing direction
- **borehole** (kisima) — a deep vertical well drilled to reach groundwater

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What does a wind pump do that a wind turbine does not?
2. Name one place in Kenya known for strong steady winds.
3. Why is an open hilltop better for a windmill than a sheltered valley?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A wind pump drives a mechanical water pump (lifting water from a well or borehole). A wind turbine drives a generator to make electricity. Both use the same rotor-and-blade principle, but the output is different: water vs electricity.
2. Any correct answer: Lake Turkana corridor, Ngong Hills, Marsabit, the coast (monsoon winds).
3. An open hilltop has unobstructed wind — no trees, buildings, or hills to slow or deflect it. A sheltered valley blocks the wind, reducing speed and power (P ∝ v³, so even a small reduction in speed means a large loss of power).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show a photo of a Kijito wind pump. Ask: *what do you think this does?* Many learners will say "makes electricity." Reveal: it pumps water from a borehole — no electricity at all, just wind turning a crank that lifts water. Board: **wind does not have to make electricity — it can pump water directly.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — two stories. **Story 1: Kijito Windpumps.** Kenya's Kijito (now Kijito Energy) has manufactured and installed wind pumps across East Africa since the 1980s. The multi-blade rotor (typically 12–20 blades) turns slowly but with high torque — ideal for driving a piston pump. The pumps serve farms, missions, and rural communities where the grid does not reach. **Story 2: William Kamkwamba.** In 2002, at age 14, William built a windmill from scrap (PVC pipe blades, a bicycle frame, a tractor fan, and a dynamo) in his village in Malawi after seeing a picture in a library book. His first windmill powered one light bulb; later versions pumped water for irrigation and charged phones. He was eventually invited to speak at TED. Both stories show the same principle: wind → rotor → mechanical work. The Kijito pump is engineered and manufactured; Kamkwamba's was jua kali — built from what was available. Both work. Learners discuss in pairs: what makes a good wind site? (steady wind, open exposure, access for maintenance, close to the water source).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The mechanical chain: wind → rotor blades → rotor shaft → crank (converts rotation to up-down) → pump rod → piston → water lifted. A good site needs: (1) steady wind above 3 m/s for several hours per day, (2) open exposure — no obstacles within 100 m upwind, (3) height — wind is faster at 10 m than at 2 m, (4) close to the water source (short pump rod = less friction), (5) access for maintenance (oiling bearings, replacing blades). Confront the misconception that "wind pumps are old-fashioned" — they are still the most cost-effective off-grid water pumping solution in windy regions. Solar pumps are simpler and more reliable in most of Kenya, but in the Turkana corridor and highland ridges, wind pumps still win. Also address: "more blades = more power" — not exactly. More blades give more starting torque (good for pumps that need a strong push to start) but less top speed (bad for generators). A 12-blade Kijito rotor is optimized for torque; a 3-blade wind turbine is optimized for speed.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *wind can pump water directly — no electricity needed. Kijito and Kamkwamba prove it works at every scale.* Homework: on the map of Kenya, mark three sites where a wind pump might work and one site where a solar pump would be better.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What does a wind pump do that a wind turbine does not? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Name one place in Kenya known for strong steady winds. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why is an open hilltop better for a windmill than a sheltered valley? (mirrors pre-test)
4. List the mechanical chain from wind to water in a wind pump (five links).
5. Challenge: compare a wind pump and a solar pump for a borehole. Give one advantage of each.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A wind pump drives a mechanical water pump; a wind turbine drives a generator to make electricity.
2. Any correct answer: Lake Turkana, Ngong Hills, Marsabit, the coast.
3. Open hilltop = unobstructed wind; sheltered valley = blocked wind, lower speed, much less power (P ∝ v³).
4. Wind → rotor blades → rotor shaft → crank → pump rod → piston → water. (Any correct ordering of these five links.)
5. Wind pump advantage: works at night and in early morning (when solar is off); provides high mechanical torque for pumping. Solar pump advantage: more predictable in most of Kenya (sunshine is more reliable than wind); no moving parts in the panel; easier to maintain; silent.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A Kijito wind pump has a 3 m rotor diameter and operates at 15% efficiency in a 5 m/s wind. How much wind power is available, and how much is converted to useful pumping power? (ρ_air = 1.2 kg/m³.)
   **Worked Answer:** Rotor area: A = π × (1.5)² = 7.07 m². Wind power: P_wind = ½ρAv³ = 0.5 × 1.2 × 7.07 × 5³ = 0.5 × 1.2 × 7.07 × 125 = 530 W. Useful power: P_useful = 530 × 0.15 = 79.5 W ≈ 80 W. That's enough to lift about 0.8 L/s from a 10 m borehole (P = ρgQh → Q = 80 / (1000 × 10 × 10) = 0.0008 m³/s = 0.8 L/s). In 6 hours of wind: 0.8 × 6 × 3600 = 17,280 L ≈ 17 m³ — enough for a large farm.
2. **Q:** Compare a wind pump and a solar pump for a borehole needing 5000 L/day. The wind pump costs KSh 80,000 and delivers 5000 L/day in the windy season (3 months) but only 2000 L/day in the calm season (9 months). The solar pump costs KSh 120,000 and delivers 6000 L/day year-round. Which has a shorter payback if the alternative is a diesel pump costing KSh 50,000/year in fuel?
   **Worked Answer:** Wind pump: in the calm season, the shortfall is 5000 − 2000 = 3000 L/day. This must be covered by the diesel pump for 9 months: diesel cost = 50,000 × (9/12) = KSh 37,500/year. So the wind pump saves 50,000 − 37,500 = KSh 12,500/year. Payback = 80,000 / 12,500 = 6.4 years. Solar pump: delivers 6000 L/day year-round — no diesel needed at all. Saves KSh 50,000/year. Payback = 120,000 / 50,000 = 2.4 years. The solar pump pays back faster because it eliminates diesel entirely, while the wind pump still needs diesel backup for 9 months. In most of Kenya, solar is the better investment. In the Turkana corridor (steady wind year-round), the wind pump would deliver 5000 L/day every month, with a payback of 80,000 / 50,000 = 1.6 years — faster than solar.
3. **Q:** William Kamkwamba used a bicycle dynamo on his windmill. The dynamo produces 12 V at 0.5 A when spinning at 2000 rpm. His windmill rotor has a diameter of 1.5 m and spins at 300 rpm. What gear ratio is needed, and what is the electrical power output?
   **Worked Answer:** Gear ratio needed: the dynamo needs 2000 rpm but the rotor spins at 300 rpm. Gear ratio = 2000 / 300 = 6.67:1 (speed-up). The dynamo produces 12 V × 0.5 A = 6 W at 2000 rpm. With a 6.67:1 gear-up, the rotor must supply enough torque at 300 rpm to drive the dynamo at 2000 rpm. The power is conserved (minus gear losses): P_rotor = P_dynamo / η_gear. If the gear is 90% efficient: P_rotor = 6 / 0.9 = 6.7 W. The wind must provide 6.7 W at 300 rpm. Check: P_wind = ½ρAv³ with A = π × (0.75)² = 1.77 m². At v = 4 m/s: P_wind = 0.5 × 1.2 × 1.77 × 64 = 68 W. The windmill only needs 6.7 W — plenty of margin. The electrical output is 6 W (12 V, 0.5 A) — enough for one LED bulb or phone charging. This is exactly what William achieved.

## Safety Notes
- No hands-on build in this lesson — safety risks are minimal.
- If showing photos or videos of real wind pumps, ensure the content is age-appropriate.
- Do not attempt to visit a real wind pump site without permission and supervision.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 301–304 introduced wind, its measurement, and force on surfaces. This lesson is the human story — why wind power matters in East Africa.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the stories (Kijito and Kamkwamba) and the idea that wind can pump water. Ages 14–17 should compare wind vs solar pumps quantitatively and discuss site selection.
- The Kamkwamba story is powerful because William was 14 — the same age as many learners. It shows that engineering is not just for adults with factories; it is for anyone with curiosity and determination. The Kijito story shows the industrial side — a Kenyan company manufacturing wind pumps for decades.
- If possible, invite a local fundi who has worked on wind or solar pumps to speak to the class.
- This lesson motivates the windmill build project (Lessons 309–316). The learners are not just building a toy — they are following in the footsteps of Kijito and Kamkwamba.
