# Lesson 306: How a Wind Pump Works: Rotor to Piston

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Trace the energy chain from wind to lifted water in a mechanical wind pump.
2. Explain how a crank (or cam) converts rotational motion into reciprocating (up-and-down) motion.
3. Build a simple crank model from wire and demonstrate the conversion.
4. Relate crank radius to pump stroke length and to the force per stroke.

## Materials
- Stiff wire (coat-hanger wire or fencing wire, ~30 cm per pair)
- Pliers (for bending wire — share between groups)
- A pencil or stick (as the rotor shaft)
- String (as the pump rod)
- A small weight (bottle top with stones, or a nut)
- Cardboard and markers (for labelling the energy chain)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **crank** (kangi) — a bent arm on a shaft that converts rotation into back-and-forth (reciprocating) motion
- **reciprocating** (mwendo wa pande pande) — moving back and forth in a straight line, like a pump rod going up and down
- **stroke** (mwendeko) — the distance the pump piston travels in one direction (up or down); equal to twice the crank radius
- **pump rod** (fimbo ya pampu) — the rod that connects the crank to the piston and transmits the up-and-down force

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A windmill rotor spins round and round. A water pump piston goes up and down. What part converts one to the other?
2. If the crank radius is 5 cm, how far does the pump rod travel in one full stroke (from top to bottom)?
3. Why does a pump rod need a guide (a sleeve or bracket) to keep it from wobbling?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The crank (or cam). The crank is a bent arm on the rotor shaft. As the shaft rotates, the crank pin moves in a circle, and the connecting rod converts this circular motion into straight up-and-down motion.
2. The stroke = 2 × crank radius = 2 × 5 = 10 cm. The pump rod travels 10 cm from the top of the stroke to the bottom.
3. Without a guide, the pump rod wobbles sideways as the crank pushes it up and down. The wobble wastes energy (friction) and can bend the rod or break the pump. A guide (a sleeve or bracket) keeps the rod moving in a straight line, so all the force goes into lifting water.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a pencil and spin it. Ask: *how do we turn this spinning into up-and-down?* Bend the wire into a crank shape (an L or Z bend) and show how the end of the crank traces a circle while a rod attached to it goes up and down. Board: **rotation → crank → reciprocation.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build a crank model (see Hands-On Build). They bend the wire into a crank, attach it to the pencil shaft, and connect a string (pump rod) to the crank pin. They add a weight to the string and turn the crank. They observe: the weight goes up and down as the shaft rotates. They measure: the crank radius, the stroke length, and the number of rotations needed to lift the weight by 10 cm. They draw the energy chain on cardboard: wind → blades → shaft → crank → rod → piston → water.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The crank is the heart of the wind pump: it converts the rotation of the rotor into the reciprocation of the pump rod. The stroke = 2 × crank radius. A longer crank = longer stroke = more water per stroke, but also more force needed (the pump must push a taller column of water). A shorter crank = shorter stroke = less water per stroke, but less force and faster operation. Confront the misconception that "a bigger crank is always better" — a bigger crank means the pump rod travels further per stroke, which means the pump cylinder must be longer, and the force per stroke increases (F = ρgAh, where h is the lift height, not the stroke). The stroke determines the volume per stroke (V = A × stroke), not the force. The force depends on the head (lift height) and the cylinder area. Also address: "the crank is just a bent wire" — yes, but the bend is precisely engineered. The crank pin must be smooth (low friction), the right length (not too long or the rod jams), and balanced (or the rotor wobbles). The guide (sleeve) is essential — without it, the rod swings sideways and energy is lost.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the crank converts spin to push — it is the link between the wind and the water.* Homework: draw a labelled diagram of the wind pump energy chain and write the formula for the volume pumped per stroke.

## Hands-On Build: Wire Crank Model
*Build a simple crank from wire and demonstrate rotation-to-reciprocation conversion.*

### Parts
- Stiff wire (coat-hanger or fencing wire, ~30 cm)
- Pliers (shared)
- A pencil or stick (rotor shaft)
- String (pump rod, ~20 cm)
- A small weight (bottle top with stones, or a nut)
- A ruler
- Cardboard and markers (for the energy-chain diagram)

### Workflow
1. Bend the wire into a crank shape: a straight section (15 cm) along the shaft, then a 5 cm bend perpendicular, then a 2 cm crank pin parallel to the shaft. **Checkpoint:** the crank pin is offset 5 cm from the shaft centre — this is the crank radius.
2. Slide the straight section onto the pencil shaft. Tape it in place so it rotates with the pencil. **Checkpoint:** the crank rotates when the pencil spins.
3. Tie one end of the string to the crank pin. Tie the other end to the weight. **Checkpoint:** the string hangs from the crank pin with the weight at the bottom.
4. Hold the pencil horizontally and turn it slowly. Observe: the weight rises and falls as the crank rotates. **Checkpoint:** the weight moves up and down — reciprocating motion from rotation.
5. Measure: (a) crank radius (5 cm), (b) stroke length (distance from highest to lowest position of the weight = 10 cm = 2 × radius), (c) number of turns to lift the weight 10 cm (1 turn). **Checkpoint:** all three measurements are recorded.
6. Draw the energy chain on cardboard: wind → blades → shaft → crank → rod → piston → water. Label each link with the type of energy (kinetic → mechanical → hydraulic). **Checkpoint:** the diagram is labelled and correct.

### Common Mistakes
- **Crank pin too short** — if the pin is less than 2 cm, the string slips off. Make the pin at least 2 cm long.
- **String too loose** — if the string is slack, the weight doesn't rise on the up-stroke. The string must be taut when the crank is at the bottom of the stroke.
- **No guide on the rod** — without a guide, the string swings in an arc instead of going straight up. Hold the string between two fingers as a guide, or thread it through a small hole in a card.
- **Crank not fixed to the shaft** — if the wire slides on the pencil, the crank doesn't turn. Tape or glue it firmly.
- **Confusing crank radius with stroke** — the crank radius is the offset from the shaft centre to the crank pin. The stroke is twice the crank radius (the full up-and-down travel). Stroke = 2 × radius.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What part converts rotation to reciprocation in a wind pump? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If the crank radius is 5 cm, what is the stroke length? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why does the pump rod need a guide? (mirrors pre-test)
4. A pump cylinder has a bore (diameter) of 3 cm and a stroke of 10 cm. What volume of water is pumped per stroke?
5. Challenge: if the rotor spins at 60 rpm (1 stroke per rotation), what is the flow rate in L/min?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The crank (or cam). It is a bent arm on the rotor shaft that converts circular motion to up-and-down motion.
2. Stroke = 2 × crank radius = 2 × 5 = 10 cm.
3. Without a guide, the rod wobbles sideways, wasting energy and potentially bending or breaking the rod. The guide keeps it moving in a straight line.
4. Bore = 3 cm → radius = 1.5 cm → area = π × (1.5)² = 7.07 cm². Volume = area × stroke = 7.07 × 10 = 70.7 cm³ ≈ 71 mL per stroke. (1 cm³ = 1 mL.)
5. At 60 rpm, there are 60 strokes per minute (one per rotation). Volume per minute = 71 mL × 60 = 4260 mL ≈ 4.3 L/min. (Assuming one pump stroke per rotation and 100% volumetric efficiency — in practice, 50–80% is realistic, so 2–3.4 L/min.)

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A wind pump has a crank radius of 5 cm and a pump bore of 3 cm. The rotor spins at 60 rpm. The pump lifts water from a 10 m borehole. What is the force per stroke, the hydraulic power, and the flow rate? (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s².)
   **Worked Answer:** Bore = 3 cm → radius = 1.5 cm = 0.015 m. Cylinder area = π × (0.015)² = 7.07 × 10⁻⁴ m². Stroke = 2 × 5 = 10 cm = 0.1 m. Force per stroke: the pump must lift a column of water 10 m high. F = pressure × area = (ρgh) × A = (1000 × 10 × 10) × 7.07 × 10⁻⁴ = 100,000 × 7.07 × 10⁻⁴ = 70.7 N. Volume per stroke: V = A × stroke = 7.07 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.1 = 7.07 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = 0.0707 L. Flow rate at 60 rpm: Q = 0.0707 × 60 = 4.24 L/min = 0.0707 L/s = 7.07 × 10⁻⁵ m³/s. Hydraulic power: P = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 7.07 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 = 7.07 W. Check: P = F × (stroke per second) = 70.7 × (0.1 × 1) = 7.07 W (at 1 stroke/s = 60 rpm). The pump delivers about 7 W of hydraulic power — enough to fill a jerrican in about 2.5 minutes (20 L / 4.24 L/min).
2. **Q:** The rotor produces a torque of 5 N·m at 60 rpm. The crank radius is 5 cm. What force does the crank exert on the pump rod, and is it enough to lift the water in Q1?
   **Worked Answer:** Torque = force × crank radius. Force = torque / radius = 5 / 0.05 = 100 N. The crank exerts 100 N on the pump rod. The force needed to lift the water (from Q1) is 70.7 N. Since 100 > 70.7, the rotor has enough torque to drive the pump — with a margin of 29.3 N for friction and losses. The efficiency of the pump: η = useful force / applied force = 70.7 / 100 = 70.7%. This is realistic for a well-maintained piston pump. If the rotor torque drops below 70.7 × 0.05 = 3.54 N·m, the pump stalls. This is the stall torque — below it, the wind is too weak to turn the pump.
3. **Q:** Design a crank for a wind pump that delivers 10 L/min from a 15 m borehole with a rotor speed of 40 rpm. Choose a pump bore and crank radius, and compute the required rotor torque.
   **Worked Answer:** Target: 10 L/min = 0.167 L/s = 1.67 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s. At 40 rpm (0.667 strokes/s), volume per stroke = 1.67 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.667 = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m³ = 250 mL. Choose a pump bore of 4 cm (radius = 2 cm = 0.02 m). Cylinder area = π × (0.02)² = 1.257 × 10⁻³ m² = 12.57 cm². Stroke = V / A = 250 / 12.57 = 19.9 cm ≈ 20 cm. Crank radius = stroke / 2 = 10 cm. Force to lift water from 15 m: F = ρgh × A = 1000 × 10 × 15 × 1.257 × 10⁻³ = 188.5 N. Required torque: τ = F × crank radius = 188.5 × 0.1 = 18.85 N·m. The rotor must produce at least 18.85 N·m at 40 rpm. Check power: P = τ × ω = 18.85 × (40 × 2π/60) = 18.85 × 4.19 = 79 W. The wind must provide 79 W of mechanical power at the rotor. With a 2 m rotor in a 5 m/s wind: P_wind = 0.5 × 1.2 × π × 1 × 125 = 236 W. At 15% efficiency: P_mech = 35.4 W — not enough! The rotor is too small or the wind too weak. A 3 m rotor in 6 m/s wind: P_wind = 0.5 × 1.2 × 7.07 × 216 = 916 W. At 15%: 137 W — enough. The design needs a bigger rotor or stronger wind.

## Safety Notes
- Wire ends are sharp — pliers must be used to bend, not fingers. File or tape the cut ends.
- The weight on the string can swing — keep clear of other learners when turning the crank.
- Pliers can pinch — demonstrate proper grip before distributing.
- Do not use thick steel wire that is hard to bend — coat-hanger wire or 2 mm fencing wire is sufficient.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 305 introduced wind pumps. This lesson explains the mechanism. Lessons 309–312 (windmill project) will apply this to the actual build.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 build the crank model and observe the conversion. Ages 14–17 compute the force, torque, and power relationships.
- The crank model is simple but powerful — it is the same mechanism used in real wind pumps, car engines (crankshaft), and reciprocating saws. The conversion of rotation to reciprocation (and vice versa) is one of the most important mechanical principles in engineering.
- The key insight is that the crank radius determines the stroke, and the stroke determines the volume per stroke. The force depends on the head (lift height) and the cylinder area, not on the crank radius. This separates the two design choices: crank radius controls volume, cylinder bore controls force.
- This lesson directly supports the windmill project (Lessons 309–312), where learners will build the crank and connect it to the pump.
