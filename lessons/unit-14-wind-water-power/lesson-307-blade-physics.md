# Lesson 307: Blade Physics: Angle, Lift and Drag

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain how blade angle (pitch) affects the spin speed and starting torque of a rotor.
2. Compare flat, curved, and angled blades in fan wind and measure the difference in spin speed.
3. Describe why modern wind turbine blades are curved and twisted, not flat.
4. Explain why a blade with too steep a pitch stalls (stops producing lift).

## Materials
- Card stock or stiff plastic (for making blades — 3 sheets per group)
- Scissors
- A test rotor (a pin or nail on a pencil — from Lesson 308's pinwheel or a simple hub)
- A fan (or stand in a windy spot)
- A stopwatch
- A protractor (or a card with 10°, 20°, 30°, 45° angles drawn)
- Tape
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **pitch** (inama) — the angle of the blade relative to the wind; 0° = flat (facing the wind), 90° = edge-on (no catch)
- **lift** (inua) — the force perpendicular to the wind that makes the blade move sideways (and the rotor spin)
- **drag** (kuvuta) — the force parallel to the wind that pushes the blade backwards
- **stall** (anguka) — when the blade angle is too steep and the wind separates from the blade surface, killing the lift

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A flat card faces the wind head-on. Does it spin or just get pushed? Why?
2. If you tilt the card slightly (about 15°), what happens?
3. Why does a curved blade (like a modern wind turbine) outperform a flat one?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. It just gets pushed — the force is directly backwards (drag), with no sideways component (lift). A rotor needs a sideways force to spin. A flat card facing the wind cannot spin a rotor.
2. Tilting the card creates a sideways force component (lift) — the wind now pushes the card both backwards and sideways. The sideways component is what makes the rotor spin. A small tilt (10–20°) gives the best spin for a flat blade.
3. A curved blade keeps the wind flowing smoothly over its surface, maintaining lift even at higher angles. A flat blade causes the wind to separate (break away from the surface) at moderate angles, killing the lift. The curve (airfoil shape) delays stall and produces more lift for the same wind.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold a card flat against the fan. It blows backwards. Now tilt it 15° — it pushes sideways. Ask: *which way does the rotor spin?* Board: **flat = drag only (push); tilted = lift + drag (spin). The angle is everything.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups make three blade types and test them on the rotor (see Hands-On Build). Type 1: flat blade, 0° pitch. Type 2: flat blade, 20° pitch. Type 3: curved blade (pre-curved by bending card over a bottle), 20° pitch. They mount each on the test rotor, hold it in the fan wind, and count turns for 10 seconds. They try 10°, 20°, 30°, and 45° pitch for the flat blade and find the best angle. Results go in a table: blade type | pitch angle | turns in 10 s | observations.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The flat blade at 0° produces only drag (push) — no spin. At 15–25°, the flat blade produces lift and spins well. Above 30°, the wind separates from the flat surface — the blade stalls and the spin drops. The curved blade at 20° spins faster than the flat blade at 20° because the curve keeps the wind attached to the surface, producing more lift. Modern wind turbine blades are curved and twisted: the root (near the hub) has a high pitch (for torque), and the tip has a low pitch (for speed). This is called twist. Confront the misconception that "more angle = more spin" — no, there is an optimum (around 15–25° for flat blades, higher for curved). Beyond the optimum, the blade stalls. Also address: "more blades = more power" — not for speed. More blades give more starting torque (good for pumps) but create turbulence that limits top speed. Three blades is the modern compromise: enough torque to start, low turbulence for high speed.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *tilt the blade to catch the wind sideways — the curve keeps it spinning at angles where a flat blade would stall.* Homework: draw a side view of a flat blade and a curved blade at 30° and show where the wind separates on the flat blade.

## Hands-On Build: Blade Angle Test Rig
*Test flat vs curved vs angled blades on a simple rotor in fan wind.*

### Parts
- Card stock or stiff plastic (3 sheets per group)
- Scissors
- A test rotor (pin or nail on a pencil, or a simple hub from bottle tops)
- A fan (or a windy spot outside)
- A stopwatch
- A protractor (or a card with 10°, 20°, 30°, 45° angles)
- Tape
- A bottle (for pre-curving blades)

### Workflow
1. Cut three identical rectangular blades (8 cm × 3 cm) from card stock. Leave one flat. Bend the second over a bottle to give it a curve (airfoil shape). Leave the third flat for angle testing. **Checkpoint:** three blades are prepared — one flat, one curved, one flat for angle tests.
2. Mount the flat blade on the test rotor at 0° pitch (facing the fan head-on). Hold the rotor in the fan wind. Count turns for 10 seconds. **Checkpoint:** the blade is pushed backwards but does not spin (or spins very slowly). Record the result.
3. Mount the flat blade at 20° pitch (tilted 20° from the wind direction). Hold in the fan. Count turns for 10 seconds. **Checkpoint:** the blade spins. Record the turn count.
4. Mount the curved blade at 20° pitch. Hold in the fan. Count turns for 10 seconds. **Checkpoint:** the curved blade spins faster than the flat blade at the same angle. Record the turn count.
5. Test the flat blade at 10°, 20°, 30°, and 45°. Count turns for 10 seconds at each angle. Find the best angle. **Checkpoint:** the best angle is around 15–25°. Above 30°, the spin drops (stall). Record all results.
6. Fill in the results table: blade type | pitch angle | turns in 10 s | observations (spinning? wobbling? stalled?). **Checkpoint:** the table is complete with all test results.

### Common Mistakes
- **Blade too flexible** — thin card bends in the wind and changes angle. Use stiff card or plastic. Tape a stick along the leading edge for stiffness.
- **Rotor not free to spin** — if the pin is too tight in the pencil, friction stops the rotor. Lubricate with a drop of oil or use a smoother axle.
- **Fan too close** — if the fan is too close, the airflow is turbulent. Stand 50–100 cm from the fan.
- **Not measuring the angle** — "about 20°" is not enough. Use the protractor or the angle card to set the pitch precisely. Small changes in angle make big differences in spin speed.
- **Comparing different wind** — if the fan speed changes between tests, the comparison is invalid. Keep the fan on the same setting throughout.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A flat card faces the wind head-on. Does it spin or just get pushed? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If you tilt the card slightly (about 15°), what happens? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why does a curved blade outperform a flat one? (mirrors pre-test)
4. What happens to a flat blade at 45° pitch, and what is this called?
5. Challenge: why do modern wind turbines use 3 blades, not 12?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. It just gets pushed — the force is all drag (backwards), with no lift (sideways). No spin.
2. Tilting creates a sideways force (lift) — the card now spins. The wind pushes it both backwards and sideways; the sideways component drives the rotation.
3. The curve keeps the wind flowing smoothly over the surface, maintaining lift at higher angles. A flat blade causes the wind to separate at moderate angles, killing the lift. The curve delays stall.
4. The blade stalls — the wind separates from the surface, the lift collapses, and the rotor slows or stops. This is called stall.
5. Three blades is the compromise: enough blade area to produce torque (to start the rotor and drive the generator) but few enough to minimise turbulence (each blade leaves a wake that the next blade hits — more blades = more turbulence = less efficiency). Twelve blades (like a Kijito pump) give high starting torque for a piston pump but low top speed. Three blades give high speed for a generator. The choice depends on the application: pump = many blades, generator = few blades.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A flat blade at 20° pitch produces a lift force of 0.5 N and a drag force of 0.3 N. What is the tangential force (the component that drives the rotor), and what is the axial force (the component that pushes the rotor backwards)? Assume the lift is perpendicular to the blade and the drag is parallel to the wind.
   **Worked Answer:** The blade is at 20° to the wind. Lift is perpendicular to the blade. The tangential force (driving the rotor) is the component of lift in the direction of rotation. For a blade moving in the plane of rotation: F_tangential = L × sin(20°) = 0.5 × 0.342 = 0.171 N. The axial force (pushing the rotor backwards, away from the wind) is: F_axial = L × cos(20°) + D = 0.5 × 0.940 + 0.3 = 0.470 + 0.3 = 0.770 N. The tangential force (0.171 N) is what drives the rotor. The axial force (0.770 N) pushes on the tower and bearing — it does not contribute to rotation. The ratio of tangential to axial (0.171/0.770 = 0.22) shows why wind turbine blades are not very efficient at converting wind force to rotation — most of the force is axial (pushing), not tangential (spinning).
2. **Q:** Explain lift on a curved blade using Bernoulli's principle. Why does too-steep a pitch cause stall?
   **Worked Answer:** Bernoulli's principle: faster-moving air has lower pressure. On a curved blade (airfoil), the wind travels faster over the curved (top) surface and slower over the flatter (bottom) surface. The pressure difference (low on top, high on bottom) creates a net upward force — lift. This is the same principle that keeps an aeroplane wing working. At moderate pitch angles (10–25°), the wind follows the curve smoothly, and the lift is strong. At steep pitch angles (above 30° for a flat blade, higher for a curved one), the wind cannot follow the curve — it separates from the surface, creating a turbulent wake. When the flow separates, the pressure difference collapses, and the lift drops suddenly. This is stall. The blade still has drag (pushed by the wind) but no lift (no spin). Stall is the wind-turbine equivalent of an aeroplane wing stalling at too high an angle of attack.
3. **Q:** A 3-blade rotor has a diameter of 2 m and operates at 15° blade pitch. The lift coefficient is 0.8 and the drag coefficient is 0.15. In a 6 m/s wind, what is the tangential force per blade, the total torque at the hub (radius 0.05 m), and the rotational speed if the rotor turns at a tip-speed ratio of 6? (ρ = 1.2 kg/m³.)
   **Worked Answer:** Blade area (approximate): each blade is 0.9 m × 0.1 m = 0.09 m². Dynamic pressure: q = ½ρv² = 0.5 × 1.2 × 36 = 21.6 Pa. Lift per blade: L = q × C_L × A = 21.6 × 0.8 × 0.09 = 1.56 N. Drag per blade: D = q × C_D × A = 21.6 × 0.15 × 0.09 = 0.292 N. At 15° pitch, tangential force ≈ L × sin(15°) = 1.56 × 0.259 = 0.404 N. Total tangential force (3 blades): 1.21 N. Torque at hub: τ = F_tangential × R = 1.21 × 1.0 (using the rotor radius, not the hub radius, because the force acts at the blade centre of pressure, roughly at 0.7R = 0.7 m). τ ≈ 1.21 × 0.7 = 0.847 N·m. Tip-speed ratio (TSR) = 6: v_tip = TSR × v_wind = 6 × 6 = 36 m/s. Rotational speed: ω = v_tip / R = 36 / 1 = 36 rad/s. RPM = 36 × 60 / (2π) = 344 rpm. Power: P = τ × ω = 0.847 × 36 = 30.5 W. Compare with the available wind power: P_wind = ½ρAv³ = 0.5 × 1.2 × π × 1 × 216 = 407 W. Efficiency = 30.5 / 407 = 7.5%. This is low because the simple blade has a low lift coefficient; real airfoils achieve C_L = 1.0–1.5 and efficiencies of 35–45%.

## Safety Notes
- Scissors: supervise cutting. Collect scissors when not in use.
- The rotor can spin fast in strong fan wind — keep fingers clear of the blades.
- The pin or nail axle is sharp — hold by the pencil, not the pin.
- Do not stand too close to the fan — loose clothing or hair can be caught.
- Card blades can fly off if the tape fails — wear eye protection or stand to the side.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 301–304 introduced wind and force on surfaces. This lesson deepens the blade physics. Lesson 308 (paper pinwheel) applies it to a first rotor build.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 test flat vs curved vs angled and observe the differences. Ages 14–17 should decompose the lift and drag forces, explain Bernoulli, and compute torque and power.
- The key insight is that blade angle (pitch) is the single most important design variable. A small change in pitch (from 15° to 25°) can double or halve the spin speed. This is why real wind turbines have adjustable pitch — they change the angle to optimise for different wind speeds.
- The stall concept is critical: a blade that works well at 20° can stop working at 35°. This is why wind turbines have a "cut-out speed" — in very strong winds, the blades are pitched to stall to protect the turbine.
- This lesson supports the windmill project (Lessons 309–316), where learners will choose and set the blade pitch on their windmill rotor. The data from this lesson informs the design.
