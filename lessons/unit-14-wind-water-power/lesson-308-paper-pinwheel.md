# Lesson 308: Build: The Paper Pinwheel

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a simple pinwheel rotor from paper, a pin, and a pencil.
2. Test different blade counts (2, 4, 6) and compare free-spin speed and loaded lifting ability.
3. Explain why a 3-blade rotor often spins faster than a 6-blade rotor, but a 6-blade rotor has more starting torque.
4. Measure and compare the lifting capacity (thread + beads) of different rotor configurations.

## Materials
- Square paper (A4 cut into squares, or origami paper — 2 squares per learner)
- Pins (1 per learner — sewing pins with round heads)
- Pencils (1 per learner — as the axle/handle)
- Thread or light string
- Small beads or washers (as weights — 5 per group)
- A fan or a windy spot
- A stopwatch
- Scissors
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **pinwheel** (duyuga) — a simple rotor made from paper and a pin; the first wind rotor
- **blade count** (idadi ya pembe) — the number of blades on a rotor; affects both torque and speed
- **starting torque** (nguvu ya kuanza) — the turning force when the rotor is stationary; more blades = more starting torque
- **free spin** (kuzunguka bila mzigo) — spinning with no load; the maximum speed the rotor can reach
- **loaded spin** (kuzunguka na mzigo) — spinning while lifting a weight; the useful work the rotor can do

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A pinwheel with 2 blades and one with 6 blades are held in the same wind. Which spins faster with no load?
2. Which has more starting torque — the 2-blade or the 6-blade pinwheel?
3. Why does adding a weight (load) slow the rotor down?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The 2-blade pinwheel usually spins faster (higher free-spin speed) because fewer blades create less turbulence and less drag on each other. Each blade gets cleaner wind.
2. The 6-blade pinwheel has more starting torque because more blades catch the wind at the same time, providing more force to overcome the initial friction and start the rotor turning.
3. Adding a weight means the rotor must do work (lift the weight) in addition to overcoming its own friction. The energy that goes into lifting the weight is taken from the rotational kinetic energy, so the rotor slows down. If the load is too heavy, the rotor stalls (stops).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a completed pinwheel. Blow on it. It spins. Ask: *how many blades does it have? What happens if we change the number?* Board: **today we build, test 2/4/6 blades, free spin vs loaded.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build pinwheels with different blade counts and test them (see Hands-On Build). They make a 4-blade pinwheel first (standard), then modify to 2 blades, then 6 blades. For each, they measure free-spin turns in 10 seconds (fan or wind), then attach a thread with beads and measure how many beads the rotor can lift. Results go in a table: blade count | free-spin turns/10 s | max beads lifted | observations.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The 2-blade pinwheel spins fastest (free spin) but lifts the fewest beads (low torque). The 6-blade pinwheel spins slowest but lifts the most beads (high torque). The 4-blade is a compromise. This mirrors the real-world choice: wind turbines (generators) use 2–3 blades for speed; wind pumps use 12–20 blades for torque. Confront the misconception that "more blades = more power" — the total power is roughly the same for all blade counts (the wind energy is fixed), but the trade-off is between speed and torque. More blades = more torque, less speed. Fewer blades = less torque, more speed. Power = torque × speed, so the product is similar. Also address: "6 blades should spin faster because more wind is caught" — no, the 6th blade runs in the turbulent wake of the 5th blade, getting less clean wind. The turbulence actually slows the rotor. This is why modern wind turbines use 3 blades — it is the minimum that gives enough torque while keeping turbulence low.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *fewer blades = more speed, more blades = more torque — power is the trade-off between the two.* Homework: make a 3-blade pinwheel at home and compare its free-spin speed with the 4-blade.

## Hands-On Build: Paper Pinwheel with Variable Blade Count
*Build a pinwheel and test how blade count affects speed and torque.*

### Parts
- Square paper (2 squares per learner)
- Pins (1 per learner)
- Pencils (1 per learner)
- Thread or light string
- Small beads or washers (5 per group)
- A fan or a windy spot
- A stopwatch
- Scissors
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. **Build a 4-blade pinwheel.** Cut a square (15 cm × 15 cm). Cut from each corner towards the centre, stopping 2 cm from the centre. Fold alternating corners to the centre. Pin through the centre and into the pencil eraser. **Checkpoint:** the pinwheel spins freely on the pin.
2. **Test free spin.** Hold the pinwheel in the fan (or wind). Count turns for 10 seconds. Repeat 3 times and average. **Checkpoint:** the free-spin turn count is recorded.
3. **Test loaded spin.** Tie a thread to the pin (just behind the pinwheel). Tie a bead to the other end. Hold the pinwheel in the fan. Does it spin and lift the bead? Add beads one at a time until the rotor stalls. Record the maximum number of beads lifted. **Checkpoint:** the max bead count is recorded.
4. **Make a 2-blade pinwheel.** Take a new square. Cut only two opposite corners to the centre. Fold to the centre. Pin and mount. **Checkpoint:** the 2-blade pinwheel spins freely.
5. **Test the 2-blade.** Free-spin turns/10 s and max beads. Record. **Checkpoint:** results are in the table.
6. **Make a 6-blade pinwheel.** Cut a square into 6 equal sectors (or use a hexagonal template). Fold alternating sectors to the centre. Pin and mount. **Checkpoint:** the 6-blade pinwheel spins freely.
7. **Test the 6-blade.** Free-spin turns/10 s and max beads. Record. **Checkpoint:** results are in the table.
8. **Compare.** Which blade count has the highest free-spin speed? Which lifts the most beads? Is there a trade-off? **Checkpoint:** the comparison table is complete with conclusions.

### Common Mistakes
- **Pin too tight** — if the pin is pushed too far into the eraser, the pinwheel jams. Leave 2–3 mm of play so the pinwheel can spin.
- **Blades folded the wrong way** — all blades must fold in the same direction (all clockwise or all anticlockwise). If one is backwards, it blocks the wind instead of catching it.
- **Paper too thick** — heavy card is hard to fold and adds inertia. Use regular paper or light card.
- **Thread rubbing on the pencil** — if the thread wraps around the pin and rubs on the pencil, friction stops the lift. Use a smooth pin and keep the thread clear.
- **Fan wind too weak** — if the fan is on low, the pinwheel may not spin. Use medium or high, or go outside.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A 2-blade and a 6-blade pinwheel are in the same wind. Which spins faster with no load? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Which has more starting torque — the 2-blade or the 6-blade? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why does adding a weight slow the rotor? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your 4-blade pinwheel lifts 3 beads. Your 2-blade pinwheel lifts only 1. What does this tell you about the trade-off?
5. Challenge: a wind turbine (generator) uses 3 blades and a wind pump uses 12. Why?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The 2-blade pinwheel spins faster (free spin) — fewer blades mean less turbulence and each blade gets cleaner wind.
2. The 6-blade has more starting torque — more blades catch the wind simultaneously, providing more force to start.
3. The weight is a load — the rotor must do work to lift it. The energy comes from the rotational kinetic energy, slowing the rotor. Too much load stalls it.
4. The 2-blade is fast but weak (low torque — lifts only 1 bead). The 4-blade is slower but stronger (higher torque — lifts 3 beads). The trade-off is speed vs torque: more blades = more torque but less speed. The total power (torque × speed) may be similar.
5. A generator needs high speed (to spin the alternator fast) and low starting torque (a generator is easy to start). So 3 blades: fast, low turbulence, enough torque to start. A pump needs high starting torque (a piston pump is hard to start — it must lift water from the first stroke) and does not need high speed (pumps work well at low rpm). So 12 blades: high torque, low speed, easy to start. The application determines the blade count.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A 4-blade pinwheel spins at 300 rpm free-spin and lifts 2 beads (each 2 g) on a 5 cm radius thread. A 2-blade pinwheel spins at 450 rpm but lifts only 1 bead. Compute the power of each and verify that the total power is similar.
   **Worked Answer:** 4-blade: the rotor lifts 2 × 2 g = 4 g = 0.004 kg at 5 cm = 0.05 m radius. Force = mg = 0.004 × 10 = 0.04 N. The thread unwinds at the rotor speed: the circumference per turn = 2π × 0.05 = 0.314 m. At 300 rpm = 5 rps, the lifting speed = 5 × 0.314 = 1.57 m/s. Power = F × v = 0.04 × 1.57 = 0.063 W. 2-blade: lifts 1 × 2 g = 2 g = 0.002 kg. Force = 0.02 N. At 450 rpm = 7.5 rps, lifting speed = 7.5 × 0.314 = 2.36 m/s. Power = 0.02 × 2.36 = 0.047 W. The powers are similar (0.063 vs 0.047 W) — the 4-blade has slightly more, but both are in the same range. The trade-off: the 2-blade is 50% faster but lifts half as much. The total power is roughly constant because the available wind energy is the same for both. (Differences are due to turbulence and friction.)
2. **Q:** Why does a 3-blade rotor often beat a 6-blade rotor for speed but lose on starting torque? Design a test to verify this with your pinwheel.
   **Worked Answer:** Speed: the 3-blade rotor has less turbulence because each blade has more space before the next one, so each blade gets cleaner wind. The 6-blade has blades close together, so each blade runs in the wake of the previous one — the wake is slower and more turbulent, reducing the lift on the next blade. The 3-blade also has less total drag (fewer surfaces) and less inertia (less paper to accelerate). Starting torque: the 6-blade has more blades catching the wind at the same time, so the total tangential force when stationary is higher. The 3-blade has fewer blades, so less total force to overcome the initial static friction. Test: make a 3-blade and a 6-blade pinwheel. (1) Free-spin: count turns/10 s — the 3-blade should be faster. (2) Starting torque: hold the rotor stationary, attach a thread with a bead, release — the 6-blade should lift the bead from stationary more reliably (it starts under load), while the 3-blade may stall. (3) Loaded speed: both lift beads, but the 3-blade lifts at higher speed (faster unwinding) while the 6-blade lifts more beads (higher load capacity).
3. **Q:** A real wind turbine has a tip-speed ratio (TSR) of 6, meaning the blade tips move at 6× the wind speed. If the wind is 8 m/s and the rotor diameter is 80 m, what is the rotational speed in rpm? Why does a high TSR favour fewer blades?
   **Worked Answer:** Tip speed = TSR × v = 6 × 8 = 48 m/s. Rotational speed: ω = v_tip / R = 48 / 40 = 1.2 rad/s. RPM = 1.2 × 60 / (2π) = 11.5 rpm. (Real 80 m turbines at 8 m/s run at about 10–15 rpm — confirmed.) High TSR favours fewer blades because: (1) at high tip speed, the blade moves through the air fast — if the next blade is too close (many blades), it arrives before the wake of the previous blade has cleared, hitting turbulent air. (2) Fewer blades = more spacing = each blade hits clean air. (3) Fewer blades = less total drag (fewer surfaces rubbing the air). (4) Fewer blades = less material = lighter rotor = less inertia. The optimum TSR for 3 blades is 5–7; for 2 blades, 6–10; for many blades (pumps), 1–2. High TSR = few blades = high speed (generators). Low TSR = many blades = high torque (pumps).

## Safety Notes
- Pins are sharp — supervise piercing. Learners pin through the paper and into the eraser, not towards their hand.
- The pinwheel can spin fast — keep it away from faces and eyes.
- Collect pins when not in use — do not leave them on the desk.
- Do not use long pins that protrude far past the eraser — they can scratch.
- Scissors: supervise cutting. Collect when not in use.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 307 (blade physics) introduced pitch, lift, drag, and stall. This lesson applies it to a first build. The pinwheel is the simplest possible rotor — it introduces the concepts that will be scaled up in the windmill project (Lessons 309–316).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 build and test the pinwheels, observing that fewer blades spin faster. Ages 14–17 should compute the power trade-off and explain the turbulence argument.
- The pinwheel is deceptively simple — it is a real rotor with real aerodynamics. The blade count trade-off (speed vs torque) is the same trade-off that wind turbine engineers face. The pinwheel makes it tangible.
- This lesson is a bridge between the blade physics (Lesson 307) and the windmill build (Lessons 309–316). The pinwheel is to the windmill what the paper plane is to the aeroplane — a model that captures the essential physics.
- The homework (3-blade pinwheel) sets up the windmill project, where the rotor will typically have 3–4 blades. Learners who test 3 blades at home will have data to inform their windmill design.
