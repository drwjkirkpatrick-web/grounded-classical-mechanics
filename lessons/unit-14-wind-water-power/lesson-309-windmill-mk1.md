# Lesson 309: Windmill Project I: Rotor and Hub

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** windmill

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a working rotor with 3–4 blades mounted on a hub that spins freely on a bearing.
2. Explain that the rotor extracts energy from the wind by slowing it down and converting its kinetic energy into rotational motion.
3. Balance the rotor so it spins smoothly without wobbling, using counterweights where needed.
4. Describe one source of rotor wobble and state the fix they applied.

## Materials
- Blade material: mabati (corrugated iron) offcuts, stiff plastic from jerricans, or plywood offcuts — 3 or 4 equal pieces, each about 15 cm × 5 cm
- Hub: an old bicycle front hub is ideal; a thick nail or bolt with washers works as a substitute bearing
- Nails, wire, or small bolts for fastening blades to the hub
- A piece of wire or a thin metal rod as the axle
- Pliers, a small hammer, and a file (teacher-supervised)
- A hand drill or heated nail for making holes in mabati or plastic
- A marker pen and a ruler
- A piece of string and a small weight (plumb bob) for balance checks
- Cooking oil or grease for the bearing
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **rotor** (rotaxi) — the spinning part of a windmill that carries the blades and captures the wind's energy
- **hub** (kituo cha mzunguko) — the central piece that holds the blades and rotates on the axle
- **blade** (kisu) — a flat or curved surface that the wind pushes against to turn the rotor
- **bearing** (tegemeo) — the surface the axle turns on; a smooth, oiled bearing reduces friction
- **balance** (usawaziko) — arranging the blades so the rotor's weight is even all around, preventing wobble
- **counterweight** (uzito wa kusawazisha) — a small weight added opposite a heavy blade to make the rotor balance

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What does the rotor of a windmill do?
2. Why should a rotor be balanced — what goes wrong if it is not?
3. Name one material found around a Kenyan home or jua kali workshop that you could use to make a blade.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The rotor is the spinning part that carries the blades and captures the wind's kinetic energy, converting it into rotational motion.
2. An unbalanced rotor wobbles as it spins. The wobble wastes energy, shakes the tower, wears the bearing, and can shake blades loose.
3. Any of: mabati offcuts, stiff plastic from a jerrican or bucket, plywood, thin sheet metal, or even flattened tin cans. Accept any locally available stiff, light material.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a paper pinwheel (from Lesson 308) and blow on it. Ask: *This spins, but could it lift water? What is missing?* Board the learners' ideas and steer toward: stronger blades, a solid hub, and a real bearing. Tell learners that today they build the heart of the windmill — the rotor.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners build the rotor. They mark and cut equal blades, drill or punch holes, fasten the blades to the hub at the same angle, oil the bearing, and spin-test it. The teacher moves between groups checking blade angle, fastening, and bearing smoothness. Each group spins its rotor by hand and watches for wobble.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw the rotor from the front and from the side. Label blade, hub, axle, bearing. Confront the misconception that *more blades always means more power* — a rotor with too many blades blocks the wind for the blades behind it (shading), and the extra weight and friction can slow the rotor. Three or four well-shaped blades usually outperform six. Then draw a balanced vs. an unbalanced rotor and explain how a heavy blade on one side causes wobble, fixed by a counterweight on the opposite side or by trimming the heavy blade.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the rotor captures the wind; a balanced, oiled rotor spins freely and loses less energy.* Homework: at home, find one object that spins on a bearing (bicycle wheel, fan, grinding mill) and write one sentence about what would happen if it were unbalanced.

## Hands-On Build: Rotor and Hub
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Gather parts the day before.*

### Parts
- 3 or 4 blade blanks (mabati offcut, stiff plastic, or plywood), each about 15 cm × 5 cm
- Hub: old bicycle front hub with cone-and-cup bearing, or a thick nail/bolt with two washers
- Axle: a wire or thin rod that fits snugly through the hub and spins freely
- Small nails, wire, or bolts for fastening blades to the hub
- Pliers, small hammer, file, hand drill or heated nail
- Cooking oil or grease
- String and a small weight for a plumb-bob balance check
- Marker pen and ruler

### Workflow
1. Mark each blade blank to the same size (15 cm × 5 cm) and cut it out. File any sharp edges flat. **Checkpoint:** all blades are the same length and width — lay them on top of each other to compare.
2. Mark a hole point on one end of each blade, about 1 cm from the edge, in the same position on every blade. Drill or punch the holes. **Checkpoint:** a nail passes cleanly through every hole.
3. Fasten each blade to the hub using a nail or bolt through the hole. Set every blade at the same angle to the hub face (start with about 15–20° pitch, as in Lesson 307). **Checkpoint:** looking from the front, all blades sit at the same angle and none is twisted backward or forward.
4. Oil the bearing. Slide the axle through the hub and check that the hub spins freely on the axle with a light flick. **Checkpoint:** a gentle flick of a blade keeps the rotor spinning for at least two full turns before it stops.
5. Balance check: hold the axle horizontally and let the rotor hang. If one blade always swings to the bottom, that side is heavier. Add a small counterweight (a nut, a piece of wire, or a fold of mabati) to the opposite blade, or trim the heavy blade until the rotor stays put in any position. **Checkpoint:** the rotor has no preferred "down" — it rests in any orientation when held by the axle.
6. Final spin test: hold the axle and blow hard on the blades from the front. The rotor should spin smoothly with no visible wobble. **Checkpoint:** the rotor spins for several turns and the axle does not jump or rattle in your hand.

### Common Mistakes
- **Blades at different angles** — if one blade is flat and another is pitched, the rotor turns unevenly and captures less wind. Lay a straight edge across two blades to check they match; re-bend the odd one.
- **Bearing too tight or dry** — a dry or over-tightened bearing wastes most of the wind's energy as friction. Oil it and loosen the cones slightly until the hub spins freely with no sideways play.
- **Blades not equal in size** — unequal blades cause imbalance even if the angles match. Re-cut or trim the odd blade to match the others before mounting.
- **Loose fastenings** — a blade that rattles on its nail will work loose and fly off. Crimp the nail or tighten the bolt until the blade cannot move relative to the hub.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What does the rotor of a windmill do? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why should a rotor be balanced — what goes wrong if it is not? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name one material found around a Kenyan home or jua kali workshop that you could use to make a blade. (mirrors pre-test)
4. A group mounts six blades on a small hub and finds the rotor turns slowly. Give one reason why fewer blades might spin faster.
5. Challenge: your rotor always swings so the same blade points down when you hold the axle. How do you fix this, and what is the name of the weight you add?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The rotor is the spinning part that carries the blades and captures the wind's kinetic energy, converting it into rotational motion.
2. An unbalanced rotor wobbles, wasting energy, shaking the tower, wearing the bearing, and risking a blade shaking loose.
3. Any of: mabati offcuts, stiff plastic from a jerrican or bucket, plywood, thin sheet metal, or flattened tin cans. Accept any locally available stiff, light material.
4. With too many blades, each blade sits in the wind-shadow (wake) of the one in front, so less wind reaches it. Extra blades also add weight and friction, slowing the rotor. Three or four well-spaced blades usually spin faster.
5. The heavy blade is pulling that side down. Add a counterweight to the opposite blade, or trim the heavy blade, until the rotor has no preferred position. The weight added is called a counterweight.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A rotor has three blades. One blade has a mass of 40 g at a radius of 0.15 m from the axle; the other two are 35 g each at the same radius. What counterweight mass, placed on the hub directly opposite the heavy blade at the same radius, makes the rotor statically balanced?
   **Worked Answer:** Take moments about the axle. Place the heavy blade at angle 0°. For three blades at 120° spacing, the two 35 g blades sit at 120° and 240°. Their combined mass effect along the 0° axis: each contributes 35 g × cos(120°) = 35 × (−0.5) = −17.5 g, so both together give −17.5 × 2 = −35 g-equivalent along the 0° axis. The heavy blade gives +40 g. Net imbalance along 0° = 40 − 35 = +5 g at 0.15 m. A counterweight directly opposite (at 180°) must provide −5 g-equivalent at the same radius, so its mass is 5 g. Place a 5 g counterweight opposite the heavy blade.
2. **Q:** The rotor spins on a bearing with a friction torque of 0.02 N·m. The blades have a total radius of 0.15 m. Estimate the minimum tangential wind force on the blades needed just to start the rotor turning. If the wind exerts 0.5 N, will the rotor start, and what torque is available to accelerate it?
   **Worked Answer:** Torque to overcome friction = 0.02 N·m. At a radius of 0.15 m, the tangential force needed is τ/r = 0.02 / 0.15 ≈ 0.13 N. The wind exerts 0.5 N, which is greater than 0.13 N, so the rotor starts. The net accelerating torque = (0.5 × 0.15) − 0.02 = 0.075 − 0.02 = 0.055 N·m.
3. **Q:** Explain two sources of dynamic wobble (wobble that appears only when the rotor is spinning, not when it is held still) and state one fix for each.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Aerodynamic imbalance: blades at slightly different pitch angles produce different lift/drag forces at speed, even if the masses match. Fix: re-set every blade to the same pitch using a template gauge. (b) Mass distribution off the plane of rotation: if a blade's mass is not in the same plane as the others (e.g., one blade bent forward), it creates a rocking couple at speed. Fix: re-bend the blade into the plane, or add a small in-plane counterweight so each blade's centre of mass lies in the rotor plane.

## Safety Notes
- Cutting mabati or sheet metal produces sharp edges that can cut deeply. File all edges flat before handling; wear gloves if available. The teacher supervises all cutting.
- A heated nail for punching holes is hot — use pliers to hold it and keep a cup of water nearby to cool it.
- The hand drill and hammer can pinch fingers. Demonstrate correct grip before learners use them.
- Never spin a rotor near someone's face. Hold the axle at arm's length and spin away from your body when testing.
- A loose blade can fly off under spin. Check every fastening before each spin test; tighten or re-crimp any loose nail.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have built the paper pinwheel (Lesson 308) and studied blade angle and lift/drag (Lesson 307). The pinwheel taught the idea that an angled blade turns in the wind; this lesson scales that up to a working rotor.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on cutting equal blades, fastening them at the same angle, and the spin test. Ages 14–17 tackle the balance calculation, friction torque, and dynamic wobble analysis in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: a bicycle fundi or jua kali workshop is the best source for a front hub with a good bearing. If no hub is available, a thick nail through two washers, well-oiled, makes a workable substitute bearing. Mabati offcuts are often free from roofing fundis; stiff plastic from broken jerricans is plentiful.
- Save each group's rotor — it is the core of the windmill built across Lessons 309–312 and tested in Lesson 313. Label rotors with the group name.
- A rotor that does not spin freely almost always has a bearing problem, not a blade problem. Check oil and cone tightness before blaming the blades.
