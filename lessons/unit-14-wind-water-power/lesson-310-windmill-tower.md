# Lesson 310: Windmill Project II: Tower and Yaw

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** windmill

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a triangulated tower from poles and wire that stands 2–3 m high and holds the rotor steady.
2. Explain why triangulation makes a tower rigid using the Unit 13 result that a triangle is the only rigid polygon.
3. Fit a tail vane so the rotor yawes (turns) to face the wind automatically.
4. State a safe tower height for the school grounds and justify it in terms of access and safety.

## Materials
- Poles: eucalyptus (mururwa) or bamboo, four pieces about 2.5–3 m long and two shorter pieces about 1 m for cross-bracing
- Wire or strong string for lashing joints (fencing wire, binding wire, or sisal)
- Pliers for tightening wire
- A piece of mabati, stiff plastic, or plywood about 30 cm × 25 cm for the tail vane
- Wooden pegs or heavy stones to anchor the tower legs
- A rope or guy line, two or three lengths, to steady the tower
- The rotor from Lesson 309
- A compass or phone compass app
- A tape measure or marked string
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **tower** (mnara) — the structure that holds the rotor up into smoother, stronger wind
- **triangulation** (pembe tatu) — building with triangles so the structure cannot be pushed out of shape; a triangle is the only rigid polygon
- **yaw** (kugeuka kufuata upepo) — the rotor turning about a vertical axis to face the wind
- **tail vane** (mkia wa upepo) — a flat sheet behind the rotor that the wind pushes to keep the rotor facing into the wind
- **guy line** (kamba ya kushikilia) — a rope from the tower to the ground that steadies it against the wind
- **overturning moment** (mzozo wa kugeuza) — the wind's force on the rotor tending to tip the tower over; resisted by the base and guys

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Why is a triangle more rigid than a square built from the same poles?
2. What must a windmill's rotor do so that it always faces the wind, even when the wind changes direction?
3. Why is a taller tower generally better for catching wind than a short one?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A triangle is the only polygon whose shape is fixed by its side lengths — you cannot push it out of shape without bending or breaking a side. A square can be pushed into a rhombus without changing any side length, so it is not rigid on its own.
2. The rotor must yaw — turn about a vertical axis — and a tail vane behind the rotor uses the wind to keep it pointed into the wind automatically.
3. Wind is stronger and smoother higher up, away from the friction of the ground, trees, and buildings. A taller tower reaches into faster, less turbulent air.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — push a single pole standing upright; it wobbles. Push a tripod of three poles lashed at the top; it holds firm. Ask: *Why does the tripod stand?* Connect to Unit 13 — the triangle is the rigid shape. Tell learners today they build the tower that will carry their rotor.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups, learners build a tripod or A-frame tower. They lash three long poles at the top, splay the legs, add cross-braces to form triangles along the legs, anchor the feet with pegs or stones, and run guy lines. They mount the rotor on a stub axle at the top and fit the tail vane so it can swing the rotor into the wind. They test yaw by turning the rotor away from the wind and watching it swing back.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board, draw the tower from the side and front. Mark the triangles in the bracing. Confront the misconception that *a heavier tower is always steadier* — weight helps but the real rigidity comes from triangulation; a heavy but un-braced tower still sags and wobbles. Then draw the top view: the tail vane behind the rotor, the wind pushing the tail so the rotor faces upwind. Explain the overturning moment: the wind force on the rotor disc tries to tip the tower, and the base width plus guy lines resist it.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *triangulation makes a tower rigid; a tail vane keeps the rotor facing the wind.* Homework: look at one real tower near your home (electricity pylon, water tank stand, radio mast) and count how many triangles you can see in its structure.

## Hands-On Build: Tower and Yaw
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Gather poles and wire the day before.*

### Parts
- Four long poles (eucalyptus or bamboo), 2.5–3 m each
- Two short poles, about 1 m each, for cross-bracing
- Binding wire or strong sisal string
- Pliers
- Tail vane: mabati, stiff plastic, or plywood, about 30 cm × 25 cm
- Wooden pegs or heavy stones for anchoring the feet
- Two or three lengths of rope for guy lines
- The rotor from Lesson 309
- Compass or phone compass

### Workflow
1. Lash three long poles together at the top with wire, leaving about 20 cm above the lashing to mount the rotor. Stand the tripod up and splay the legs about 1 m apart at the base. **Checkpoint:** the tripod stands on its own and the top does not sag when you push down on it.
2. Add cross-braces: lash a short pole between two legs about 1 m above the ground, forming a triangle with the leg and the ground. Repeat for each face. **Checkpoint:** when you push the top sideways, the tower barely moves — the triangles hold it rigid.
3. Anchor each foot: drive a wooden peg beside the foot and lash it, or pile stones around the foot. Run a guy line from near the top of the tower to a peg in the ground on each of two or three sides. **Checkpoint:** a firm push on the tower does not lift any foot off the ground.
4. Mount the rotor: fix a short stub axle to the top of the tower so the rotor spins freely on it. The rotor assembly (from Lesson 309) should turn as a unit about the vertical axis for yaw. **Checkpoint:** the rotor spins on its axle and the whole rotor-head can be turned by hand about the vertical axis.
5. Fit the tail vane: lash or bolt the tail sheet to the back of the rotor-head, opposite the blades, angled flat to the wind. **Checkpoint:** when you turn the rotor-head away from the wind and let go, the tail swings it back to face the wind.
6. Check the height: measure from the ground to the rotor centre. Aim for 2–3 m — high enough to reach cleaner wind but low enough that a learner standing on a low stool can reach the hub safely. **Checkpoint:** the height is between 2 m and 3 m and recorded on the notebook.

### Common Mistakes
- **No cross-bracing** — a tripod without braces sags and wobbles. Add the short cross-poles to form triangles on every face before raising the tower.
- **Tail vane too small or too close to the rotor** — a tiny tail does not catch enough wind to yaw the rotor. Use a sheet at least 30 cm across and mount it well behind the rotor so the wind has leverage on it.
- **Tail fixed rigidly** — if the tail cannot swing, the rotor cannot yaw. The whole rotor-head (blades plus tail) must turn as one unit about the vertical axis.
- **Feet not anchored** — the first gust lifts a leg and the tower tips. Peg or stone every foot and tighten the guy lines so no foot lifts under a push.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Why is a triangle more rigid than a square built from the same poles? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What must a windmill's rotor do so that it always faces the wind, even when the wind changes direction? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why is a taller tower generally better for catching wind than a short one? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Name two features of the tower that resist the wind's overturning moment.
5. Challenge: your rotor will not yaw — it stays turned away from the wind. Give one likely cause and the fix.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A triangle is the only polygon whose shape is fixed by its side lengths; it cannot be deformed without bending or breaking a side. A square can be sheared into a rhombus without changing side lengths, so it is not rigid on its own.
2. The rotor must yaw — turn about a vertical axis — and a tail vane behind the rotor uses the wind's push to keep it facing into the wind automatically.
3. Wind is stronger and less turbulent higher up, away from ground friction, trees, and buildings. A taller tower reaches faster, smoother air.
4. Any two of: a wide base (spread of the legs), cross-bracing triangles, guy lines anchored to the ground, and pegged or weighted feet.
5. Likely cause: the tail vane is too small, mounted too close to the rotor, or the rotor-head is stuck and cannot turn about the vertical axis. Fix: enlarge the tail, move it further behind the rotor, and free the yaw pivot so the head swings easily.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** The wind exerts a force of 8 N on the rotor disc. The rotor centre is 2.5 m above the ground. The tower base is a triangle with legs 1.0 m apart. Estimate the overturning moment about the leeward edge of the base and state the resisting moment the base weight must provide. If the tower weighs 120 N and its centre of mass is 0.5 m from the tipping edge, is it stable?
   **Worked Answer:** Overturning moment = force × height = 8 N × 2.5 m = 20 N·m about the leeward edge. Resisting moment from the tower weight = weight × distance of CG from tipping edge = 120 N × 0.5 m = 60 N·m. Since 60 N·m > 20 N·m, the tower is stable; the resisting moment exceeds the overturning moment by 40 N·m.
2. **Q:** A guy line is anchored to the ground 2 m from the base of the tower and attached 2 m up the tower. If the horizontal wind force on the rotor is 10 N applied at the top (2.5 m), what tension in the guy line (assuming it acts along its length) provides an equal resisting horizontal moment? Take the guy line angle from horizontal as arctan(2/2) = 45°.
   **Worked Answer:** Overturning moment = 10 N × 2.5 m = 25 N·m. The guy line attaches 2 m up the tower. Its horizontal component is T cos(45°) = T × 0.707, giving a resisting moment of (T × 0.707) × 2 m = 1.414 T. Set 1.414 T = 25, so T = 25 / 1.414 ≈ 17.7 N. The guy line must carry about 18 N of tension to resist the overturning moment.
3. **Q:** The wind speed at 2 m height is 3 m/s. Using the rough power-law profile v(h) ∝ h^(1/7), estimate the wind speed at 3 m height on the same tower, and state the percentage increase in wind power available (which goes as v³).
   **Worked Answer:** v(3 m) / v(2 m) = (3/2)^(1/7) = (1.5)^(1/7). ln(1.5) ≈ 0.405, so (1.5)^(1/7) = e^(0.405/7) = e^0.0579 ≈ 1.0596. So v(3 m) ≈ 3 × 1.0596 ≈ 3.18 m/s. Power ratio = (v₃/v₂)³ = (1.0596)³ ≈ 1.189. So the available wind power increases by about 19% when the tower goes from 2 m to 3 m.

## Safety Notes
- Raising a 2–3 m tower needs two people: one holds the top while the other pegs the feet. Never raise a tower alone in wind.
- Keep the tower away from overhead power lines. Check the site for lines before raising; allow at least 5 m horizontal clearance.
- The rotor has sharp metal edges. Mount it only after the tower is up and stable; do not handle the rotor while raising the tower.
- Guy lines are trip hazards. Mark them with a cloth or chalk so no one walks into them.
- If the wind gusts above Beaufort 5 (small trees sway) during the build, lower the tower and wait. A half-built tower can blow over.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have built the rotor (Lesson 309) and studied triangulation and rigidity in Unit 13 (Elasticity, Materials & Structures). The tripod demo at the start recalls the Unit 13 result that a triangle is rigid.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the tripod, adding braces, and fitting the tail. Ages 14–17 do the overturning-moment and guy-line calculations and the wind-speed profile in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: eucalyptus poles (mururwa) are sold cheaply at timber yards; bamboo is free in many areas. Binding wire is available at hardware shops. A piece of mabati or a broken jerrican side makes a good tail vane.
- A 2–3 m height is a deliberate safety choice for a school build: it reaches cleaner wind but stays low enough that a learner on a low stool can reach the hub for adjustments. Do not exceed 3 m without a proper engineered base and guy anchors.
- Save the tower — Lessons 311 and 312 add the drive train and pump to this structure, and Lesson 313 tests the whole machine on this tower.
