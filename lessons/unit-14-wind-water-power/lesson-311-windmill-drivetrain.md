# Lesson 311: Windmill Project III: The Drive Train

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** windmill

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a crank or cam that converts the rotor's rotational motion into reciprocating (up-and-down) motion.
2. Connect a rod from the crank to a guide so the rod moves in a straight line.
3. Identify each friction point in the drive train and oil it.
4. Measure the stroke length of the rod and relate it to the crank radius.

## Materials
- The rotor and tower from Lessons 309–310
- Crank wire or a bent rod: a piece of stiff wire or thin rod about 20 cm long, bent into a crank shape (an offset bend)
- Connecting rod: a straight piece of wire or thin rod about 30 cm long
- Guides: two short pieces of pipe, bamboo, or wood with a hole drilled through, fixed to the tower to keep the rod moving straight
- A small bolt or nail to act as the crank pin
- Pliers, a small hammer, and a file (teacher-supervised)
- Cooking oil or grease for friction points
- A ruler or tape measure
- A marker pen
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **drive train** (mnyororo wa nguvu) — the parts that transfer motion from the rotor to the pump: crank, connecting rod, and guides
- **crank** (kila) — a bent shaft that converts rotation into reciprocating motion; the offset bend is the crank radius
- **cam** (kibo) — a rotating off-centre shape that pushes a rod up and down, an alternative to a crank
- **reciprocating motion** (mwendo wa pande pande) — back-and-forth or up-and-down motion, as opposed to rotation
- **connecting rod** (kijiti cha kuunganisha) — the rod that links the crank pin to the pump, transferring the up-and-down motion
- **stroke** (mzunguko wa kishindo) — the total up-and-down distance the rod travels in one turn; equal to twice the crank radius
- **friction point** (pengo ya msuguano) — any place two parts rub together, such as the crank pin, the guides, and the bearing

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A windmill's rotor spins round and round, but a water pump moves up and down. What part converts one motion into the other?
2. If a crank has a radius of 4 cm, what is the stroke length of the rod it drives?
3. Why should every friction point in the drive train be oiled?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A crank (or cam) converts the rotor's rotational motion into the reciprocating (up-and-down) motion the pump needs.
2. Stroke = 2 × crank radius = 2 × 4 cm = 8 cm.
3. Oil reduces friction at rubbing surfaces, so less of the wind's energy is wasted as heat and the drive train runs smoothly. Un-oiled friction can stall the rotor or wear the parts.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — turn a bicycle pedal and ask: *The pedal goes round, but the chain pulls back and forth. What makes the round motion become straight motion?* Show a bent wire crank and move it so learners see the pin go round while a rod moves up and down. Tell learners today they add the drive train that connects their rotor to a pump.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups, learners bend a crank wire with an offset (the crank radius), fix it to the rotor shaft, attach a connecting rod to the crank pin, pass the rod through two guides fixed to the tower, and oil every friction point. They turn the rotor by hand and watch the rod move up and down. They measure the stroke length with a ruler.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board, draw the crank from the side: a circle for the crank pin's path, a rod pinned to it, and the rod moving straight through the guides. Show that the pin goes round (radius = crank radius) while the rod moves between top and bottom positions separated by 2 × crank radius = the stroke. Confront the misconception that *a bigger crank radius always pumps more water* — a larger stroke moves the rod further per turn, but it also loads the rotor more (more work per stroke), so the rotor slows down. The best crank radius balances stroke length against rotor speed.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a crank turns rotation into up-and-down motion; the stroke is twice the crank radius.* Homework: find one machine near your home that uses a crank or cam (bicycle, grinding mill, sewing machine, car engine) and write one sentence about what it converts.

## Hands-On Build: The Drive Train
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Gather wire and guides the day before.*

### Parts
- Crank wire or bent rod, about 20 cm long
- Crank pin: a small bolt or nail
- Connecting rod: straight wire or thin rod, about 30 cm long
- Two guides: short pipe, bamboo, or wood blocks with a hole, fixed to the tower
- Pliers, small hammer, file
- Cooking oil or grease
- Ruler and marker pen
- The rotor and tower from Lessons 309–310

### Workflow
1. Bend the crank: take the 20 cm wire and make an offset bend so one end (the crank pin) sits about 3–4 cm off the centre line of the shaft. Fix the straight end to the rotor shaft so it turns with the rotor. **Checkpoint:** when you spin the rotor, the crank pin traces a circle of the chosen radius.
2. Attach the connecting rod: drill or punch a hole near one end of the 30 cm rod and pass the crank pin through it. Secure the pin with a folded wire or nut so the rod cannot slip off. **Checkpoint:** the rod swings freely on the pin with no binding.
3. Fit the guides: fix two guide blocks (pipe or drilled wood) to the tower about 20 cm apart, aligned so the rod passes straight through both holes. Thread the free end of the rod through the guides. **Checkpoint:** when you turn the rotor, the rod moves up and down in a straight line through the guides without sideways swing.
4. Oil every friction point: the crank pin, the guide holes, and the bearing. Spin the rotor by hand and feel the difference before and after oiling. **Checkpoint:** after oiling, a gentle flick of a blade keeps the rotor turning and the rod moving for at least two full strokes.
5. Measure the stroke: mark the rod at its highest and lowest positions, then measure the distance between the marks with a ruler. Record the value. **Checkpoint:** the measured stroke is approximately 2 × the crank radius you bent; if it is not, find the error (pin slipping, guide misaligned) and fix it.
6. Count strokes per turn: turn the rotor exactly one full turn and count how many times the rod goes up and down. For a simple crank, it should be one stroke per turn. Record this. **Checkpoint:** one full rotor turn gives exactly one rod stroke, confirmed by counting.

### Common Mistakes
- **Guides misaligned** — if the two guides are not in a straight line with the crank pin's plane, the rod bends and jams. Re-align the guides so the rod slides freely.
- **Crank pin too tight** — a pin that grips the rod stops it swinging. The pin must rotate freely in the rod's hole; oil it and loosen the fit.
- **Crank radius too large** — a big radius loads the rotor heavily and it stalls in light wind. Start with 3–4 cm; you can increase it later if the rotor has power to spare.
- **No oil on guides** — dry guides drag and can stop the whole drive train. Oil every rubbing surface and re-oil after any long run.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A windmill's rotor spins round and round, but a water pump moves up and down. What part converts one motion into the other? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If a crank has a radius of 4 cm, what is the stroke length of the rod it drives? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why should every friction point in the drive train be oiled? (mirrors pre-test)
4. You turn the rotor one full turn and the rod goes up and down once. What is the relationship between rotor rpm (turns per minute) and strokes per minute?
5. Challenge: your rotor slows down and nearly stops after you add the drive train. Name one likely cause and the fix.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A crank (or cam) converts the rotor's rotational motion into the pump's reciprocating (up-and-down) motion.
2. Stroke = 2 × crank radius = 2 × 4 cm = 8 cm.
3. Oil reduces friction at rubbing surfaces, so less energy is wasted as heat and the drive train runs smoothly; un-oiled friction can stall the rotor or wear the parts.
4. Strokes per minute = rotor rpm × 1 (one stroke per turn for a simple crank). So if the rotor turns at 60 rpm, the rod makes 60 strokes per minute.
5. Likely cause: too much friction (dry crank pin or guides) or the crank radius is too large, loading the rotor. Fix: oil every friction point, and if it still stalls, reduce the crank radius so each stroke does less work.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** Your crank radius is 4 cm and the rotor turns at 80 rpm. What is the stroke length and how many strokes per minute does the pump rod make? If each stroke lifts 50 cm³ of water, what is the flow rate in litres per minute?
   **Worked Answer:** Stroke = 2 × 4 cm = 8 cm. Strokes per minute = 80 rpm × 1 stroke/turn = 80 strokes/min. Volume per minute = 80 × 50 cm³ = 4000 cm³ = 4.0 litres. Flow rate = 4.0 L/min.
2. **Q:** You want to double the flow rate by increasing the crank radius from 4 cm to 8 cm, keeping the same rotor rpm. Explain why the rotor will actually slow down, and predict whether the flow rate will double, less than double, or more than double.
   **Worked Answer:** Doubling the crank radius doubles the stroke length, so each stroke moves twice as much water — but it also doubles the work done per stroke (the pump must push the water twice as far). At the same wind power, the rotor cannot do twice the work at the same speed, so it slows down. Because flow rate = strokes per minute × volume per stroke, and strokes per minute falls while volume per stroke doubles, the flow rate will increase by less than double. The exact outcome depends on the rotor's torque-speed curve, but it will not reach 2×.
3. **Q:** A gear of 20 teeth on the rotor shaft drives a gear of 40 teeth on the crank shaft. What is the gear ratio, and how many strokes per minute does the pump make if the rotor turns at 120 rpm? Why might you choose this gearing for a windy site?
   **Worked Answer:** Gear ratio = driven teeth / driver teeth = 40 / 20 = 2:1 (the crank shaft turns at half the rotor speed). Crank rpm = 120 / 2 = 60 rpm. Strokes per minute = 60. At a windy site the rotor spins fast but the pump needs a slower, more powerful stroke; the 2:1 reduction trades speed for torque so the pump is not overloaded at high rotor rpm.

## Safety Notes
- The crank pin and rod move with force when the rotor turns. Keep fingers clear of the crank pin and guide holes during the spin test.
- The bent wire ends are sharp. File them flat before handling, and wear gloves if available when bending the crank.
- Pliers and the hammer can pinch fingers. Demonstrate correct grip before learners use them.
- Do not spin the rotor hard during the build — a sudden fast turn can fling the connecting rod if it is not secured. Spin gently by hand.
- Keep oil away from eyes and mouths. Wipe spills so the ground is not slippery.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have built the rotor (Lesson 309) and tower (Lesson 310). The idea that a crank converts rotation to reciprocation is new, but the bicycle-pedal hook connects it to everyday experience.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on bending the crank, fitting the guides, oiling, and measuring the stroke. Ages 14–17 do the stroke-to-flow-rate, load-vs-speed, and gear-ratio calculations in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: stiff wire from old fences or bicycle spokes works for the crank and connecting rod. Short pipe offcuts or drilled wood blocks make excellent guides. Cooking oil is a fine lubricant for a school build; grease lasts longer if available.
- Save the drive train — Lesson 312 connects the lower end of the rod to the diaphragm pump from Unit 8, and Lesson 313 tests the complete machine.
- If the rotor stalls after adding the drive train, the first check is always friction, not the crank radius. Oil the pin and guides, re-test, and only then consider reducing the crank radius.
