# Lesson 312: Windmill Project IV: Linking the Pump

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** windmill

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Connect the drive train rod to the diaphragm pump from Unit 8 so the rotor's up-and-down motion drives the pump.
2. Run a suction line from a water container to the pump and a discharge line to a raised point, fixing both with clamps.
3. Verify that each check valve is oriented to let water flow one way only and not back.
4. Run the windmill by hand and confirm that water is actually lifted from the source to the discharge.

## Materials
- The windmill with rotor, tower, and drive train from Lessons 309–311
- The diaphragm pump built in Unit 8 (Lesson 189 or the Unit 8 pump build)
- Suction tube: clear or opaque plastic tubing about 1 m long, sized to fit the pump inlet
- Discharge tube: plastic tubing about 1 m long, sized to fit the pump outlet
- A 20-litre jerrican or bucket of water as the source supply
- A second container or measuring jar to catch the discharge
- Hose clamps or wire to secure the tubes to the pump fittings
- A marker pen to mark flow direction on the check valves
- Cooking oil or grease for the pump linkage
- A ruler or tape measure to set the head height
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **diaphragm pump** (pampu ya kifuko) — a pump that moves water by flexing a flexible diaphragm up and down, drawing water in on the upstroke and pushing it out on the downstroke
- **suction line** (mrija wa kuvutia) — the tube that carries water from the source container up to the pump inlet
- **discharge line** (mrija wa kutoa) — the tube that carries water from the pump outlet up to the delivery point
- **check valve** (valvu ya kwenda moja) — a one-way valve that lets water flow in one direction only and blocks it from flowing back
- **head** (kichwa cha maji) — the vertical height the water is lifted from source to discharge, in metres
- **stroke volume** (kiwango cha kila kishindo) — the volume of water pushed per stroke of the pump, in millilitres or cm³
- **priming** (kujaza maji) — filling the pump and suction line with water to remove air before starting so the pump can draw

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What does a check valve do in a pump?
2. If the suction line has air in it, why will the pump not draw water?
3. Name the two lines connected to a pump and state which way water flows in each.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A check valve lets water flow in one direction only and blocks it from flowing back, so water is drawn in on the suction stroke and pushed out on the discharge stroke without running back.
2. Air is compressible, so when the diaphragm moves it compresses the air instead of drawing water. The pump cannot create enough suction to lift water through an air-filled line until the air is removed (priming).
3. The suction line carries water from the source container to the pump inlet (flow toward the pump). The discharge line carries water from the pump outlet to the delivery point (flow away from the pump).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the diaphragm pump from Unit 8 and squeeze it by hand into a bucket. Ask: *What would it take to make the windmill do this squeezing for us?* Board the answers and steer toward: connect the rod, add suction and discharge lines, and check the valves. Tell learners today they finish the windmill by linking the pump.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups, learners connect the lower end of the drive train rod to the pump's diaphragm plunger, run the suction line from the jerrican to the pump inlet and the discharge line to a raised jar, clamp both lines, mark and verify the check valve directions, prime the pump by filling the lines with water, and turn the rotor by hand to confirm water is lifted.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board, draw the pump with the suction line down to the source and the discharge line up to the delivery point. Mark the two check valves and their flow directions. Confront the misconception that *the pump sucks water up* — the pump does not suck; it lowers the pressure at its inlet, and atmospheric pressure pushes the water up the suction line. That is why a pump cannot lift water more than about 10 m even with a perfect vacuum, and why air in the line defeats it. Show how priming removes the air so atmospheric pressure can do its work.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the windmill drives the pump; check valves keep water moving one way; prime to remove air.* Homework: at home, find a hand pump (bicycle pump, water pump, jerrican tap) and write one sentence about which way the valves let fluid flow.

## Hands-On Build: Linking the Pump
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Gather tubes and clamps the day before.*

### Parts
- Windmill with rotor, tower, and drive train (Lessons 309–311)
- Diaphragm pump from Unit 8
- Suction tube, about 1 m long, fitting the pump inlet
- Discharge tube, about 1 m long, fitting the pump outlet
- 20-litre jerrican of water (source)
- Measuring jar or second container (discharge catch)
- Hose clamps or binding wire
- Marker pen for valve direction
- Cooking oil or grease
- Ruler or tape measure

### Workflow
1. Connect the rod to the pump: fix the lower end of the drive train rod to the diaphragm plunger so the rod's up-and-down motion flexes the diaphragm. Oil the linkage pin. **Checkpoint:** when you move the rod by hand, the diaphragm flexes fully with no binding and the linkage pin is oiled.
2. Fit the suction line: push one end of the suction tube onto the pump inlet and the other end into the source jerrican, below the water surface. Secure both ends with clamps. **Checkpoint:** the tube is firmly attached and cannot pull off when you tug gently.
3. Fit the discharge line: push one end of the discharge tube onto the pump outlet and route the other end to the catch container at the delivery height. Clamp both ends. **Checkpoint:** the discharge tube runs uphill from the pump to the catch container with no kinks.
4. Verify the check valves: locate each check valve (inlet and outlet). Mark the flow direction with an arrow. Suck gently on the suction end — water should flow toward the pump. Blow gently on the suction end — flow should be blocked. Repeat for the discharge valve. **Checkpoint:** each valve passes flow one way and blocks it the other way; if a valve is reversed, remove and refit it the correct way.
5. Prime the pump: fill the suction line and pump chamber with water from the top to push out the air. Pour water into the discharge line as well. **Checkpoint:** no air bubbles come out of the suction line when you fill it; the line is full of water.
6. Run by hand: turn the rotor by hand for several strokes. Water should rise through the suction line and flow out of the discharge line into the catch container. **Checkpoint:** water appears at the discharge within the first few strokes and continues with each stroke; if not, check for air leaks or a reversed valve and re-prime.
7. Measure the head: measure the vertical height from the water surface in the source jerrican to the discharge point. Record it. **Checkpoint:** the head is measured vertically (not along the tube) and recorded in metres.

### Common Mistakes
- **Check valve reversed** — if a valve is in backwards, water is blocked and the pump will not draw or discharge. Mark the flow direction and test each valve before running.
- **Air in the suction line** — the pump compresses air instead of drawing water. Prime the line fully until no bubbles appear.
- **Loose tube fittings** — a loose clamp lets air in, breaking the suction. Tighten every clamp and re-test.
- **Discharge tube kinked or running downhill then uphill** — a kink blocks flow and an air pocket can form. Route the discharge tube in a smooth uphill run with no dips.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What does a check valve do in a pump? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If the suction line has air in it, why will the pump not draw water? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name the two lines connected to a pump and state which way water flows in each. (mirrors pre-test)
4. You prime the pump and turn the rotor, but no water comes out. Name two things you should check.
5. Challenge: why is it more accurate to say atmospheric pressure pushes water up the suction line than to say the pump sucks it up?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A check valve lets water flow in one direction only and blocks it from flowing back, ensuring water is drawn in on the suction stroke and pushed out on the discharge stroke.
2. Air is compressible, so the diaphragm compresses the air instead of creating the low pressure needed to draw water; the pump cannot lift water through an air-filled line until the air is removed by priming.
3. The suction line carries water from the source to the pump inlet (toward the pump). The discharge line carries water from the pump outlet to the delivery point (away from the pump).
4. Any two of: a reversed check valve, air in the suction line (not primed), a loose tube fitting letting air in, a kinked discharge tube, or the rod not actually moving the diaphragm.
5. The pump lowers the pressure at its inlet, and it is atmospheric pressure acting on the surface of the source water that pushes the water up the suction line. The pump cannot pull; it can only create a pressure difference. That is why the lift is limited by atmospheric pressure (about 10 m for water) and why air in the line, which the pump can compress, defeats the suction.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** Your pump has a stroke volume of 80 cm³ per stroke. The rotor turns at 60 rpm and one stroke occurs per turn. The measured efficiency is 40% (only 40% of the theoretical strokes actually deliver water due to leakage and valve timing). What is the actual flow rate in litres per minute?
   **Worked Answer:** Strokes per minute = 60 rpm × 1 stroke/turn = 60 strokes/min. Theoretical flow = 60 × 80 cm³ = 4800 cm³ = 4.8 L/min. Actual flow = 4.8 × 0.40 = 1.92 L/min ≈ 1.9 L/min.
2. **Q:** The pump lifts water to a head of 1.5 m at the flow rate from question 1 (1.92 L/min). Calculate the hydraulic power output in watts. Take ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s². If the wind power input to the rotor is 25 W, what is the overall efficiency of the windmill-pump system?
   **Worked Answer:** Flow rate Q = 1.92 L/min = 1.92 × 10⁻³ m³ / 60 s = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ m³/s. Hydraulic power P = ρgQh = 1000 × 9.81 × 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 1.5 = 0.47 W. Overall efficiency = P_out / P_in = 0.47 / 25 = 0.019 = 1.9%.
3. **Q:** You want to predict the flow rate at a windier time when the rotor turns at 90 rpm instead of 60 rpm, with the same stroke volume and efficiency. What flow rate do you predict, and state one assumption you are making.
   **Worked Answer:** At 90 rpm, strokes per minute = 90. Theoretical flow = 90 × 80 cm³ = 7200 cm³ = 7.2 L/min. At the same 40% efficiency, actual flow = 7.2 × 0.40 = 2.88 L/min ≈ 2.9 L/min. The assumption is that the efficiency stays the same at the higher speed — in practice, higher speed can increase leakage or valve losses, so the real flow may be slightly less than predicted.

## Safety Notes
- The pump rod moves with force when the rotor turns. Keep fingers clear of the rod-to-pump linkage during the spin test.
- Water spills make the ground slippery. Wipe up spills and keep the work area clear; set the jerrican on a stable, level surface.
- Do not drink the test water — the pump and tubes are not food-grade. Keep a separate drinking supply.
- If the wind gusts during the outdoor test, stand clear of the rotor and lower the tower if the gust exceeds Beaufort 5.
- Tubing under suction can collapse if pinched; check the suction line for flattening during the run and re-route it if it kinks.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have built the diaphragm pump in Unit 8 (fluids in motion) and completed the rotor, tower, and drive train in Lessons 309–311. The idea that the pump lowers inlet pressure and atmospheric pressure pushes water up is the key concept to reinforce.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on connecting the rod, fitting and clamping the tubes, priming, and confirming water flows. Ages 14–17 do the flow-rate, head-power, and efficiency calculations in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: plastic tubing is sold at hardware shops; clear tubing lets learners see the water and any air bubbles. Hose clamps or binding wire secure the fittings; a 20-litre jerrican is the standard Kenyan water container. The diaphragm pump from Unit 8 should be reused; if it is unavailable, a simple piston-in-pipe pump can substitute but needs its own check valves.
- This lesson completes the windmill. Lesson 313 tests the whole machine outdoors and measures the water lifted per minute; the data feeds the iteration (Lesson 314) and the engineering report (Lesson 316). Save every part of the assembly.
- If the pump will not draw after priming, the first check is always the check valves (reversed?) and the second is the fittings (air leak?). A tiny air leak on the suction side defeats the pump; tighten every clamp and re-prime.
