# Lesson 314: Windmill Project VI: Iteration

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** windmill

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Choose one specific improvement to test on their windmill (blade pitch, tail size, bearing friction, or stroke length).
2. Run an A/B test that compares the modified windmill against the baseline log from Lesson 313.
3. Record before-and-after data with wind speed noted for every trial so the comparison is fair.
4. Explain why wind variation between trials can fool you and how interleaving (alternating A and B) controls for it.

## Materials
- Windmill and pump from Lesson 313
- Log sheets from Lesson 313 (the baseline)
- Spare blades of different sizes or materials
- Small weights or washers to adjust tail size
- Tools: screwdriver, pliers, adjustable spanner, sandpaper, candle wax
- Stopwatch and measuring jar (same as test day)
- Cup anemometer or Beaufort card
- Fresh log sheets (trial | config | wind speed | time | water | notes)
- Pencil and clipboard

## Vocabulary
- **iteration** (marudio) — making one change, testing, and learning from the result before the next change
- **A/B test** (jaribio la kulinganisha) — comparing two configurations (A = baseline, B = modified) under the same conditions
- **blade pitch** (mtego wa waru) — the angle of the blade relative to the wind; steeper pitch catches more or less wind
- **bearing friction** (msuguano wa beringi) — resistance in the rotating shaft support; less friction means more power reaches the pump
- **stroke length** (urefu wa mshuko) — how far the pump rod travels up and down per revolution; longer stroke moves more water per cycle
- **interleaving** (kubadilishana) — alternating A and B trials so wind changes affect both equally

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Your team changed the blade pitch AND the tail size on the same day. The output went up. Can you tell which change caused the improvement? Why or why not?
2. The wind was stronger during your modified test than during your baseline test. The output went up. Is the windmill better, or was it just the wind?
3. What does "bearing friction" mean, and why does reducing it help a windmill pump more water?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. No — you changed two variables at once, so you cannot tell which one (or which combination) caused the improvement. Change only one thing per iteration.
2. You cannot tell from this test alone — the stronger wind may have caused the higher output even if the modification did nothing. You must compare at the same wind speed, or interleave trials so both configurations face the same wind.
3. Bearing friction is the resistance in the shaft support (where the axle rotates). Reducing it means less of the wind's power is wasted as heat in the bearing, so more power reaches the pump to lift water.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — ask: *one thing you would change to make your windmill pump more water.* Take three answers and board them. Introduce the rule of iteration: change ONE thing, test, learn. Show the baseline log from Lesson 313 and say: *today we beat this number — or find out why we can't.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — each team picks one improvement: blade pitch (bend the blades 10° steeper or shallower), tail size (add or remove a tail weight), bearing friction (wax the shaft or sand the bearing surface), or stroke length (move the crank pin one hole out). They run an A/B test: two trials with the baseline config (A), two with the modified config (B), alternating A-B-A-B so wind changes affect both equally. Every trial records wind speed, time, and water volume. They photograph any visible change.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — teams write their A and B results on the board alongside the wind speed for each trial. Confront the misconception that *B beat A, so the modification worked* — if the wind was stronger during the B trials, the improvement may be wind, not the change. Show how to compute water-per-wind-speed (output ÷ wind speed) for each trial to normalise. A modification only counts as an improvement if B's normalised output beats A's normalised output consistently.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *change one thing, interleave, normalise for wind*. Homework: write up the A/B result in one paragraph — what you changed, what happened, and whether it was a real improvement.

## Hands-On Build: A/B Iteration Test
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Windmill and pump from Lesson 313
- Spare blades, tail weights, wax, sandpaper, tools
- Stopwatch, measuring jar, anemometer
- Baseline log from Lesson 313
- Fresh log sheets and pencil

### Workflow
1. Choose ONE improvement to test. Write it down: "We change _______." Do not touch anything else on the windmill. **Checkpoint:** only one variable is changed; all other parts stay the same.
2. Run trial A1 (baseline config) for 60 seconds. Record wind speed, time, and water volume on the log sheet. **Checkpoint:** the log row is complete with units before moving on.
3. Swap to config B (apply the modification). Run trial B1 for 60 seconds. Record the same fields. **Checkpoint:** the modification is visibly in place (photograph if helpful).
4. Swap back to config A. Run trial A2. Then swap to B and run trial B2. This A-B-A-B interleaving means any wind shift between trials affects both configs equally. **Checkpoint:** four trials are logged, alternating A and B.
5. For each trial, compute the normalised output: water volume ÷ wind speed. Compare A's average normalised output with B's. **Checkpoint:** the comparison uses normalised values, not raw water volume.
6. Decide: did B beat A consistently (both B trials outperform both A trials on normalised output)? Or was it mixed? Record the conclusion on the log sheet. **Checkpoint:** the conclusion states whether the improvement was real, inconclusive, or negative.

### Common Mistakes
- **Changing two things at once** — if you adjust pitch and wax the bearing in the same session, you cannot attribute the result to either. Change one, test, then change the next.
- **Not interleaving** — running all A trials first, then all B trials, means B always faces the later wind. If the wind picks up, B looks better unfairly. Alternate A-B-A-B.
- **Forgetting to log wind speed** — without wind speed you cannot normalise, and the A/B comparison is meaningless.
- **Over-tightening the crank pin** — moving the stroke length pin too far out can jam the pump rod against the guide, stalling the rotor. Check that the rod moves freely after each adjustment.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Your team changed the blade pitch AND the tail size on the same day. The output went up. Can you tell which change caused the improvement? Why or why not? (mirrors pre-test)
2. The wind was stronger during your modified test than during your baseline test. The output went up. Is the windmill better, or was it just the wind?
3. What does "bearing friction" mean, and why does reducing it help a windmill pump more water?
4. You ran A, then B, then A, then B. A's normalised output was 1.2 and 1.3. B's was 1.5 and 1.6. Was the modification a real improvement? How do you know?
5. Challenge: your A/B test was inconclusive — B was better in one trial, A was better in the other. What should you do next?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. No — two variables changed at once, so the improvement could be from either one, or from both together. To know, change only one variable per iteration and test again.
2. You cannot tell — the stronger wind may have caused the higher output regardless of the modification. You must compare at the same wind speed or interleave trials and normalise by wind speed.
3. Bearing friction is the resistance in the rotating shaft support. Reducing it wastes less of the wind's power as heat, so more power reaches the pump to lift water.
4. Yes — B outperformed A in both trials on normalised output (1.5 and 1.6 vs 1.2 and 1.3). The consistent improvement across both B trials, with interleaving controlling for wind variation, indicates a real improvement.
5. The result is inconclusive because the two B trials disagreed. Next steps: run more trials (at least two more A-B pairs) to see if a pattern emerges, or check whether the wind was unusually variable that day. If the result remains mixed, the modification has no clear effect — try a different improvement.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your A/B test produced these normalised outputs (water mL ÷ wind speed m/s): A = 1.2, 1.3, 1.1; B = 1.5, 1.6, 1.4. Calculate the mean and the range (max − min) for each config. Is the improvement statistically meaningful given the spread?
   **Worked Answer:** Mean A = (1.2 + 1.3 + 1.1) / 3 = 1.20. Range A = 1.3 − 1.1 = 0.2. Mean B = (1.5 + 1.6 + 1.4) / 3 = 1.50. Range B = 1.6 − 1.4 = 0.2. The improvement (1.50 − 1.20 = 0.30) is larger than the range of either config (0.2), so the B trials consistently outperform A. The improvement is meaningful — B's worst trial (1.4) still beats A's best (1.3). The error bars (ranges) do not overlap, which is a strong indication of a real effect.
2. **Q:** Design an A/B test for blade pitch that controls for wind variation. Write the trial order, what you measure, and how you decide if the new pitch is better.
   **Worked Answer:** Trial order: A-B-A-B-A-B (six trials, interleaved). A = current pitch, B = new pitch (10° steeper). For each trial, measure wind speed (anemometer), time (60 s), and water volume (measuring jar). Compute normalised output = water volume ÷ wind speed for each trial. Calculate the mean normalised output for A and for B. The new pitch is better if mean B > mean A AND the ranges do not overlap (B's worst exceeds A's best, or the means differ by more than the larger range). If the ranges overlap, the result is inconclusive — run more trials. Record everything on a log sheet with the pitch angle noted for each config.
3. **Q:** You reduced bearing friction by waxing the shaft. The raw output went up by 10%, but the wind speed was 5% higher during the B trials. Is the improvement real? Calculate the expected output due to wind alone (wind power ∝ v³) and compare.
   **Worked Answer:** Wind power ∝ v³, so a 5% increase in wind speed gives (1.05)³ = 1.158, a 15.8% increase in available wind power. If the windmill efficiency stayed the same, the output should rise by ~15.8% from wind alone. The actual improvement was only 10%, which is LESS than the 15.8% expected from the wind increase. So after accounting for wind, the waxed bearing actually made the windmill perform slightly worse (or at best, no better) — the apparent improvement was entirely due to the stronger wind. This shows why raw output comparisons are misleading and normalisation is essential.

## Safety Notes
- Maintain the 2 m safety perimeter throughout all trials — the rotor spins during every A and B run.
- When adjusting blade pitch or the crank pin, stop the rotor first. Never reach into the rotor plane while the windmill is facing the wind.
- Wax and sandpaper can make the shaft slippery — wipe hands and tools before handling the windmill.
- Spare blades have sharp edges. Carry them flat and away from your body.
- If the wind gusts above Beaufort 6, stop the test and wait. A modified windmill may behave unpredictably in strong gusts.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners need the baseline log from Lesson 313 and a working windmill. The concept of power (P = ρgQh) and wind power (½ρAv³) was introduced in Lesson 313.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on making one visible change (blade pitch or tail size) and recording before-and-after water volume. Ages 14–17 do the full normalised A/B analysis and the wind-power correction in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: candle wax or soap works as a bearing lubricant. Spare blades can be cut from old jerricans or cardboard. Tail weights can be washers or small stones taped to the tail vane.
- The key habit being taught is single-variable change with interleaved trials. This is the most important engineering habit in the unit — resist the urge to let teams change everything at once. If a team wants to change two things, have them do two separate iterations.
- Save the A/B log sheets — they feed directly into Lesson 315 (data and graphs) and the engineering report in Lesson 316.
