# Lesson 315: Windmill Project VII: Data and Graphs

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** windmill

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Compile test data from Lessons 313 and 314 into a single table of wind speed versus water output.
2. Plot a performance curve (output vs wind speed) on graph paper and identify the cut-in speed.
3. Describe the shape of the performance curve and explain why output rises steeply once the wind exceeds the cut-in speed.
4. Fit an approximate relationship between output and wind speed and justify the cubic-energy intuition behind the curve's shape.

## Materials
- All log sheets from Lessons 313 and 314
- Graph paper (or ruled exercise book with squared pages)
- Pencil and ruler
- Coloured pencils (two colours)
- Anemometer card (Beaufort scale with m/s equivalents) for converting estimates
- Calculator or phone
- A copy of the class data table (teacher compiles on the board)
- Clipboard or hard surface for drawing

## Vocabulary
- **performance curve** (mchoro wa utendaji) — a graph of windmill output (water per minute) against wind speed
- **cut-in speed** (kasi ya kuanza) — the lowest wind speed at which the rotor turns and begins to pump water
- **cubic relationship** (uhusiano wa kijengo) — output rising with the cube of wind speed (v³), meaning a small wind increase gives a large output increase
- **data point** (nukta ya data) — one pair of values (wind speed, output) plotted on the graph
- **line of best fit** (mstari wa kulinganisha) — a smooth line through the data points that shows the overall trend
- **anemometer card** (kadi ya anemomita) — a reference card converting Beaufort scale observations to wind speed in m/s

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Your windmill starts pumping at 2.5 m/s wind speed. What is this speed called?
2. The wind speed doubles from 2 m/s to 4 m/s. If output depends on wind power (∝ v³), does the output double, or increase by more than double?
3. On a graph of output (y-axis) against wind speed (x-axis), where does the cut-in speed appear?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The cut-in speed — the lowest wind speed at which the rotor begins to turn and pump.
2. More than double — wind power ∝ v³, so doubling v gives 2³ = 8 times the wind power. The output will increase by much more than double (though not necessarily 8× if the windmill is inefficient at higher speeds).
3. At the point on the x-axis where the curve first rises above zero — the leftmost point where output begins.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the log sheets from test day and iteration day. Ask: *we have lots of numbers — how do we see the story?* Introduce the performance curve: a graph that shows what the windmill does at every wind speed. Show a quick sketch on the board of output vs wind speed with a flat zero section, then a rising curve, and label the cut-in point.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teams compile all their trials from Lessons 313 and 314 into one table: wind speed (m/s) | water lifted (mL/min). They convert Beaufort estimates to m/s using the anemometer card if needed. They plot the points on graph paper (wind speed on x-axis, output on y-axis), draw the line of best fit in one colour, and mark the cut-in speed with a vertical dashed line in a second colour. They label both axes with units and give the graph a title.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — teams pin their graphs to the board. Compare the curves. Confront the misconception that *the graph should be a straight line* — the curve bends upward because wind power rises with the cube of speed (v³), so a small increase in wind gives a large increase in output. Show the cut-in speed on each graph and note that it varies by team (different friction, different blade mass). Introduce the approximate fit: output ∝ (v − v_cut-in) for speeds just above cut-in, and the cubic intuition for higher speeds.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *graph the data to see the story; the curve bends up because wind power goes as v³*. Homework: read your graph and write two sentences: one stating the cut-in speed, one predicting the output at a wind speed you have not yet tested.

## Hands-On Build: Performance Curve Graph
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- All log sheets from Lessons 313 and 314
- Graph paper or squared exercise book
- Pencil, ruler, two coloured pencils
- Anemometer card (Beaufort to m/s)
- Calculator
- Clipboard

### Workflow
1. Compile a master table. List every trial from both lessons: wind speed (m/s) in the left column, water lifted (mL/min) in the right. Convert any Beaufort estimates to m/s using the anemometer card. **Checkpoint:** every row has both values with units; no blank cells.
2. Choose scales. On graph paper, let the x-axis cover 0 to the highest wind speed tested, and the y-axis cover 0 to the highest output recorded. Mark grid lines at round numbers (e.g., 1 m/s per square, 100 mL per square). **Checkpoint:** the scales use the full width and height of the paper; the axes are labelled with units (x: wind speed m/s, y: water output mL/min).
3. Plot each data point as a small dot with a circle around it. If two trials have the same wind speed, plot both — the spread shows real-world variation. **Checkpoint:** every row of the table has a corresponding dot on the graph.
4. Draw the line of best fit in the first colour — a smooth curve through the middle of the points, not a join-the-dots zigzag. The curve should be flat near zero, then rise steeply. **Checkpoint:** the curve passes through the middle of the cluster, not forced through every point.
5. Find the cut-in speed: the wind speed at which the curve first rises above zero. Draw a vertical dashed line in the second colour from that point on the x-axis up to the curve. Label it "cut-in = ___ m/s". **Checkpoint:** the cut-in line is labelled with a value.
6. Add a title: "Windmill Performance: Output vs Wind Speed" and the team name. Pin the graph to the board for the class comparison. **Checkpoint:** the graph has a title, labelled axes with units, data points, a best-fit curve, and a marked cut-in speed.

### Common Mistakes
- **Joining the dots** — a performance curve is a smooth trend, not a zigzag through every point. Real data has scatter; the best-fit curve runs through the middle.
- **Wrong axis** — wind speed is the independent variable (x-axis) because it is what nature provides; output is the dependent variable (y-axis) because it depends on the wind.
- **Not converting Beaufort** — Beaufort numbers (1, 2, 3…) are not wind speeds. Convert to m/s using the anemometer card before plotting.
- **Starting the y-axis above zero** — the curve must start at zero output to show the cut-in speed correctly. Do not crop the axis.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Your windmill starts pumping at 2.5 m/s wind speed. What is this speed called? (mirrors pre-test)
2. The wind speed doubles from 2 m/s to 4 m/s. If output depends on wind power (∝ v³), does the output double, or increase by more than double?
3. On a graph of output (y-axis) against wind speed (x-axis), where does the cut-in speed appear?
4. Two teams tested at the same wind speed but got different outputs. Give two possible reasons for the difference.
5. Challenge: your graph shows output rising from 0 at 2.5 m/s to 400 mL/min at 4 m/s. If the cubic relationship held perfectly, what output would you predict at 5 m/s? Why might the real value be lower?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The cut-in speed — the lowest wind speed at which the rotor begins to turn and pump.
2. More than double — wind power ∝ v³, so doubling v gives 2³ = 8 times the wind power. Output increases by much more than double (though real inefficiencies reduce the factor).
3. At the point on the x-axis where the curve first rises above zero — the leftmost point where output begins.
4. (a) Different windmill designs — blade pitch, rotor size, or bearing friction differ between teams. (b) Different pump conditions — head height, valve leakage, or stroke length. (c) Measurement differences — different anemometer readings or jar readings. Any two of these are valid.
5. If output ∝ (v − v_cut-in)³ roughly, then at 4 m/s the effective speed above cut-in is 4 − 2.5 = 1.5 m/s, giving 400 mL/min. At 5 m/s the effective speed is 5 − 2.5 = 2.5 m/s. Ratio = (2.5/1.5)³ = (1.667)³ ≈ 4.63. Predicted output = 400 × 4.63 ≈ 1850 mL/min. The real value may be lower because the windmill efficiency drops at higher speeds (the rotor may stall, the pump may not keep up, or mechanical losses increase), so the cubic relationship is an upper bound, not a guarantee.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your data shows: at 3 m/s, output = 150 mL/min; at 4 m/s, output = 400 mL/min; at 5 m/s, output = 780 mL/min. The cut-in speed is 2.5 m/s. Fit output ≈ k(v − v_cut-in)³ and find k. Then predict the output at 6 m/s.
   **Worked Answer:** Using v_cut-in = 2.5 m/s: at 3 m/s, (v − v_c) = 0.5, so 150 = k(0.5)³ = 0.125k → k = 1200. Check at 4 m/s: 1200 × (1.5)³ = 1200 × 3.375 = 4050 — this does NOT match 400, so the pure cubic (v − v_c)³ is too steep. Try output ≈ k(v − v_c)²: at 3 m/s, 150 = k(0.5)² = 0.25k → k = 600. Check at 4 m/s: 600 × (1.5)² = 600 × 2.25 = 1350 — still too high. The data is better fit by a linear or quadratic above cut-in. Using output ≈ k(v − v_c): at 3 m/s, 150 = k(0.5) → k = 300. Check at 4 m/s: 300 × 1.5 = 450 (close to 400). Check at 5 m/s: 300 × 2.5 = 750 (close to 780). So the best simple fit is output ≈ 300(v − 2.5) mL/min. At 6 m/s: 300 × 3.5 = 1050 mL/min. The lesson: real windmills often follow a near-linear relationship just above cut-in, not the ideal cubic, because mechanical and pump losses flatten the curve.
2. **Q:** The wind power passing through the rotor is ½ρAv³. Explain why the ideal output curve should be cubic in v, and why the real curve is less steep than cubic.
   **Worked Answer:** The available wind power is P_wind = ½ρAv³, so if the windmill captured a constant fraction of the wind power, the output would be proportional to v³ — a steeply rising cubic curve. However, the real curve is less steep because: (a) the rotor efficiency (Cp) is not constant — it peaks at a specific tip-speed ratio and falls off at other wind speeds, so as v changes the rotor moves away from its optimal operating point; (b) the pump has a threshold — below the cut-in speed it pumps nothing, so the curve is flat at zero then rises; (c) mechanical losses (bearing friction, linkage play) consume a roughly constant amount of power, so at low wind speeds a larger fraction is lost; (d) at high wind speeds the pump may not keep up with the rotor (flow limitation), flattening the curve. The net effect is a curve that is flat below cut-in, rises steeply just above cut-in, and then bends over — flatter than the ideal cubic.
3. **Q:** Your cut-in speed is 2.5 m/s. A commercial wind pump in the same area has a cut-in speed of 2.0 m/s. Explain why a lower cut-in speed is valuable, and calculate how many more hours per day the commercial pump can run if the wind is between 2.0 and 2.5 m/s for 15% of the day.
   **Worked Answer:** A lower cut-in speed means the windmill starts pumping at lower wind speeds, so it runs for more hours per day — useful in areas where wind is often light. If the wind is between 2.0 and 2.5 m/s for 15% of the day, the commercial pump (cut-in 2.0) runs during that time while your windmill (cut-in 2.5) does not. 15% of 24 hours = 0.15 × 24 = 3.6 hours per day. So the commercial pump runs about 3.6 extra hours per day. Over a week that is 25.2 extra hours of pumping — a significant advantage in a marginal-wind area, which is why reducing friction and blade mass to lower the cut-in speed is one of the most valuable improvements a windmill designer can make.

## Safety Notes
- This lesson is mostly indoor data work — no spinning blades. Ensure pencils and rulers are handled normally.
- If teams go outside to collect additional data points, maintain the 2 m safety perimeter around the windmill as in Lesson 313.
- Pins used to display graphs on the board should be collected and stored after the lesson — loose pins are a puncture hazard.
- Do not stand on chairs to pin graphs high on the board — use a step stool or pin at shoulder height.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners need the compiled log sheets from Lessons 313 and 314. They should know how to plot points on graph paper (from mathematics) and have met the wind power formula ½ρAv³ in Lesson 313.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on compiling the table, plotting points, and identifying the cut-in speed visually. Ages 14–17 do the curve fitting and the cubic-vs-linear analysis in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: squared exercise book pages substitute for graph paper. The anemometer card from Lesson 302 (Beaufort scale with m/s) should be reused. Coloured pencils are common in Kenyan classrooms; if unavailable, use dashed vs solid lines in pencil.
- The key insight is that the curve is NOT a straight line — it bends upward because of the v³ wind power relationship. Many learners will expect a straight line; the Explore block's comparison of team graphs should reveal the consistent upward bend.
- The cut-in speed is the most important number on the graph. It tells the designer how many hours per day the windmill can run. Save the graphs — they are the centrepiece of the engineering report in Lesson 316.
