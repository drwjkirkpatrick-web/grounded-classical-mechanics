# Lesson 316: Windmill Project VIII: Engineering Report

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** windmill

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Present a windmill project to an audience using a poster that covers design, data, failures, fixes, and costs.
2. Explain the engineering decisions behind the windmill using evidence from the test logs and performance graphs.
3. Compare the windmill's water output with the cost and effort of carrying water by jerrican.
4. Respond to audience questions with specific data from the project log.

## Materials
- Windmill and pump (for live demonstration if wind permits)
- Performance graph from Lesson 315
- Test logs from Lessons 313 and 314
- Poster paper or large sheets of card
- Coloured markers or pencils
- Cost sheet (materials list with prices in KSh)
- Camera or phone for documentation
- A jerrican (20 L) for the cost comparison demonstration
- Stopwatch (for the live demo, if wind permits)
- Measuring jar (for the live demo, if wind permits)

## Vocabulary
- **engineering report** (ripoti ya uhandisi) — a structured presentation of a design, its test data, failures, fixes, and costs
- **costing** (gharama) — a list of all materials, their prices, and the total cost of the project
- **failure mode** (njia ya kushindwa) — a specific way the design failed during testing (e.g., blade stall, pump leak, hub slip)
- **jerrican comparison** (linganisho la jerrican) — comparing the windmill's water output with the labour of carrying the same volume by hand
- **design brief** (muhtasari wa ubunifu) — the original goal the windmill was built to meet
- **evidence** (ushahidi) — specific data from the logs that supports a claim about the windmill's performance

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Name two things an engineering report must include besides the final design.
2. Your windmill lifts 200 L of water per day. A jerrican holds 20 L. How many jerrican-trips per day does the windmill save?
3. Why is it important to report failures and fixes, not just successes?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Any two of: test data (the performance graph), failures and how they were fixed, costs (materials and time), and a comparison with an alternative (e.g., carrying water by hand).
2. 200 L ÷ 20 L per jerrican = 10 jerrican-trips per day saved.
3. Because failures and fixes are where the real engineering learning happens — they show what does not work and why, so the next design can avoid the same problems. A report that only lists successes is not honest and does not help others learn.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a jerrican and ask: *how long does it take to carry this 20 L from the river to the house?* Take answers (typically 15–30 minutes per trip depending on distance). Then ask: *if our windmill pumps 200 L in a day, how many trips does it save?* Board the calculation. Introduce the engineering report as the way we tell the whole story — design, data, failures, fixes, and costs — so someone else could build and improve on our work.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teams assemble their posters. Each poster has five sections: (1) Design — a labelled sketch of the windmill with key dimensions; (2) Data — the performance graph from Lesson 315 with the cut-in speed marked; (3) Failures and Fixes — a list of what went wrong (from the logs) and what was tried; (4) Costs — a table of materials, quantities, and prices in KSh, with a total; (5) Comparison — water per day from the windmill vs jerrican-trips saved. Teams pin the poster next to their windmill. If a jua kali guest is present, they circulate and ask questions.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — each team gives a 90-second presentation covering the five sections. The teacher or guest asks one question per team that requires citing specific data (e.g., *what was your cut-in speed and how do you know?*). Confront the misconception that *a good report only shows the best result* — the honest report shows the failures and the data that did not improve, because that is what makes the report trustworthy and useful.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *an engineering report tells the whole story — design, data, failures, fixes, costs — so others can build on your work*. Homework: write a one-page summary of the windmill project for a student who was absent for the whole unit — what was built, how it was tested, what was learned.

## Hands-On Build: Engineering Report Poster
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Poster paper or large card
- Coloured markers or pencils
- Performance graph from Lesson 315
- Test logs from Lessons 313 and 314
- Cost sheet (materials and prices in KSh)
- Ruler and pencil for the design sketch
- The windmill itself (displayed next to the poster)

### Workflow
1. Divide the poster into five sections with a ruler: Design (top left), Data (top right), Failures and Fixes (middle), Costs (bottom left), Comparison (bottom right). Write the team name and "Windmill Engineering Report" as the title. **Checkpoint:** all five sections are outlined with headings before filling in any content.
2. In the Design section, draw a labelled sketch of the windmill: rotor diameter, tower height, blade count, pump stroke length, and head. Label the key parts (rotor, hub, shaft, crank, pump rod, cylinder, valves). **Checkpoint:** the sketch has at least four labelled parts and the key dimensions.
3. In the Data section, attach or copy the performance graph from Lesson 315. Mark the cut-in speed and the best output. Write one sentence: "Cut-in speed = ___ m/s; best output = ___ mL/min at ___ m/s." **Checkpoint:** the graph is attached and the cut-in speed and best output are stated with numbers.
4. In the Failures and Fixes section, list at least two failures from the logs (e.g., "hub slipped at high wind — fixed by tightening the bolt and adding a second nut") and the fix tried. Include any fix that did NOT work. **Checkpoint:** at least two failures are listed with their fixes, and at least one is honestly noted as "not fixed" or "fix did not work."
5. In the Costs section, make a table: material | quantity | unit price (KSh) | total. Include everything: wood, blades, fasteners, pipe, wire, wax, paint. Sum the total. Add a row for "build hours" with the team's estimate of total construction time. **Checkpoint:** the table has at least five materials and a total cost in KSh.
6. In the Comparison section, state the windmill's daily output (from the best sustained trial, extrapolated to a full day) and the number of 20 L jerrican-trips it saves. Show the calculation. **Checkpoint:** the comparison states both the windmill output and the jerrican-trips saved, with the calculation shown.
7. Pin the poster next to the windmill. Prepare a 90-second talk covering all five sections. **Checkpoint:** each team member can name the section they will present.

### Common Mistakes
- **Only showing successes** — a report that hides failures is not trustworthy. List what went wrong and what was tried, including fixes that failed.
- **No numbers in the data section** — "it worked well" is not data. State the cut-in speed, the best output, and the wind speed at which it was measured.
- **Missing costs** — the costing must include everything, even scavenged items (state "0 KSh — found" for free materials). A report without costs cannot be compared with alternatives.
- **Illegible sketch** — the design sketch must be neat and labelled. If the audience cannot read it, the report fails its purpose.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Name two things an engineering report must include besides the final design. (mirrors pre-test)
2. Your windmill lifts 200 L of water per day. A jerrican holds 20 L. How many jerrican-trips per day does the windmill save?
3. Why is it important to report failures and fixes, not just successes?
4. A team's poster says "our windmill works great" but shows no graph, no numbers, and no failure list. Give two specific reasons this report is weak.
5. Challenge: your windmill cost 1,500 KSh in materials and took 20 hours to build. It pumps 150 L per day. A family currently carries 150 L by jerrican, taking 30 minutes per trip (7.5 trips). Is the windmill worth it? Justify with numbers.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Any two of: test data (performance graph), failures and fixes, costs (materials and time), comparison with an alternative (jerrican carrying).
2. 200 L ÷ 20 L per jerrican = 10 jerrican-trips per day saved.
3. Failures and fixes are where the real learning happens — they show what does not work and why, so the next design avoids the same problems. A report that only lists successes is not honest and does not help others learn.
4. (a) No data — without the graph and numbers, the audience cannot judge whether the windmill actually performs well or what wind speed it needs. (b) No failure list — without failures and fixes, the audience cannot learn what to avoid or what improvements to try. (c) No costs — without a costing, the audience cannot compare the windmill with alternatives. Any two are valid.
5. The family saves 7.5 trips × 30 minutes = 225 minutes = 3 hours 45 minutes of labour per day. Over a month (30 days), that is 112.5 hours of labour saved. The windmill cost 1,500 KSh and 20 hours to build. In terms of labour alone, the windmill pays for itself in time saved within the first month (20 build hours vs 112.5 hours saved per month). Whether the 1,500 KSh is worth it depends on the family's income, but the labour savings are substantial. A reasonable answer: yes, the windmill is worth it — the 20 hours of build time are recovered in less than a week of saved carrying time, and the 1,500 KSh is a one-time cost against ongoing daily labour. The answer should cite the numbers to justify the conclusion.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your windmill cost 1,800 KSh in materials and took 24 hours to build across four lessons. It pumps an average of 180 L per day. A jerrican carries 20 L and a round trip to the river takes 25 minutes. Calculate the daily labour saved, the payback time in days (labour only), and the cost per litre of water over a 1-year period.
   **Worked Answer:** Daily jerrican-trips saved = 180 ÷ 20 = 9 trips. Daily labour saved = 9 × 25 min = 225 min = 3.75 hours. Payback time (labour only) = 24 build hours ÷ 3.75 hours/day = 6.4 days. Cost per litre over 1 year: 1,800 KSh ÷ (180 L/day × 365 days) = 1,800 ÷ 65,700 = 0.027 KSh per litre. After the first year the marginal cost per litre is near zero (only maintenance), while jerrican carrying costs continue indefinitely in labour.
2. **Q:** A jua kali guest asks: "Your windmill pumps 180 L/day, but a commercial wind pump costs 45,000 KSh and pumps 2,000 L/day. Which is the better investment for a village of 50 families needing 5,000 L/day total?" Justify with calculations.
   **Worked Answer:** Village need: 50 families × 100 L/day each = 5,000 L/day. Option A — your windmills: each pumps 180 L/day, so 5,000 ÷ 180 = 28 windmills needed. Cost = 28 × 1,800 = 50,400 KSh. Build time = 28 × 24 = 672 hours. Option B — one commercial pump: 45,000 KSh, pumps 2,000 L/day, so you would need 5,000 ÷ 2,000 = 2.5, meaning 3 commercial pumps. Cost = 3 × 45,000 = 135,000 KSh. Comparison: 28 student windmills cost 50,400 KSh vs 3 commercial pumps at 135,000 KSh. The student windmills are cheaper in cash (50,400 vs 135,000) but require 672 hours of build labour and ongoing maintenance of 28 separate machines. The commercial pumps cost 2.7× more in cash but are fewer units to maintain and likely more reliable. The best answer depends on available cash vs available labour: if the village has more labour than cash, the student windmills are the better investment; if cash is available, the commercial pumps are more reliable per litre. A strong answer presents both sides with the numbers.
3. **Q:** Your performance graph shows output plateauing (levelling off) at wind speeds above 5 m/s, even though the wind power formula predicts continued cubic growth. Give two physical reasons for the plateau and suggest one design change that might raise the plateau.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) The pump is flow-limited — the cylinder and valves can only move water so fast per stroke, so even if the rotor spins faster, the pump cannot deliver more water per minute; excess rotor speed is wasted. (b) The rotor feathers or stalls — at high wind speeds the blades may twist or the angle of attack changes, causing the rotor to lose efficiency (aerodynamic stall) rather than capture more power. One design change: increase the pump cylinder diameter or the stroke length so the pump can move more water per cycle, raising the flow limit. Alternatively, add a second pump cylinder in parallel. Alternatively, redesign the blades with a fixed, optimised pitch that maintains the angle of attack across a wider speed range. The key insight is that the plateau is set by the pump, not the rotor — improving the rotor alone will not raise the plateau if the pump is the bottleneck.

## Safety Notes
- If a live outdoor demonstration is included, maintain the 2 m safety perimeter as in Lesson 313.
- Pins and tacks used to mount posters should be collected after the lesson — loose pins are a puncture hazard.
- Do not stand on chairs to pin posters high on the board — use a step stool or pin at shoulder height.
- The jerrican demonstration involves lifting 20 L of water — this is heavy. Do not require learners to lift it; the teacher may demonstrate, or use an empty jerrican for the visual.
- If a jua kali guest is present, ensure they are introduced and briefed on the safety perimeter before any outdoor demonstration.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners need the completed windmill, the performance graph from Lesson 315, and the test logs from Lessons 313 and 314. The costing should be compiled from the materials lists across the windmill build lessons (309–312).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the poster build, the labelled design sketch, and stating the cut-in speed and best output. Ages 14–17 do the full costing, the jerrican comparison calculation, and the investment comparison in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: poster paper can be brown wrapping paper or the back of a calendar. Coloured markers or pencils are standard in Kenyan classrooms. A jua kali guest (local metalworker or mechanic) adds enormous value — invite one if possible, even for 15 minutes. If no guest is available, another teacher or the head teacher can serve as the audience.
- This is the capstone of the windmill project (Lessons 309–316). The posters and presentations should be photographed for the school's records and for the Unit 14 showcase in Lesson 328.
- The costing is the most commonly omitted section. Insist that teams include it — even scavenged materials should be listed as "0 KSh — found." The comparison with jerrican carrying is what makes the project real to the community: it translates litres per day into hours of labour saved.
- Celebrate the completion of the project. Every team that built, tested, iterated, and reported has done genuine engineering.
