# Lesson 317: Water Power: Head and Flow

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-4.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that falling water stores gravitational potential energy E = mgh.
2. Define head (vertical drop) and flow (volume per second) and explain how both contribute to power.
3. State the hydraulic power formula P = ρgQh and compute it for a real stream.
4. Describe micro-hydro reality in Kenya's highlands and explain the head-vs-flow trade for power.

## Materials
- Map of Kenya showing the highlands (Aberdares, Mt Kenya, Mau, Elgeyo Escarpment)
- A jerrican and a step (for a teacher demo: lift jerrican = energy, pour = power)
- A tape measure
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **head** (kimo) — the vertical distance the water falls; measured in metres. More head = more pressure = more power.
- **flow** (mtiririko) — the volume of water passing per second; measured in litres per second (L/s). More flow = more mass = more power.
- **hydraulic power** (nguvu ya maji) — the rate at which falling water can do work: P = ρgQh
- **micro-hydro** (mikio hidro) — a small water turbine (usually under 100 kW) serving a home, school, or village

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A full jerrican (10 kg) sits on a shelf 3 m high. How much gravitational energy does it have?
2. If you pour the jerrican out in 10 seconds, what is the power?
3. To get more power from a stream, what two things can you increase?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. E = mgh = 10 × 10 × 3 = 300 J. (Using g ≈ 10 m/s².)
2. P = E / t = 300 / 10 = 30 W. The power is 30 W — enough to light 3 LED bulbs.
3. Head (the height of the fall) and flow (the volume per second). P = ρgQh: increase h (build a dam or pipe the water downhill) or increase Q (use a bigger stream or divert more water).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — teacher demo: lift a jerrican onto a step. *This is stored energy — E = mgh.* Pour it out slowly. *This is power — energy per second.* Now: imagine a stream flowing off a 10 m cliff — that's a jerrican every second, all day, all night. Board: **water power = head × flow × g.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — class works through three scenarios on the board. **Scenario 1: Low head, high flow.** A wide slow river on flat land: h = 1 m, Q = 100 L/s. P = 1000 × 10 × 0.1 × 1 = 1000 W = 1 kW. **Scenario 2: High head, low flow.** A mountain spring on a cliff: h = 50 m, Q = 2 L/s. P = 1000 × 10 × 0.002 × 50 = 1000 W = 1 kW. **Scenario 3: Both moderate.** A highland stream: h = 10 m, Q = 5 L/s. P = 1000 × 10 × 0.005 × 10 = 500 W. Learners compute each and notice: 1 kW can come from low-head/high-flow OR high-head/low-flow. The trade: high head is easier to pipe (small pipe, high pressure) but harder to find (need a hill or cliff). Low head is common but needs a big wheel or turbine and a lot of water. Kenya's highlands (Aberdares, Mt Kenya, Elgeyo Escarpment) have both head and flow — ideal for micro-hydro.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The formula P = ρgQh is the hydraulic power — the maximum available from the falling water. A real turbine captures 50–80% of this (the rest is lost to friction, turbulence, and generator inefficiency). The key design trade: **high head + low flow** is cheaper to build (small pipe, small turbine, less water needed) but requires a steep site. **Low head + high flow** needs a big wheel, a lot of water, and a large structure (a dam or weir) — more expensive. Confront the misconception that "any stream can power a school" — no, the stream must have enough head × flow. A flat, slow stream (h = 0.5 m, Q = 10 L/s) gives P = 50 W — enough for a phone charger, not a school. Also address: "head is the distance from the source to the turbine" — no, head is the vertical drop, not the pipe length. A 100 m pipe on a 5° slope has a head of 100 × sin(5°) ≈ 8.7 m, not 100 m. Measure head with a water level (Unit 7) or a GPS, not by pacing the pipe length.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *water power = head × flow × g — high head or high flow, both work, but you need enough of at least one.* Homework: if a stream near your school has a 3 m drop and flows at 5 L/s, what is the available power? What could it run?

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A full jerrican (10 kg) sits on a shelf 3 m high. How much energy does it have? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If you pour it out in 10 seconds, what is the power? (mirrors pre-test)
3. To get more power from a stream, what two things can you increase? (mirrors pre-test)
4. A stream has a head of 20 m and a flow of 1 L/s. What is the available hydraulic power?
5. Challenge: explain why high-head/low-flow is often cheaper to build than low-head/high-flow.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. E = mgh = 10 × 10 × 3 = 300 J.
2. P = E / t = 300 / 10 = 30 W.
3. Head (h) and flow (Q). P = ρgQh: increase the drop height or the volume per second.
4. P = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 0.001 × 20 = 200 W. (1 L/s = 0.001 m³/s.)
5. High-head/low-flow uses a small pipe (low flow = small diameter = cheaper) and a small turbine (high pressure = compact Pelton or Turgo wheel). Low-head/high-flow needs a large pipe or channel (high flow = big diameter = expensive), a large wheel (undershot or Kaplan), and often a dam or weir to create the head — a major civil engineering cost. The high-head system is compact and piped; the low-head system is large and open. For the same power, the high-head system is usually cheaper — if you can find the head.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A stream in the Aberdares has a flow of 2 L/s and falls 5 m. What is the available hydraulic power? If a micro-turbine is 60% efficient, what electrical power is delivered? What could it run?
   **Worked Answer:** Available power: P = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 0.002 × 5 = 100 W. Electrical power: P_elec = 100 × 0.60 = 60 W. That's enough to light 6 LED bulbs (10 W each), or charge 12 phones (5 W each), or run a small radio (20 W) plus 4 LED bulbs (40 W). Not enough for a computer (300 W) or a fridge (150 W). For a rural school, 60 W is transformative: lighting for evening study and phone charging for teachers.
2. **Q:** A micro-hydro site has two options. Option A: pipe the water 200 m along a 15° slope to a turbine at the bottom. Option B: pipe it 50 m along a 30° slope to a turbine halfway down. The stream flow is 3 L/s. Which gives more power, and by how much?
   **Worked Answer:** Option A: head = 200 × sin(15°) = 200 × 0.259 = 51.8 m. P_A = 1000 × 10 × 0.003 × 51.8 = 1554 W. Option B: head = 50 × sin(30°) = 50 × 0.5 = 25 m. P_B = 1000 × 10 × 0.003 × 25 = 750 W. Option A gives more than double the power (1554 vs 750 W) because the head is more than double (51.8 vs 25 m). The longer pipe costs more and has more friction loss, but the extra head more than compensates. The trade: Option A needs a longer, more expensive pipe, but the power gain is worth it if the pipe friction loss is less than the extra head gained. (Friction loss in a 200 m, 1-inch pipe at 3 L/s is about 5 m of head, so the net head is 51.8 − 5 = 46.8 m, and P_A = 1404 W — still almost double Option B.)
3. **Q:** Design a micro-hydro system for a village needing 500 W of electricity. The nearest stream has a flow of 5 L/s. What head is needed? What pipe diameter is recommended (keep friction loss below 10% of head)?
   **Worked Answer:** P = ρgQh × η. At 60% turbine efficiency: 500 = 1000 × 10 × 0.005 × h × 0.6 → h = 500 / (1000 × 10 × 0.005 × 0.6) = 500 / 30 = 16.7 m. The head needed is about 17 m. With 10% friction allowance: the gross head should be 17 / 0.9 = 18.9 m ≈ 19 m. Pipe selection: for 5 L/s (0.005 m³/s), a 2-inch (50 mm) PVC pipe has a flow velocity of v = Q/A = 0.005 / (π × 0.025²) = 0.005 / 0.00196 = 2.55 m/s. This is within the recommended range (1–3 m/s). Friction loss in a 50 mm pipe at 5 L/s is about 5 m per 100 m of pipe. If the pipe run is 100 m (along a 10° slope for 19 m head), the friction loss is 5 m — which is 26% of the head, too much. A 3-inch (75 mm) pipe reduces the loss to about 1.5 m per 100 m — 8% of head, acceptable. So the design: 19 m gross head, 100 m of 3-inch pipe, 5 L/s flow, 60% efficient turbine, delivering 500 W. Cost estimate: 100 m of 3-inch PVC pipe + a small Pelton turbine + generator ≈ KSh 150,000–300,000. This is realistic for a village micro-hydro project in the Kenyan highlands.

## Safety Notes
- No hands-on build in this lesson — safety risks are minimal.
- If demonstrating the jerrican pour, do it over a drain or outside — water on the floor is a slip hazard.
- Do not attempt to visit a real micro-hydro site without permission and supervision. Mountain streams are dangerous (flash floods, slippery rocks).

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 301–316 covered wind power. This lesson pivots to water power. The formula P = ρgQh is the water equivalent of P = ½ρAv³ for wind.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 compute power for the three scenarios and understand "head = height, flow = amount." Ages 14–17 should design a full micro-hydro system including pipe sizing and friction loss.
- The key insight is that head and flow are interchangeable up to a point: you can trade one for the other and get the same power. But the cost changes: high head is cheaper to pipe (small pipe) but harder to find; low head is common but expensive to build (big wheel, big channel).
- Kenya's highlands are ideal for micro-hydro: the Aberdares, Mt Kenya, Mau Forest, Elgeyo Escarpment, and Nyambene Hills all have steep streams with both head and flow. Several community micro-hydro projects exist (e.g., Kirinyaga, Meru, Nyeri).
- This lesson supports the water turbine project (Lessons 319–320) and the design brief (Lesson 327), where teams may choose water power for their school.
