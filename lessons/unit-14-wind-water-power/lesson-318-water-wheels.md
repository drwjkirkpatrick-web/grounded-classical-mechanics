# Lesson 318: Water Wheels: Undershot and Overshot

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe the two classic water wheel types: undershot (water flows underneath) and overshot (water pours over the top).
2. Explain why overshot wheels are more efficient — they harvest the weight (gravitational PE) of the water, not just its speed (kinetic energy).
3. Build and test model undershot and overshot wheels and compare their performance.
4. Relate water wheel efficiency to the energy forms each type captures.

## Materials
- Two plastic bottles (1.5 L, with caps — for wheel bodies)
- Card stock or plastic sheets (for blades/scoops)
- Scissors
- Wire or a nail (for the axle)
- A tray or shallow pan (for the undershot test)
- A jug or bottle (for pouring water over the top — overshot test)
- String and small weights (to measure the lifting power of each wheel)
- A stopwatch
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **undershot wheel** (gurudumu la chini) — a water wheel where the water flows underneath the wheel, pushing the lower blades; uses the kinetic energy of the moving water
- **overshot wheel** (gurudumu la juu) — a water wheel where the water pours over the top, filling buckets that weigh down one side; uses the gravitational potential energy of the water
- **kinetic energy** (nishati ya mwendo) — the energy of motion; harvested by the undershot wheel
- **gravitational potential energy** (nishati ya kimo) — the energy of height; harvested by the overshot wheel

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. An undershot wheel is pushed by water flowing underneath. What kind of energy does it harvest?
2. An overshot wheel has water poured over the top. What kind of energy does it harvest?
3. Which type of wheel is more efficient, and why?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Kinetic energy — the energy of the moving water. The water's speed pushes the blades.
2. Gravitational potential energy — the energy of the water's weight at height. The water fills buckets and its weight turns the wheel.
3. The overshot wheel is more efficient because it harvests the weight (gravitational PE) of the water, which is typically much larger than the kinetic energy. For the same water: PE = mgh (where h is the wheel diameter) vs KE = ½mv² (where v is the stream speed). For a 2 m wheel and a 1 m/s stream: PE/KE = (gh) / (½v²) = (10 × 2) / (0.5 × 1) = 40. The overshot wheel has 40× more energy available — but in practice it captures a fraction of this, so the efficiency ratio is more like 2–3×.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show two images (or draw on the board): an undershot wheel (blades dipping into a stream) and an overshot wheel (water pouring over the top). Ask: *which one gets more energy from the same water?* Board: **undershot = speed; overshot = weight.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build two model wheels and test them (see Hands-On Build). They build an undershot wheel (flat blades on a bottle, tested in a tray with water poured past it) and an overshot wheel (scoop-shaped blades, tested with water poured over the top). For each, they measure: (a) does it spin? (b) how fast (turns in 10 s)? (c) how much weight can it lift (thread + beads)? Results go in a table: wheel type | turns/10 s | max beads lifted | observations.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The overshot wheel is more efficient because it captures the gravitational PE of the water (mgh, where h is the wheel height), while the undershot only captures the KE (½mv², where v is the stream speed). For a typical stream (v ≈ 1 m/s) and a typical wheel (h ≈ 2 m): PE = m × 10 × 2 = 20m J. KE = ½m × 1² = 0.5m J. The PE is 40× the KE. Real overshot wheels achieve 60–80% efficiency; undershot wheels 20–40%. Confront the misconception that "the undershot wheel is simpler, so it's almost as good" — no, the energy difference is huge. The overshot captures the weight of the water; the undershot only captures its speed. For the same water, the overshot does 2–4× more work. Also address: "why not always use overshot?" — because the overshot needs a way to get water to the top (a mill race, a leat, a small aqueduct). If the site has a natural drop (a waterfall or a steep slope), the overshot is easy. If the site is flat (a slow river), you can't get water to the top without pumping — and then you're using energy to lift the water that the wheel harvests. On flat ground, the undershot is the only option.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *overshot captures weight, undershot captures speed — for the same water, weight wins.* Homework: draw both wheel types and label the energy form each harvests.

## Hands-On Build: Model Undershot and Overshot Wheels
*Build and compare the two classic water wheel types.*

### Parts
- Two plastic bottles (1.5 L)
- Card stock or plastic sheets
- Scissors
- Wire or a nail (for the axle)
- A tray or shallow pan
- A jug or bottle (for pouring)
- String and small weights (beads)
- A stopwatch
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. **Build the undershot wheel.** Cut a bottle in half horizontally. Cut 6 rectangular blades (5 cm × 3 cm) from card. Tape the blades flat around the cut edge of the bottle, all facing the same direction. Push a wire axle through the bottle cap and the base. Mount the axle on two supports (books or blocks) so the lower blades dip into the tray. **Checkpoint:** the undershot wheel spins when water flows past it in the tray.
2. **Test the undershot wheel.** Pour water from a jug along the tray so it flows past the lower blades. Count turns for 10 seconds. Then attach a thread with beads to the axle and see how many beads it can lift. **Checkpoint:** turns/10 s and max beads are recorded.
3. **Build the overshot wheel.** Cut the second bottle in half. Cut 6 scoop-shaped blades (curved like a spoon) from card. Tape them around the cut edge, scoop-side up, so water poured over the top fills them. Mount on the axle so the top is accessible from above. **Checkpoint:** the overshot wheel spins when water is poured over the top.
4. **Test the overshot wheel.** Pour water from a jug over the top of the wheel so it fills the scoops. Count turns for 10 seconds. Attach the thread and beads. See how many beads it can lift. **Checkpoint:** turns/10 s and max beads are recorded.
5. **Compare.** Which wheel spins faster? Which lifts more? Is the overshot more efficient? Record observations. **Checkpoint:** the comparison table is complete with conclusions.

### Common Mistakes
- **Blades on the undershot too flat** — if the blades are perfectly flat, the water just pushes them without catching. A slight angle (like a paddle) catches more water. But too much angle and the water bounces off.
- **Scoops on the overshot too shallow** — if the scoops are too shallow, the water pours out before the wheel turns. Deep scoops hold the water longer, transferring more weight.
- **Water poured too fast** — if the water is poured too fast, it splashes over the wheel without filling the scoops. Pour slowly and steadily.
- **Axle friction** — if the axle is too tight, the wheel doesn't spin freely. Use a smooth wire and lubricate with a drop of oil.
- **Not comparing fairly** — use the same amount of water for both tests (one jug-full). Pour at the same rate. The comparison is only valid if the water input is the same.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What kind of energy does the undershot wheel harvest? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What kind of energy does the overshot wheel harvest? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Which is more efficient, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your overshot model lifts 4 beads and your undershot model lifts 1. What is the efficiency ratio?
5. Challenge: why can't you use an overshot wheel on a flat, slow river?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Kinetic energy — the energy of the moving water's speed.
2. Gravitational potential energy — the energy of the water's weight at height.
3. The overshot. It harvests the weight (PE = mgh) of the water, which is much larger than the speed (KE = ½mv²) for typical streams and wheel sizes.
4. 4 beads / 1 bead = 4×. The overshot wheel is 4× more efficient in this model. (Real overshot wheels are 2–4× more efficient than undershot.)
5. An overshot wheel needs water delivered to the top. On a flat river, there is no natural way to get water above the wheel — you would need to pump it up, and the energy to pump is more than the wheel harvests. On flat ground, the undershot (or a modern Kaplan turbine) is the only practical option. Overshot wheels require a hill, a waterfall, or a mill race (a channel that brings water from upstream at a higher level).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A stream flows at 1.5 m/s. An undershot wheel with 8 blades, each 0.1 m², is placed in the stream. What is the available kinetic power? If the wheel is 30% efficient, what is the output power?
   **Worked Answer:** KE of the water hitting the blades: the mass flow rate = ρ × Q = ρ × A × v (where A is the total blade area and v is the stream speed). But more accurately, the power available is the rate of KE hitting the blades: P_KE = ½ × (mass flow rate) × v² = ½ × ρ × A × v × v² = ½ × ρ × A × v³. With A = 8 × 0.1 = 0.8 m², v = 1.5 m/s: P = 0.5 × 1000 × 0.8 × 1.5³ = 0.5 × 1000 × 0.8 × 3.375 = 1350 W. At 30% efficiency: P_out = 1350 × 0.3 = 405 W. (Note: this assumes the blades sweep the full flow — in reality, a wheel only captures a fraction of the stream's width.)
2. **Q:** An overshot wheel with diameter 3 m processes 20 L/s. The water enters at the top and exits at the bottom. What is the available gravitational power? If the wheel is 70% efficient, what is the output power? Compare with the undershot in Q1.
   **Worked Answer:** Available gravitational power: P = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 0.02 × 3 = 600 W. At 70% efficiency: P_out = 600 × 0.7 = 420 W. The overshot delivers 420 W from 20 L/s on a 3 m wheel. The undershot (Q1) delivers 405 W from a stream at 1.5 m/s. The powers are similar, but the overshot uses only 20 L/s while the undershot needs a fast stream. The key difference: the overshot's power comes from the head (3 m), while the undershot's comes from the speed (1.5 m/s). For the same power, the overshot needs less water but more height; the undershot needs more water but no height. This is why overshot wheels were preferred in hilly areas (plenty of head) and undershot wheels on flat rivers (plenty of flow, no head).
3. **Q:** A modern Pelton turbine replaces an overshot wheel. The Pelton has a head of 50 m and a flow of 2 L/s, and is 80% efficient. What is the output power? How does this compare with an overshot wheel of the same flow and 70% efficiency on a 3 m head? What does this tell you about the advantage of high head?
   **Worked Answer:** Pelton: P = ρgQh × η = 1000 × 10 × 0.002 × 50 × 0.8 = 800 W. Overshot: P = 1000 × 10 × 0.002 × 3 × 0.7 = 42 W. The Pelton produces 800 W from the same 2 L/s that the overshot wheel turns into 42 W — a 19× improvement. The difference is the head: 50 m vs 3 m. The Pelton captures the same gravitational PE but from 17× the height. This is why modern micro-hydro uses high-head turbines (Pelton, Turgo) piped from mountain streams — the high pressure at the nozzle makes a small, fast, efficient turbine. The overshot wheel is a low-head, low-efficiency ancestor; the Pelton is its high-head, high-efficiency descendant. The principle is the same (gravitational PE → mechanical work), but the scale of the head determines the power.

## Safety Notes
- Water on the floor is a slip hazard — work over a tray or outside. Clean up spills immediately.
- Scissors: supervise cutting. Collect when not in use.
- The wire axle is sharp — hold by the bottle, not the wire.
- Do not pour water near electrical equipment (the fan from previous lessons, laptops, etc.).
- The wheels can spin fast — keep fingers clear of the blades.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 317 introduced the power formula P = ρgQh. This lesson contrasts two historical methods of capturing that power — the undershot and overshot wheel. The key physics is the difference between kinetic energy (½mv²) and gravitational potential energy (mgh).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 build and test both wheels, observing that the overshot lifts more. Ages 14–17 should compute the available KE and PE for the same water and explain the efficiency difference quantitatively.
- The water wheel is the oldest form of water power — used in Roman times for grinding grain. The overshot wheel was the most efficient machine of the pre-industrial era (60–80%). The undershot was used where no head was available (flat rivers, tidal mills).
- The key insight is that the same water can yield very different amounts of energy depending on how you capture it. This is the foundation of all hydropower engineering: maximise the head (pipe the water from as high as possible) and match the turbine type to the head (Pelton for high head, Francis for medium, Kaplan for low).
- This lesson supports the turbine project (Lessons 319–320), where learners build a spoon-wheel turbine — a simple Pelton-type turbine. The spoon turbine is the modern descendant of the overshot wheel: it uses the water's momentum (from a high-head nozzle) to spin a compact wheel at high speed.
