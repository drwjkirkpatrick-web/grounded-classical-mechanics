# Lesson 319: Build: The Spoon Wheel Turbine I

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** water-turbine

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a working spoon-wheel turbine from a plastic bottle, spoons/scoops, and a wire axle.
2. Mount the turbine so it spins freely and can be loaded with a string-and-bobbin lifting mechanism.
3. Predict the lift rate from the cup volume and the rotational speed (rpm).
4. Identify the two main energy losses in the turbine (splash-out and bearing friction).

## Materials
- A plastic bottle (1.5 L or 2 L — the turbine body)
- 6–8 plastic spoons or bottle-top scoops (the turbine buckets)
- Wire or a straight nail (the axle, ~20 cm)
- String or thread (the lifting line)
- A small bobbin or pulley (or a bottle cap with a hole — to wind the string)
- Small weights (beads, washers, or bottle tops with stones)
- A drill or heated nail (to make holes in the bottle — teacher supervised)
- Tape or wire (to attach the spoons)
- A stand or support (two books or blocks to hold the axle)
- A jug or container of water (for testing — done in Lesson 320)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **turbine** (turbini) — a rotary engine that extracts energy from a flowing fluid; the spoon wheel is a simple impulse turbine
- **impulse turbine** (turbini ya nguvu) — a turbine where the water hits the buckets at high speed and the force of the impact turns the wheel (like a Pelton wheel)
- **bucket** (kikombe) — the scoop or cup on the turbine that catches the water jet
- **nozzle** (mdomo) — the narrow opening that converts water pressure into a fast jet (for this model, the jug spout)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A spoon-wheel turbine has 8 spoons, each holding 10 mL of water. The wheel spins at 60 rpm (1 rotation per second). What is the flow rate through the turbine in L/s?
2. Where does the energy to lift the weight come from?
3. Name two ways the turbine loses energy.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Volume per rotation: 8 × 10 = 80 mL = 0.08 L. At 1 rotation per second: Q = 0.08 L/s. (In practice, the spoons don't fill completely on every rotation, and some water splashes out, so the effective flow is lower.)
2. From the gravitational potential energy of the water. The water falls from the jug (at height) and hits the spoons. The impact transfers kinetic energy to the wheel, which does work (lifting the weight). The energy chain: gravitational PE → kinetic energy of the jet → rotational KE of the wheel → work done on the weight.
3. Any two correct answers: (a) splash-out — water bounces off the spoons without transferring all its energy; (b) bearing friction — the axle rubs on the supports, converting mechanical energy to heat; (c) water leaving the spoon still has some velocity (not all KE transferred); (d) turbulence — chaotic flow wastes energy; (e) windage — air resistance on the spinning wheel.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a spoon. Pour water onto it. It pushes the spoon down and sideways. Now imagine 8 spoons on a wheel — the water hits each in turn, spinning the wheel. Board: **water jet → spoons → spin → lift.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build the spoon-wheel turbine (see Hands-On Build). They attach 6–8 spoons around the bottle, mount it on the wire axle, and rig the string-and-bobbin lifting mechanism. They spin it by hand to check it runs freely. They predict: if each spoon holds 10 mL and the wheel spins at 2 rotations per second, what weight can it lift? (Testing with water is in Lesson 320 — this lesson is the build.) **Checkpoint:** the turbine spins freely on the axle and the lifting mechanism works (string winds onto the bobbin).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. The spoon wheel is a simple impulse turbine — the simplest type of water turbine. The water jet hits the spoons (buckets) at speed, and the impact force turns the wheel. This is the same principle as a Pelton wheel (used in real micro-hydro): a jet of water from a nozzle hits bucket-shaped blades, transferring kinetic energy. The energy chain: gravitational PE (water at height) → KE (water jet) → rotational KE (wheel) → work (lifting a weight). The two main losses: (1) splash-out — the water bounces off the spoons instead of transferring all its momentum, and (2) bearing friction — the axle rubs on the supports. Confront the misconception that "the spoons should fill with water and hold it" — no, this is an impulse turbine, not a reaction turbine. The water should hit the spoon and deflect sideways (like a Pelton bucket), not fill and stay. The spoon should be angled so the water enters, transfers its momentum, and exits to the side. Also address: "more spoons = more power" — not necessarily. More spoons mean each spoon is smaller and closer together, so the jet hits the next spoon before the previous one has cleared. The optimum is usually 6–12 spoons, depending on the jet size and wheel diameter.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the spoon wheel is a Pelton turbine in miniature — jet hits buckets, wheel spins, weight lifts.* Homework: draw the energy chain from water in the jug to the lifted weight, and label where each loss occurs.

## Hands-On Build: Spoon Wheel Turbine
*Build a working impulse turbine from a bottle, spoons, and wire.*

### Parts
- A plastic bottle (1.5 L or 2 L)
- 6–8 plastic spoons or bottle-top scoops
- Wire or a straight nail (~20 cm, as the axle)
- String or thread
- A small bobbin or pulley (or a bottle cap with a hole)
- Small weights (beads or washers)
- Tape or wire (to attach spoons)
- A drill or heated nail (to make holes — teacher supervised)
- A stand (two books or blocks)

### Workflow
1. **Prepare the bottle.** Remove the cap and base. The cylinder is the turbine body. The teacher drills or burns two holes: one in the cap end and one in the base, centered, for the axle. **Checkpoint:** the axle hole goes through the centre of both ends.
2. **Attach the spoons.** Tape or wire 6–8 spoons evenly around the outside of the bottle, all facing the same direction (so the concave side catches the water). The spoons should be angled slightly (about 20–30° from the tangent) so the water deflects sideways after impact. **Checkpoint:** all spoons face the same direction and are angled to deflect water.
3. **Thread the axle.** Push the wire through the axle holes in the bottle. The bottle should spin freely on the wire. **Checkpoint:** the turbine spins freely — no binding.
4. **Mount the turbine.** Rest the wire axle on two supports (books or blocks) so the turbine is horizontal and the spoons are accessible from above (for pouring water). **Checkpoint:** the turbine is mounted and spins freely.
5. **Rig the lifting mechanism.** Tie the string to the wire axle (or to a bobbin/pulley on the axle). Tie a bead or washer to the other end. When the turbine spins, the string should wind onto the axle and lift the weight. **Checkpoint:** the string winds onto the axle when the turbine is spun by hand, lifting the weight.
6. **Predict the lift rate.** If each spoon holds 10 mL and the wheel spins at 2 rotations per second (120 rpm), the flow rate is 8 × 10 × 2 = 160 mL/s = 0.16 L/s. The power available is P = ρgQh (where h is the height of the water jug above the wheel). Record the prediction. **Checkpoint:** the prediction is written down for comparison with the test in Lesson 320.

### Common Mistakes
- **Spoons facing different directions** — if some spoons face clockwise and others anticlockwise, the water pushes them in opposite directions and the wheel doesn't spin. All spoons must face the same way.
- **Spoons too flat** — if the spoons are flat against the bottle (0° angle), the water bounces off without transferring momentum. A 20–30° angle lets the water enter, transfer its momentum, and deflect sideways.
- **Axle too tight** — if the axle holes are too small, friction stops the wheel. Make the holes slightly larger than the wire and lubricate with a drop of oil.
- **String too thick** — thick string has high friction on the axle and doesn't wind properly. Use thin thread or fishing line.
- **Bobbin slipping** — if the bobbin is not fixed to the axle, it spins independently and doesn't lift the weight. Fix it with tape or glue.
- **Not enough spoons** — with only 3–4 spoons, the jet often misses all of them. 6–8 spoons ensure the jet always hits at least one.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. A turbine has 8 spoons holding 10 mL each, spinning at 1 rotation per second. What is the flow rate? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Where does the energy to lift the weight come from? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name two ways the turbine loses energy. (mirrors pre-test)
4. Why should the spoons be angled, not flat?
5. Challenge: what is the difference between an impulse turbine (like the spoon wheel) and a reaction turbine (like a submerged propeller)?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 8 × 10 = 80 mL per rotation. At 1 rotation/s: Q = 80 mL/s = 0.08 L/s.
2. From the gravitational potential energy of the water falling from the jug. The energy chain: gravitational PE → kinetic energy of the jet → rotational KE of the wheel → work done on the weight.
3. Any two: splash-out (water bounces off without transferring momentum), bearing friction (axle rubs on supports), water leaving with residual velocity, turbulence, windage (air resistance).
4. A flat spoon bounces the water off without transferring much momentum. An angled spoon lets the water enter, transfer its momentum to the spoon, and deflect sideways — like a Pelton bucket. The angle maximises the momentum transfer.
5. An impulse turbine has the water hit the buckets at high speed from a nozzle — the wheel spins in air, and only the buckets are in the jet. A reaction turbine is fully submerged — the water flows through the blades and the pressure difference (like a wing) creates the force. Impulse turbines are for high head (high pressure → fast jet); reaction turbines are for low head (high flow, low pressure). The spoon wheel is an impulse turbine; a propeller in a pipe is a reaction turbine.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your spoon turbine has 8 spoons, each with a cup volume of 12 mL. The water jug is 1 m above the wheel. The wheel spins at 90 rpm. Predict the flow rate and the available hydraulic power.
   **Worked Answer:** Volume per rotation: 8 × 12 = 96 mL = 0.096 L. At 90 rpm = 1.5 rotations/s: Q = 0.096 × 1.5 = 0.144 L/s = 1.44 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s. Available hydraulic power: P = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 1.44 × 10⁻⁴ × 1 = 1.44 W. (Note: this is the power from the water falling 1 m to the wheel. The jet speed at the spoon is v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 1) = 4.47 m/s. The KE per unit mass is ½v² = ½ × 20 = 10 J/kg — same as gh = 10 × 1 = 10 J/kg. So P = ṁ × gh = Q × ρ × g × h, confirming the calculation.)
2. **Q:** The two main losses in the spoon turbine are splash-out (water misses or bounces off the spoon) and bearing friction. If the splash-out is 40% (only 60% of the water transfers its momentum) and the bearing efficiency is 80%, what is the overall efficiency and the output power for Q1?
   **Worked Answer:** Overall efficiency: η = η_splash × η_bearing = 0.60 × 0.80 = 0.48 = 48%. Output power: P_out = P_available × η = 1.44 × 0.48 = 0.69 W. That's enough to lift a 10 g weight at about 7 cm/s (P = mg × v → v = P / mg = 0.69 / (0.01 × 10) = 6.9 m/s — wait, that seems too fast. Let me recheck. 10 g = 0.01 kg. F = mg = 0.1 N. v = P/F = 0.69/0.1 = 6.9 m/s. That's very fast — but the string winds onto the axle at the wheel's tangential speed, which is much less than 6.9 m/s for a small wheel at 90 rpm. The power is 0.69 W but the speed is limited by the wheel geometry. The torque is τ = P / ω = 0.69 / (90 × 2π/60) = 0.69 / 9.42 = 0.073 N·m. At a 2 cm axle radius: F = τ / r = 0.073 / 0.02 = 3.66 N. So the turbine can lift 3.66 N ≈ 370 g, but slowly: v = ω × r = 9.42 × 0.02 = 0.188 m/s = 18.8 cm/s. The power (0.69 W) = force × speed = 3.66 × 0.188 = 0.69 W. Confirmed.)
3. **Q:** A real Pelton turbine has a nozzle diameter of 10 mm and a head of 30 m. The jet speed is v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 30) = 24.5 m/s. The flow rate is Q = A × v = π × (0.005)² × 24.5 = 0.00192 m³/s = 1.92 L/s. The wheel diameter is 20 cm and the bucket deflects the jet by 165°. What is the force on the bucket and the power at 80% efficiency?
   **Worked Answer:** Force on the bucket (for a Pelton, the jet is deflected by ~165°, so the momentum change is nearly 2× the jet momentum): F = ρ × Q × Δv ≈ ρ × Q × 2v (for 180° deflection; 165° is slightly less). F = 1000 × 0.00192 × 2 × 24.5 = 1000 × 0.00192 × 49 = 94.1 N. (At 165°: the factor is 1 + cos(15°) = 1 + 0.966 = 1.966, so F = ρ × Q × 1.966 × v = 1000 × 0.00192 × 1.966 × 24.5 = 92.5 N. Close to the 180° approximation.) Wheel speed at the bucket radius (0.1 m): for maximum efficiency, the bucket moves at half the jet speed: v_bucket = 12.25 m/s. ω = v_bucket / R = 12.25 / 0.1 = 122.5 rad/s. RPM = 122.5 × 60 / (2π) = 1170 rpm. Torque: τ = F × R = 92.5 × 0.1 = 9.25 N·m. Power: P = τ × ω × η = 9.25 × 122.5 × 0.8 = 906 W. Check: P_available = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 0.00192 × 30 = 576 W. Hmm, the output (906 W) exceeds the available (576 W) — that's impossible. The error is that the bucket speed is half the jet speed, so the relative velocity is halved. The correct force should use the relative velocity: F = ρ × Q × (v_jet − v_bucket) × 1.966. F = 1000 × 0.00192 × (24.5 − 12.25) × 1.966 = 1000 × 0.00192 × 12.25 × 1.966 = 46.2 N. Power: P = F × v_bucket × η = 46.2 × 12.25 × 0.8 = 453 W. Available power: 576 W. Efficiency: 453 / 576 = 78.6%. Close to the 80% target — the small difference is due to the 165° vs 180° deflection. This is the correct Pelton calculation.

## Safety Notes
- The teacher operates the drill or heated nail for making holes — learners do not use the drill or heat source.
- The wire axle is sharp — hold by the bottle, not the wire ends. File or tape the wire ends.
- The turbine can spin fast when tested — keep fingers clear of the spoons and the string.
- Water testing is in Lesson 320 — do not pour water in this lesson. The build is dry.
- Do not use glass bottles — if they break, glass shards are dangerous. Use plastic only.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 317–318 introduced water power and the overshot/undershot wheels. This lesson builds the first turbine — a simple impulse turbine (Pelton-type). The spoon wheel is to a Pelton turbine what the pinwheel (Lesson 308) is to a wind turbine: a simple model that captures the essential physics.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 build the turbine and make it spin freely. Ages 14–17 should predict the lift rate and identify the losses.
- The key insight is that the spoon wheel is an impulse turbine: the water jet transfers momentum to the spoons, and the spoons deflect the water sideways. The water does not fill and weigh down the wheel (that's the overshot wheel from Lesson 318). This is a different energy transfer mechanism.
- The build is done in two lessons: this lesson (build) and Lesson 320 (test). Splitting the build and test gives time for the glue/tape to set and for learners to think about the predictions.
- The spoon wheel is the water-power equivalent of the windmill rotor — it is the energy-capturing device. The string-and-bobbin is the load. The test (Lesson 320) measures how much power the turbine can deliver to the load.
- If plastic spoons are not available, bottle tops can be used: cut a bottle top in half and tape the concave side to the bottle. The shape is less ideal than a spoon but works adequately.
