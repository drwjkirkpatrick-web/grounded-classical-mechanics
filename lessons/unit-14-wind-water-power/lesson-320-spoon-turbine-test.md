# Lesson 320: Build: The Spoon Wheel Turbine II — Test Under the Tap

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** water-turbine

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Test the spoon-wheel turbine under a controlled water flow (tap or hose) and measure the rotational speed (rpm).
2. Measure the maximum weight the turbine can lift and compute the mechanical power output.
3. Compare the output power with the input hydraulic power and identify where the losses occur.
4. Graph the results (rpm vs flow, power vs flow) and compare with other teams.

## Materials
- The spoon-wheel turbine built in Lesson 319
- A tap or hose (for the water jet — ideally adjustable flow)
- A stopwatch
- Small weights (beads, washers — 10 per group)
- A measuring jar (to measure the flow rate)
- Graph paper
- A ruler or tape measure (to measure the height of the water source above the wheel)
- A bucket or tray (to catch the runoff water)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **flow rate** (kiwango cha mtiririko) — the volume of water delivered per second; measured by collecting the runoff in a measuring jar for a known time
- **mechanical power** (nguvu ya mitambo) — the rate at which the turbine does useful work: P = F × v (force × lifting speed) or P = τ × ω (torque × angular speed)
- **efficiency** (uhalali) — the ratio of output power to input power: η = P_out / P_in
- **performance graph** (grafu ya utendaji) — a plot of turbine output vs input (e.g., power vs flow rate)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. How do you measure the flow rate from a tap using a measuring jar and a stopwatch?
2. Your turbine lifts a 50 g weight at 20 cm/s. What is the mechanical power output?
3. If the available hydraulic power is 2 W and the turbine lifts the weight with 0.6 W, what is the efficiency?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Hold the measuring jar under the tap for a known time (e.g., 10 seconds). Measure the volume collected. Flow rate Q = volume / time. For example: 200 mL in 10 s → Q = 20 mL/s = 0.02 L/s.
2. F = mg = 0.05 × 10 = 0.5 N. P = F × v = 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1 W. The mechanical power output is 0.1 W.
3. η = P_out / P_in = 0.6 / 2 = 0.30 = 30%. The efficiency is 30%.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the turbine from Lesson 319. Ask: *how fast does it spin? How much can it lift?* Board: **today we measure: rpm, flow, max load, power, efficiency.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups test the turbine under the tap (see Hands-On Build). They measure: (a) the flow rate (measuring jar + stopwatch), (b) the rpm (count turns for 10 s, multiply by 6), (c) the maximum weight lifted (add beads until the turbine stalls). They repeat at two different flow rates (tap on low and high). They compute the mechanical power output (P = m × g × v_lift, where v_lift is the lifting speed of the string = circumference × rpm). They compute the input hydraulic power (P = ρgQh, where h is the height from the tap to the wheel). They compute the efficiency. Results go on a class table. **Checkpoint:** every group has measured rpm, flow, max load, power, and efficiency at two flow rates.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Plot the class results on the board: rpm vs flow, power vs flow. The turbine spins faster at higher flow (more water → more momentum → more force). The power increases with flow, but not linearly — at high flow, the spoons can't absorb all the momentum and the efficiency drops (splash-out increases). The maximum power is at a moderate flow, not the maximum flow. Confront the misconception that "more water = more power always" — at very high flow, the water hits the spoons so fast that it bounces off without transferring momentum (splash-out). The turbine is most efficient at the flow rate where the spoons absorb the jet cleanly. Also address: "our turbine is only 20% efficient — it failed" — no, 20% is realistic for a spoon-wheel model. Real Pelton turbines achieve 80–90%, but they have precisely engineered buckets and nozzles. The spoon wheel is a model; the physics is the same, but the efficiency is lower due to imprecise spoon angles, bearing friction, and splash-out. The point is to understand the energy chain, not to match industrial efficiency.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the turbine converts water power to mechanical power — measure both and the difference is the losses.* Homework: graph the power vs flow rate for your turbine and write one sentence about where the energy goes.

## Hands-On Build: Turbine Test Rig
*Test the spoon-wheel turbine under a controlled water jet and measure performance.*

### Parts
- The spoon-wheel turbine (from Lesson 319)
- A tap or hose (adjustable flow)
- A stopwatch
- Small weights (10 beads or washers per group)
- A measuring jar (250 mL or 500 mL)
- Graph paper
- A ruler or tape measure
- A bucket or tray (to catch runoff)
- String and bobbin (already on the turbine from Lesson 319)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. **Mount the turbine.** Place the turbine on its supports under the tap (or hold the hose above it). Position the bucket underneath to catch the runoff. Measure the height h from the tap to the turbine wheel. **Checkpoint:** the turbine is mounted and the height is recorded.
2. **Measure the flow rate.** Hold the measuring jar under the tap for 10 seconds. Measure the volume. Q = volume / 10. Convert to L/s. **Checkpoint:** the flow rate is measured and recorded.
3. **Measure the rpm (no load).** Turn on the tap (or pour from the hose) so the jet hits the spoons. Count turns for 10 seconds. Multiply by 6 to get rpm. Repeat 3 times and average. **Checkpoint:** the free-spin rpm is recorded.
4. **Measure the max load.** Attach one bead to the string. Turn on the water. Does the turbine lift the bead? If yes, add another. Keep adding until the turbine stalls (cannot lift the next bead). Record the maximum number of beads. **Checkpoint:** the max bead count is recorded.
5. **Compute the power.** Mass lifted: m = (number of beads) × (mass per bead, measured on a balance or estimated). Lifting speed: v = circumference × rps = 2π × r_axle × (rpm / 60), where r_axle is the axle radius (or the bobbin radius). Power: P_out = m × g × v. **Checkpoint:** the output power is computed.
6. **Compute the efficiency.** Input power: P_in = ρgQh (where ρ = 1000, g = 10, Q is the flow rate in m³/s, h is the height in metres). Efficiency: η = P_out / P_in × 100%. **Checkpoint:** the efficiency is computed.
7. **Repeat at a different flow rate.** Turn the tap to high (or pour faster). Repeat steps 2–6. Compare. **Checkpoint:** results at two flow rates are in the table.
8. **Graph.** Plot rpm vs flow rate and power vs flow rate on graph paper. **Checkpoint:** both graphs are drawn with labelled axes.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not measuring the height (h)** — the input power depends on h (the height from the tap to the wheel). Without h, the efficiency cannot be computed. Measure from the tap to the centre of the wheel.
- **Confusing flow rate and speed** — the flow rate (Q, in L/s) is the volume per second. The jet speed (v) is the flow rate divided by the jet area (v = Q/A). They are different quantities. For the input power, use Q (flow rate): P_in = ρgQh.
- **Not converting units** — Q must be in m³/s for the formula (1 L/s = 0.001 m³/s). Mass must be in kg (1 g = 0.001 kg). Common error: using L/s directly in the formula without converting to m³/s, giving a result 1000× too large.
- **Measuring rpm at the wrong load** — the free-spin rpm (no load) is the maximum speed. The max-load rpm (just before stalling) is much lower. For power, use the rpm at the actual load being lifted, not the free-spin rpm.
- **The water missing the spoons** — if the jet does not hit the spoons squarely, the turbine doesn't spin well. Adjust the position of the turbine so the jet hits the spoons at the top, about 90° from the axle.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. How do you measure flow rate with a measuring jar and stopwatch? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Your turbine lifts 50 g at 20 cm/s. What is the power output? (mirrors pre-test)
3. If the input power is 2 W and the output is 0.6 W, what is the efficiency? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your turbine spins at 300 rpm free-spin but only 100 rpm when lifting 5 beads. Why does the speed drop?
5. Challenge: at high flow, the efficiency drops. Give two reasons why.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Hold the jar under the tap for a known time (e.g., 10 s). Measure the volume. Q = volume / time. Convert to L/s (or m³/s = L/s × 0.001).
2. F = mg = 0.05 × 10 = 0.5 N. P = F × v = 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1 W.
3. η = P_out / P_in = 0.6 / 2 = 30%.
4. The load (5 beads) requires force to lift. The turbine must do work against gravity, which takes energy from the rotational kinetic energy. The water jet provides a fixed amount of force; at the load, some force goes to lifting and less is available to maintain speed, so the rpm drops. The equilibrium speed is where the water force equals the load force plus friction.
5. Any two: (a) splash-out increases — at high flow, the jet hits the spoons so hard that water bounces off without transferring momentum. (b) The spoons can't clear the water fast enough — at high rpm, the spoons retain water from the previous hit, reducing the momentum transfer on the next hit. (c) Bearing friction increases with speed (roughly proportional to rpm). (d) Turbulence increases — the water flow becomes chaotic, wasting energy. (e) The jet spreads at high flow (lower velocity for the same flow if the nozzle is too wide), reducing the momentum per unit volume.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your turbine has an axle (bobbin) radius of 1.5 cm. It lifts 30 g at 120 rpm. The tap flow is 15 mL/s from a height of 0.8 m. Compute the output power, the input power, and the efficiency. Where does the lost energy go?
   **Worked Answer:** Lifting speed: v = 2π × r × (rpm/60) = 2π × 0.015 × (120/60) = 2π × 0.015 × 2 = 0.188 m/s. Force: F = mg = 0.03 × 10 = 0.3 N. Output power: P_out = F × v = 0.3 × 0.188 = 0.057 W. Input power: P_in = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 0.000015 × 0.8 = 0.12 W. Efficiency: η = 0.057 / 0.12 = 47.5%. The lost energy (52.5%) goes to: (a) splash-out — water bouncing off the spoons without transferring momentum (the largest loss, maybe 30%); (b) bearing friction — the axle rubbing on the supports (maybe 10%); (c) water leaving the spoons with residual velocity (not all KE transferred, maybe 10%); (d) turbulence and windage (maybe 2.5%).
2. **Q:** Compare two turbines: Team A has 6 spoons, Team B has 10 spoons (same bottle size, same tap flow). Team A spins at 200 rpm and lifts 3 beads. Team B spins at 140 rpm and lifts 4 beads. Which has more power? Which is more efficient?
   **Worked Answer:** Assume each bead = 2 g, axle radius = 1.5 cm. Team A: m = 6 g = 0.006 kg. v = 2π × 0.015 × (200/60) = 0.314 m/s. P_A = 0.006 × 10 × 0.314 = 0.019 W. Team B: m = 8 g = 0.008 kg. v = 2π × 0.015 × (140/60) = 0.220 m/s. P_B = 0.008 × 10 × 0.220 = 0.018 W. The powers are nearly equal (0.019 vs 0.018 W). Team A is faster but lifts less; Team B is slower but lifts more. The total power is similar because the available water energy is the same. Team A (6 spoons) is slightly more efficient — it has less turbulence and less mass to accelerate. This confirms the trade-off: the total power is fixed by the water, and the blade count trades speed for torque. The "best" turbine depends on the application: high speed (generator) or high torque (pump).
3. **Q:** Design an improved turbine based on your test data. State three specific changes and predict the effect of each on efficiency.
   **Worked Answer:** Change 1: **Replace spoons with curved buckets (Pelton-style).** Current spoons are flat and bounce water off. Curved buckets that deflect the water by ~165° transfer nearly 2× the momentum. Predicted effect: efficiency increases from ~20% to ~40–50%. Change 2: **Add a nozzle.** Currently the tap flow is uncontrolled — the water spreads as it falls. A nozzle (a small hole in a bottle cap, 5 mm diameter) creates a focused, high-speed jet that hits one spoon at a time. Predicted effect: efficiency increases by 10–20% because all the water hits the spoon instead of splashing around it. Change 3: **Reduce bearing friction.** Currently the wire axle rubs on the supports. Adding a smooth tube (a piece of pen barrel) as a bearing surface, or lubricating with oil, reduces friction. Predicted effect: efficiency increases by 5–10%. Combined: from a baseline of 20%, the improved turbine might reach 40–60% — approaching the efficiency of a real Pelton wheel. The test would confirm: measure the power and efficiency with each change, one at a time, using the same A/B method as the windmill project (Lesson 314).

## Safety Notes
- Water on the floor is a slip hazard — work over a tray or bucket. Clean up spills immediately.
- Do not let water splash onto electrical equipment (laptops, fans, power strips).
- The turbine can spin fast — keep fingers clear of the spoons and string.
- Weights can fly off the string if it breaks — use a knot, not tape, and keep the weight light (beads, not metal).
- Do not turn the tap on full — a moderate flow is sufficient and safer. High flow splashes more and wastes water.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 319 (build the turbine). This lesson is the test. The test applies the power formulas from Lesson 317 and the measurement skills from the windmill project (Lessons 313–315).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 measure rpm and max load and observe that more flow = more spin. Ages 14–17 should compute power, efficiency, and graph the results.
- The key insight is that the turbine's efficiency is low (20–40%) but the physics is correct — the energy chain is real. The losses are identifiable (splash-out, friction) and fixable (better buckets, nozzle, bearings). The advanced questions ask learners to design improvements — this is the engineering loop: build → test → identify losses → improve → retest.
- This lesson mirrors the windmill test day (Lesson 313) and iteration (Lesson 314): measure, compute, identify losses, improve. The pattern is the same for any energy device: windmill, turbine, solar panel, engine.
- The class results table and graphs are important — they show the range of efficiencies and the trade-offs between speed and torque across different designs. The comparison between teams is the assessment: which team understood the physics well enough to build the most efficient turbine?
- This lesson supports the design brief (Lesson 327), where teams may choose a water turbine for their school power system. The data from this lesson (flow, head, power, efficiency) is the evidence base for the design.
