# Lesson 321: From Motion to Electricity: The Dynamo Teaser

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that a generator (dynamo) converts mechanical rotation into electrical energy and that a motor is the same device run backwards.
2. Describe the essential parts of a simple generator: magnet, coil, and motion.
3. Predict that faster spinning produces a brighter bulb and relate this to greater mechanical power in.
4. Connect the dynamo to their windmill and water-wheel builds as the final link from stream or wind to useful electricity.

## Materials
- A bicycle dynamo with its headlight bulb (or a hand-crank generator demo unit)
- A bicycle mounted on a stand (or the wheel held so the dynamo can press against the tyre)
- Two short lengths of wire with crocodile clips
- A small DC motor (taken from a broken toy or radio) with a fan blade or wheel glued to the shaft
- An LED or a 1.5 V torch bulb
- A multimeter (if available) set to volts DC
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **dynamo** (jenereta) — a small generator that makes electricity when you spin it
- **generator** (jenereta) — a machine that turns mechanical motion into electrical energy
- **motor** (mota) — a machine that turns electrical energy into motion; a generator run backwards
- **coil** (kikubaji) — many turns of copper wire that the magnet spins inside
- **magnet** (sumaku) — a piece of iron that produces a magnetic field
- **current** (mkondo wa umeme) — the flow of electricity through a wire
- **voltage** (volti) — the push that drives the current, measured in volts

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*
1. Name one machine at home or in your village that uses electricity to make something move.
2. Where does the energy in a bicycle dynamo's bulb come from when you pedal?
3. If you spin a dynamo twice as fast, what do you expect to happen to the bulb?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Any electric motor example — e.g., a fan, a blender, a phone vibrator, a water pump, a washing machine.
2. From the mechanical work of your legs turning the wheel; the dynamo converts that motion into electricity.
3. The bulb gets brighter — more mechanical power in means more electrical power out.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the bicycle with the dynamo unlit; ask learners what they must do to make the bulb glow. Spin the wheel slowly, then fast; let them see the brightness change.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — build the demo: press the dynamo head against the tyre, spin the wheel, watch the bulb light. Then swap the bulb for the small DC motor spinning a fan blade connected to the LED; blow on the fan blade (or spin it by hand) and watch the LED glow. Try to measure the voltage with the multimeter at slow and fast spin.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board draw the energy chain: legs → wheel → dynamo → wire → bulb → light and heat. Confront the misconception that the dynamo "stores" electricity — it only makes current while it spins; stop spinning and the bulb goes dark immediately. State the key reversibility: a motor is a generator run backwards.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: every windmill and water wheel they have built is half a power station; the missing half is the dynamo. Home task: find one item at home with a motor inside and bring its name next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Dynamo and Bulb Light Test
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*
### Parts
- Bicycle dynamo with headlight bulb
- Bicycle on a stand (or wheel held firmly)
- Crocodile-clip wires
- Small DC motor with fan blade
- LED
- Multimeter (optional)

### Workflow
1. Press the dynamo head against the bicycle tyre so it contacts firmly — **checkpoint:** the head sits square, not skewed.
2. Spin the wheel slowly by hand and watch the bulb — **checkpoint:** bulb glows dim or flickers.
3. Spin faster and note the brightness change — **checkpoint:** bulb is clearly brighter.
4. Disconnect the bulb, clip the LED to the small DC motor terminals, and spin the motor shaft by hand or by blowing the fan blade — **checkpoint:** LED flashes.
5. If a multimeter is available, touch the probes to the dynamo terminals at slow and fast spin and record the two voltages — **checkpoint:** fast-spin voltage is clearly higher.

### Common Mistakes
- Dynamo head not touching the tyre squarely — the wheel slips and the bulb stays dark. Fix: re-seat the head so it presses firmly and squarely.
- LED connected backwards — LEDs only light one way. Fix: swap the two wires.
- Holding the dynamo loosely — it jumps off the tyre. Fix: clamp or hold it steady against the rim.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*
1. Where does the energy come from when a bicycle dynamo lights its bulb?
2. What happens to the bulb if you spin the dynamo faster? Why?
3. A motor and a generator are the same machine. What is the difference in how you use it?
4. Your windmill turns a dynamo. Name the energy chain from wind to light, in order.
5. A dynamo makes 5 V at 0.3 A. What electrical power is it delivering? Show your working.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. From the mechanical work of pedalling — the rider's legs turn the wheel, the wheel spins the dynamo, the dynamo converts motion to electricity.
2. The bulb gets brighter — faster spin means more mechanical power delivered to the dynamo, so more electrical power comes out.
3. A motor takes electricity in and produces motion out; a generator takes motion in and produces electricity out. The direction of energy flow is reversed.
4. Wind → windmill blades → shaft → dynamo → wires → bulb → light and heat.
5. Power = voltage × current = 5 V × 0.3 A = 1.5 W.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** Your stream-driven turbine spins a dynamo that delivers 5 V and 0.3 A to a bulb. The water loses gravitational energy at a rate of 4 W as it flows through the wheel. What is the efficiency of the stream-to-light chain, and where could the lost energy have gone?
   **Worked Answer:** Electrical power out = 5 V × 0.3 A = 1.5 W. Input power from the stream = 4 W. Efficiency = 1.5 / 4 = 0.375 = 37.5 %. The lost 2.5 W went to friction in the wheel bearings, turbulence and splashing of water leaving the wheel, resistance heating in the dynamo coil, and heat in the wires and bulb beyond the useful light.
2. **Q:** A dynamo is rated 6 V at a wheel speed of 10 km/h. If voltage is roughly proportional to spin speed, what voltage do you expect at 5 km/h, and what happens to the brightness of a 6 V bulb?
   **Worked Answer:** At half the speed, voltage is roughly halved: 6 V × (5 / 10) = 3 V. A 6 V bulb at 3 V is under-driven — it glows dim orange, not full brightness, because power scales with the square of voltage (P = V²/R), so power falls to about one quarter of its rated value.
3. **Q:** The bulb lights only while the dynamo spins. Why can a dynamo not "save up" electricity for later use the way a battery can? What would you add to store energy from a wind- or water-driven dynamo?
   **Worked Answer:** A dynamo has no energy storage inside it — it converts motion to current in real time, and when the motion stops the current stops. To store the energy you would add a rechargeable battery (or a pumped-storage tank, or a raised weight). The dynamo charges the battery while the wind or water flows, and the battery supplies the bulb when the wind drops. This is the bridge to CBE 3.2 next year, which covers energy storage and circuits in detail.

## Safety Notes
- Spinning bicycle wheels can pinch fingers at the spokes or the dynamo contact point — keep hands clear of the contact zone; only the designated demonstrator touches the spinning wheel.
- LEDs can be briefly hot if over-driven; do not stare directly into a bright LED.
- Crocodile clips can have sharp ends — remind learners not to prick themselves or others.
- If a mains-powered drill or fan is used to spin the demo fast, keep it well away from water and stand clear of loose clothing near the rotating shaft.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners have built windmills (Unit 13) and water wheels and siphons (Unit 8), so they already understand mechanical rotation from natural energy. This lesson is the bridge that turns rotation into electricity.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the qualitative observation — spin faster, bulb brighter, motor = generator backwards. Ages 14–17 also handle the power calculation P = VI and the efficiency chain.
- Local resources: a bicycle dynamo is common in Kenyan cycle shops; the small DC motor can be salvaged from any broken toy, radio, or cassette player. If no dynamo is available, a hand-crank torch serves the same demonstration.
- Assessment: the Post-Test Q5 (P = VI) is the quantitative checkpoint; the energy-chain question (Q4) confirms the conceptual link to the project builds.
- Bridge: state explicitly that next year's CBE 3.2 (electromagnetism and circuits) will explain *why* the coil and magnet make current — this lesson only establishes that they do.
