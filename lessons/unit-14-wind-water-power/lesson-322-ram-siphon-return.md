# Lesson 322: The Ram and the Siphon Return

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.2; G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Compare the hydraulic ram pump and the siphon as two engine-free ways to move water.
2. State the key site requirement for each: a ram needs a fall (driving head) to lift a small fraction of the flow; a siphon needs the outlet lower than the inlet to keep flow moving.
3. Read a site card and decide which method fits — or whether neither does.
4. Defend a site choice with simple head and fall reasoning.

## Materials
- Site cards (6–8 prepared cards, each showing a spring, a tank, a field, relative heights, and distances)
- The Unit 8 build notes and sketches (ram pump and siphon) brought back to the desk
- Chalkboard and chalk
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **hydraulic ram** (pampu ya ramu) — a pump that uses a falling stream's own energy to push a small part of the water uphill
- **driving head** (kasi ya kuanguka) — the vertical fall of water that powers the ram
- **delivery head** (kasi ya kuinua) — the height the ram lifts the water above the ram itself
- **siphon** (sifoni) — a pipe that moves water over a hump from a higher inlet to a lower outlet
- **head** (kichwa cha maji) — the vertical height difference that water falls or is lifted through
- **flow rate** (kiwango cha mtiririko) — how much water passes per second, in litres per second

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*
1. A hydraulic ram pump needs water to fall before it can lift water. Why?
2. A siphon moves water over a wall but only if one end is lower than the other. Why must the outlet be lower?
3. You have a flat field with a spring at the same level as your tank. Which will work — a ram, a siphon, or neither?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The ram uses the energy of falling water (the driving head) to create a pressure spike that pushes a small part of the water uphill. With no fall, there is no energy to drive the pump.
2. Water flows from higher pressure to lower pressure. The outlet must be lower than the inlet so gravity keeps pulling water through the pipe; otherwise the flow stops and the siphon breaks.
3. Neither — a ram needs a fall and a siphon needs an outlet lower than the inlet. With everything at the same level there is no head to drive either method.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — recall the Unit 8 builds: hold up the ram sketch and the siphon sketch side by side; ask learners which one needed a waterfall and which one just needed a pipe over a wall.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, hand out site cards. Each group reads its card, identifies the available fall (for a ram) or the inlet-to-outlet height difference (for a siphon), and writes a one-line recommendation: ram, siphon, or neither, with a reason. Groups swap cards and check a second site.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board draw the two side-by-side: ram (fall → ram → lift) and siphon (high inlet → over hump → low outlet). Confront the misconception that a siphon can lift water higher than its inlet — it cannot; the outlet must always be lower than the inlet, and the pipe must stay full to keep the vacuum.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: matching the method to the site is the engineer's first job. Home task: look at any water source near your home (spring, tank, river) and note whether there is a fall available for a ram or a height drop for a siphon.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*
1. What is the main difference in what a ram pump and a siphon each need from the site?
2. A siphon's outlet is at the same height as its inlet. What happens, and why?
3. A ram pump sits below a spring with a 2 m fall. Can it lift water above the spring level? Explain.
4. A site has a spring 5 m above a river, with a field 2 m above the spring. Which method moves water to the field — ram, siphon, or neither? Why?
5. In one sentence, when would you choose a ram over a siphon?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A ram needs a fall (driving head) — water must drop into it to power the lift. A siphon needs the outlet to be lower than the inlet; it does not need a fall into the pump, only a height drop along the pipe.
2. The siphon stops — with no height difference there is no pressure difference to keep water flowing, so the flow halts and the siphon breaks.
3. Yes — the ram uses the 2 m fall to push a small fraction of the water above the ram, and it can lift it higher than the spring if the delivery head is enough (though the higher it lifts, the less water it delivers).
4. Neither, directly — the field is above the spring, so a siphon from the spring cannot reach it (outlet would be higher than inlet). A ram placed below the spring using the 5 m fall to the river could lift a small fraction of the spring water up to the field, so a ram is the viable choice here.
5. Choose a ram when a stream or spring has a usable fall and you need to lift a small flow to a higher tank; choose a siphon when a large flow must be moved downhill over a hump without an engine.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A spring sits 3 m above a field, 100 m away, with a stream crossing the path between them. The field is 1 m below the spring. Would you use a siphon, a ram, or a gravity pipe? Defend your choice with head math.
   **Worked Answer:** The field is 1 m below the spring, so gravity alone can deliver water: a simple gravity pipe (or a siphon if the pipe must rise over the stream crossing and then fall to the field) works, because the outlet (field) is lower than the inlet (spring). The 1 m drop provides the driving head. A ram is unnecessary because no lifting is required — water flows downhill. The stream crossing is a hump in the pipe route, not a height problem, as long as the pipe's highest point stays below the spring level so the siphon does not break. So: a siphon-style pipe if the route rises over the stream, otherwise a straight gravity pipe. A ram would only be needed if the field were above the spring.
2. **Q:** A ram pump has a driving head of 2 m and lifts water to a delivery head of 10 m. If the supply flow is 2 L/s, estimate the delivery flow, assuming an efficiency of about 60 % and using the ram rule Q_out ≈ efficiency × Q_in × (driving head / delivery head).
   **Worked Answer:** Q_out ≈ 0.60 × 2 L/s × (2 m / 10 m) = 0.60 × 2 × 0.2 = 0.24 L/s. So about 0.24 L/s, or roughly 14 L/min, reaches the upper tank. The trade-off is clear: lifting five times higher than the fall means only about 12 % of the supply flow is delivered.
3. **Q:** A farmer wants to move water from a river up to a tank 8 m above the river using a siphon. Why will this fail, and what should the farmer use instead?
   **Worked Answer:** A siphon cannot lift water above its inlet — the outlet must be lower than the inlet, so a siphon from the river to a tank 8 m above the river is impossible. The farmer should use a hydraulic ram (if a separate driving fall is available on the river) or a powered pump. If there is a fall of at least 1–2 m somewhere along the river, a ram could lift a small fraction of the river flow up the 8 m to the tank.

## Safety Notes
- This is a review/planning lesson with no build, so the main risk is trip hazards from site cards and bags on the floor — keep aisles clear.
- If learners visit a real site after class for the homework task, remind them never to enter a fast-flowing stream alone and to keep away from steep banks where they could slip.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners built both a ram pump and a siphon in Unit 8, so the focus here is comparison and site selection, not new construction.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the qualitative rule — ram needs a fall, siphon needs a lower outlet. Ages 14–17 also handle the delivery-flow estimate using the ram rule of thumb.
- Local resources: prepare site cards ahead of time using local examples — a spring on a hillside, a roof tank above a garden, a river below a shamba. Each card should show relative heights and the distance, enough to decide the method.
- Assessment: Post-Test Q4 (the site with spring, river, and field) is the main decision-making checkpoint; Q5 confirms the student can state the rule of thumb.
- This lesson deliberately revisits Unit 8 content to set up Unit 14's larger project thinking: choosing the right method for the right site is the foundation of any wind- or water-power scheme.
