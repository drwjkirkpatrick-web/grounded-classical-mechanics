# Lesson 323: Rainwater Harvesting Physics

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-4.1; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** tank-sizing

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Calculate the volume of water a roof can collect using Volume = rainfall depth × roof area.
2. Explain why gutters and a first-flush diverter improve water quality.
3. Size a storage tank to bridge a dry spell, given a daily demand and a dry-spell length.
4. Relate Kenyan rainfall patterns (long rains, short rains, dry seasons) to when a tank fills and when it empties.

## Materials
- A roof plan (a simple sketch of a school or home roof with length and width marked)
- A rainfall data card (annual rainfall in mm, and monthly distribution for a Kenyan town)
- A calculator (or phone calculator)
- A 1-litre bottle and a 10-litre jerrican (for volume feel)
- A tape measure
- Graph paper and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **catchment** (eneo la kukusanya) — the surface that collects rain; here, the roof
- **runoff** (maji yakimia) — the rain that flows off the roof into the gutters
- **gutter** (treno) — the channel along the roof edge that carries runoff to the tank
- **first-flush diverter** (kipengele cha taka ya kwanza) — a device that discards the first dirty wash of rain so it does not enter the tank
- **rainfall depth** (kina cha mvua) — the height of rain that falls, in millimetres
- **tank sizing** (ukubwa wa tanki) — choosing a tank volume big enough to bridge the dry season

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*
1. A roof is 10 m long and 6 m wide. What is its area?
2. If 800 mm of rain falls in a year on that roof, how many cubic metres of water could it collect (ignoring losses)?
3. Why is the first rain after a dry spell often dirty when it runs off a roof?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Area = length × width = 10 m × 6 m = 60 m².
2. Volume = rainfall depth × area = 0.800 m × 60 m² = 48 m³ (48 000 litres).
3. Dust, bird droppings, and leaves have built up on the roof during the dry spell; the first rain washes them off, so the first runoff is dirty and should be diverted away from the tank.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the 1-litre bottle and the 10-litre jerrican; ask learners how many jerricans of rain they think fall on the school roof in one year. Reveal the rainfall card and let them react to the scale.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in pairs, learners measure (or read from the roof plan) the length and width of a roof, compute its area, then use the rainfall card to compute the annual collectable volume in cubic metres and litres. They then read the daily demand and dry-spell length from the card and size a tank to bridge the dry spell. Build the hands-on gutter-and-first-flush model from a bottle and tube.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board write the two key formulas: V = rainfall × area (for supply) and tank volume = daily demand × dry-spell days (for storage). Confront the misconception that "bigger roof always means enough water" — losses (gutter overflow, evaporation, first-flush discard) typically cut the collectable volume by 20–30 %, and the tank must be sized for the dry season, not the annual total.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: a roof is a water source, and the tank is the battery that stores it for the dry months. Home task: measure the length and width of your home roof and note the nearest rainfall figure (ask at the chief's office or agriculture office).

## Hands-On Build: Bottle Gutter and First-Flush Diverter
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*
### Parts
- A 1.5-litre plastic bottle cut lengthwise to form a gutter
- A shorter bottle or cup to act as the first-flush diverter
- A small length of clear tubing or a folded paper funnel
- A 10-litre jerrican as the "tank"
- A jug of water to simulate rain
- Tape or string to fix the gutter to a table edge

### Workflow
1. Cut the 1.5-litre bottle lengthwise to make a half-pipe gutter and tape it to the edge of a table sloping slightly downhill — **checkpoint:** water poured at the top runs down to the lower end.
2. At the lower end, place the first-flush cup so the first flow goes into it, with the main tube leading to the jerrican tank — **checkpoint:** the first water fills the cup, then overflows into the tube to the tank.
3. Pour a small amount of water (the "dirty first rain") and watch it fill the first-flush cup — **checkpoint:** dirty water stays in the cup, not the tank.
4. Pour more water (the "clean rain") and watch it overflow into the tube and fill the jerrican — **checkpoint:** clean water reaches the tank.
5. Measure how much water the jerrican collected and compare to the total poured — **checkpoint:** tank volume is less than total rain, showing losses.

### Common Mistakes
- Gutter slope too flat or backwards — water pools and does not flow. Fix: raise the upper end so the gutter slopes downhill at a clear angle.
- First-flush cup too small — it overflows before the roof is washed clean, so dirty water still enters the tank. Fix: use a larger cup, sized to hold the first 0.5–1 mm of rain over the roof area.
- Tube not fully inserted into the jerrican — water spills. Fix: push the tube firmly into the tank opening and seal with tape if needed.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*
1. A roof is 12 m long and 5 m wide. What is its area?
2. If 700 mm of rain falls on that roof in a year, what volume of water could it collect (in m³ and litres)?
3. Why do we divert the first flush of rain away from the storage tank?
4. A household uses 200 litres per day and the dry spell lasts 90 days. What is the minimum tank volume needed to bridge the dry spell?
5. Annual rainfall is 800 mm but only 75 % reaches the tank after losses. If the roof area is 60 m², what is the usable annual yield in litres?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Area = 12 m × 5 m = 60 m².
2. Volume = 0.700 m × 60 m² = 42 m³ = 42 000 litres.
3. The first rain washes dust, bird droppings, and leaves off the roof; diverting it keeps that dirty water out of the tank so the stored water stays cleaner.
4. Tank volume = daily demand × dry-spell days = 200 L/day × 90 days = 18 000 litres = 18 m³.
5. Gross volume = 0.800 m × 60 m² = 48 m³ = 48 000 L. Usable = 75 % of 48 000 L = 36 000 litres.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A roof of 60 m² receives 800 mm of rain per year. A household uses 200 L per day during a dry spell of 90 days. Size the tank in m³, and state whether the annual yield is enough to refill it. Show your reasoning.
   **Worked Answer:** Annual gross yield = 0.800 m × 60 m² = 48 m³ = 48 000 L. With 25 % losses, usable yield = 0.75 × 48 000 = 36 000 L per year. Dry-spell demand = 200 L/day × 90 days = 18 000 L = 18 m³. So the tank must hold at least 18 m³ to bridge the dry spell. The annual usable yield (36 000 L) is twice the dry-spell demand (18 000 L), so the roof can refill the tank during the rainy seasons — the scheme is feasible, and an 18 m³ tank is the minimum sensible size.
2. **Q:** The same roof collects 36 000 L per year (usable). If the household uses 200 L every day of the year (73 000 L/year), is the roof enough? What must change?
   **Worked Answer:** No — annual demand (73 000 L) is more than double the usable yield (36 000 L). The roof alone cannot meet year-round demand. The household must either increase the catchment area (add a second roof or a ground catchment), reduce daily use, or supplement with another source (a borehole, a spring, or purchased water). A larger tank would not help because the limiting factor is total annual yield, not storage.
3. **Q:** Long rains deliver 500 mm in April–May and short rains deliver 250 mm in October–December; the rest of the year is dry. For a 60 m² roof with 75 % efficiency, compute the monthly yield in the rainy months and explain when the tank fills and when it empties.
   **Worked Answer:** Long rains: 0.500 m × 60 m² × 0.75 = 22.5 m³ = 22 500 L over April–May (about 11 250 L/month). Short rains: 0.250 m × 60 m² × 0.75 = 11.25 m³ = 11 250 L over October–December (about 3 750 L/month). The tank fills during and just after the long rains (April–June) and again, less fully, after the short rains (October–December). It draws down through the January–March dry stretch and the July–September cool dry season. An 18 m³ tank would fill in the long rains and then be drawn down over the following dry months, reaching its lowest near the start of the short rains.

## Safety Notes
- When cutting plastic bottles, use scissors or a craft knife with a cutting board, not a bare blade held in the air; cut away from the body.
- Do not climb on a real roof to measure it for the homework task — estimate length and width from the ground by pacing, or ask an adult.
- Stored rainwater is for washing, irrigation, and (if filtered and chlorinated) drinking; remind learners that untreated tank water is not safe to drink without boiling or chlorination.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners know area (length × width) from maths and have met volume in Unit 8 (tank and siphon work). The new skill is combining rainfall depth with area to get volume.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 compute area and use the formulas with given numbers. Ages 14–17 handle the efficiency losses, the dry-spell bridging, and the seasonal refill reasoning.
- Local resources: use a real school or home roof for the roof plan. Rainfall data for the nearest town can be obtained from the Kenya Meteorological Department or the local agriculture office; if unavailable, use typical figures (Nairobi ~900 mm, Kisumu ~1200 mm, Lodwar ~200 mm).
- Assessment: Post-Test Q4 (tank sizing for the dry spell) is the project checkpoint; Q5 (usable yield with losses) confirms the efficiency concept.
- The tank-sizing project continues in later lessons — this lesson establishes the physics; the next lessons will address pipe sizing and the full gravity-scheme design.
