# Lesson 324: Gravity Water Schemes: From Spring to Village

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.2; G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Survey the head (vertical drop) between a spring and a village using the Unit 7 water level.
2. Explain why pipe diameter matters: a narrow pipe creates more friction and reduces flow.
3. Describe the purpose of a break-pressure tank on a long, steep drop.
4. Read a simple contour sketch and mark where break-pressure tanks should go.

## Materials
- The Unit 7 water level (two stakes, a clear hose, water inside)
- A tape measure (30 m)
- A map sketch of a spring-to-village route (prepared ahead, with contour lines)
- A length of 13 mm and 19 mm pipe (or two bottles with different neck sizes) to show flow contrast
- A jug of water and a stopwatch
- Chalkboard and chalk
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **head** (kichwa cha maji) — the vertical height difference between the source and the delivery point
- **gravity scheme** (mradi wa graviti) — a water system that uses gravity, not a pump, to move water downhill
- **friction loss** (upotevu wa msuguano) — the pressure lost as water rubs against the inside of a pipe
- **pipe diameter** (kipenyo cha bomba) — the width of the pipe's inside; wider pipes have less friction
- **break-pressure tank** (tanki ya kupunguza shinikizo) — an open tank partway down a long drop that vents excess pressure so the lower pipe is not over-stressed
- **static pressure** (shinikizo tulivu) — the pressure in a full pipe when the water is not moving, due to the height of the water column above

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*
1. Water flows from a spring 50 m above a village. What is providing the energy to move the water?
2. Two pipes carry the same flow: one is narrow, one is wide. Which has more friction loss, and what is the effect?
3. A pipe drops 150 m down a hillside with no tank in between. What risk does the bottom of the pipe face?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Gravity — the water's gravitational potential energy (the 50 m head) is converted to motion as it falls.
2. The narrow pipe has more friction loss because water rubs against more pipe wall per unit of flow; the effect is reduced flow rate and lower pressure at the outlet.
3. The static pressure at the bottom is very high (about 15 atmospheres for a 150 m drop), which can burst the pipe or blow off the fittings; a break-pressure tank partway down vents the excess pressure.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)
1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — pour water through the 13 mm and 19 mm pipes (or bottle necks) side by side; ask learners which one empties the jug faster and why. Introduce the word "friction."
3. **Explore (15 min)** — outside or in the yard, use the Unit 7 water level to survey a short slope (5–10 m of head over 20–30 m of distance). Record the head. Back inside, in groups, read the prepared map sketch, mark the total head, and decide where a break-pressure tank should go. Pour water through the two pipe sizes and time the flow to feel the friction difference.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — on the board compute the static pressure at the bottom of a 120 m drop: P = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 120 ≈ 1.18 × 10⁶ Pa ≈ 12 atmospheres — far above what a typical plastic pipe can hold (rated for ~4–6 atm). Confront the misconception that "a steeper pipe is always better" — a steep pipe builds high pressure at the bottom and can burst the pipe; break-pressure tanks split the drop into safe sections.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: a gravity scheme works only if the pipe can survive the pressure; survey the head, size the pipe, and insert break-pressure tanks. Home task: find any long water pipe near your home and note whether it has any open tank partway along its route.

## Hands-On Build: Head Survey with the Water Level
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*
### Parts
- Unit 7 water level (two stakes ~1.5 m, clear hose ~10 m, water filled to mid-height)
- Tape measure (30 m)
- Notebook and pencil
- A slope with 2–5 m of fall over 15–25 m of distance (school yard or roadside)

### Workflow
1. Place stake A at the top of the slope and stake B downhill, ~5–8 m apart, with the hose between them — **checkpoint:** water level reads the same on both stakes when the hose is not kinked.
2. Read the height on stake A (reference) and on stake B; the difference is the fall over that section — **checkpoint:** a measurable height difference (e.g., 0.5–1.0 m) is recorded.
3. Move stake A to where stake B was, and place a new stake B further downhill; repeat the reading — **checkpoint:** a second fall is recorded and added to the first.
4. Continue for 3–4 sections and sum the falls to get the total head over the surveyed distance — **checkpoint:** total head is noted (e.g., 2–4 m).
5. Sketch the slope profile in the notebook with the cumulative head marked — **checkpoint:** a clear height-vs-distance sketch is drawn.

### Common Mistakes
- Air bubble in the hose — the water levels on the two stakes do not match even on flat ground. Fix: re-fill the hose, tapping out all bubbles, and check that both levels read the same on level ground before starting.
- Hose kinked or pinched — water cannot equalise and the reading is wrong. Fix: keep the hose in a smooth curve; do not step on it or bend it sharply.
- Stakes not vertical — the height mark is off. Fix: hold each stake vertical (use a plumb line or check by eye) before marking the water level.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*
1. What provides the energy to move water in a gravity scheme?
2. Why does a narrow pipe deliver less flow than a wide pipe over the same distance?
3. What is the purpose of a break-pressure tank on a long, steep drop?
4. A pipe drops 80 m with no break-pressure tank. Estimate the static pressure at the bottom in atmospheres (take 10 m of water ≈ 1 atm).
5. Name two things you must survey before choosing a pipe size for a gravity scheme.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Gravity — the head (vertical height difference) between the source and the delivery point provides the potential energy.
2. A narrow pipe has more wall surface per unit of flow, so friction loss is greater; this reduces the pressure and flow rate at the outlet.
3. A break-pressure tank vents the excess pressure built up over a long drop, splitting the total head into safe sections so the lower pipe and fittings are not over-stressed and do not burst.
4. 80 m / 10 m per atm = 8 atm. So the static pressure at the bottom is about 8 atmospheres — enough to burst a standard plastic pipe.
5. Any two of: the total head (vertical drop), the distance (pipe length), the required flow rate, the terrain (where break-pressure tanks are needed), and the available pipe sizes and their pressure ratings.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A 3 km pipe drops 120 m from a spring to a village. Why must break-pressure tanks be inserted, and what is the static pressure at the bottom of the pipe if there are none?
   **Worked Answer:** Without a break-pressure tank, the full 120 m of head acts on the bottom section of pipe. Static pressure P = ρgh = 1000 kg/m³ × 9.8 m/s² × 120 m = 1.18 × 10⁶ Pa ≈ 11.6 atm (using 10 m ≈ 1 atm gives 12 atm). A typical PVC or HDPE rural pipe is rated for about 4–6 atm, so 12 atm would burst the pipe or blow off the fittings. A break-pressure tank partway down (e.g., at 60 m) vents the upper 60 m of head to atmosphere, so the lower section sees only the remaining 60 m (about 6 atm), which a heavier-duty pipe can handle. The tank also calms the flow and lets air escape.
2. **Q:** A spring delivers 2 L/s through a 19 mm pipe over 500 m with a 30 m head. If friction loss in the pipe is about 20 m of head, what is the useful head at the village, and what does this tell you about pipe sizing?
   **Worked Answer:** Useful head = total head − friction loss = 30 m − 20 m = 10 m. So two-thirds of the available head is lost to friction in the narrow 19 mm pipe over 500 m. To deliver more flow or more pressure at the village, the pipe should be widened (e.g., to 25 mm or 32 mm), which reduces the friction loss dramatically, or the route should be shortened. This shows that pipe diameter is a design choice, not an afterthought: a pipe that is too narrow wastes the head the spring provides.
3. **Q:** Design a break-pressure layout for a 150 m drop over 2 km. If the pipe is rated for 6 atm, how many break-pressure tanks are needed, and where (approximately) should they go?
   **Worked Answer:** 6 atm corresponds to about 60 m of head. To keep each section below 60 m, split the 150 m drop into sections of no more than ~50 m (with a safety margin). So we need break-pressure tanks at roughly 50 m and 100 m down from the spring, creating three sections: 0–50 m (50 m head ≈ 5 atm), 50–100 m (50 m head ≈ 5 atm), and 100–150 m (50 m head ≈ 5 atm). Each section stays within the 6 atm rating. So two break-pressure tanks, placed at about 50 m and 100 m below the spring, protect the pipe. The tanks also serve as air-release points and allow sediment to settle.

## Safety Notes
- When surveying with the water level outside, keep learners off roads and away from steep drops; work on a gentle slope only.
- Do not let learners climb on or near the spring source if it is in a steep or unstable bank.
- The water level hose contains water — do not let learners drink from it (it may be dirty from previous field use).
- If visiting a real gravity scheme for the homework task, keep clear of pressurised pipes and valves; a burst fitting can cause injury.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners built and used the Unit 7 water level for surveying, and they understand head from Unit 8 (ram pump and siphon). The new content is friction loss and break-pressure tanks.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the qualitative ideas — head drives the flow, narrow pipes lose more, long drops need break-pressure tanks. Ages 14–17 also compute static pressure (P = ρgh) and design a break-pressure layout.
- Local resources: the Unit 7 water level is reusable; any tape measure works. The map sketch can be drawn for a real local spring-to-village route to make the lesson concrete.
- Assessment: Post-Test Q4 (static pressure estimate) is the quantitative checkpoint; Q5 (what to survey) confirms the design-thinking concept.
- This lesson ties the surveying skill (Unit 7) to the project design of Unit 14: a gravity scheme is the cheapest, most reliable rural water system, but only if the physics of pressure and friction is respected.
