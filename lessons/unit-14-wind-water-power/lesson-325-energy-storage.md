# Lesson 325: Storing Energy: Two Tanks and a Pump

**Unit:** 14 — Wind \u0026 Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-4.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain pumped storage: pump water uphill when energy is abundant, let it flow down through a turbine when needed.
2. Calculate the energy stored in a raised tank using E = mgh.
3. Compare pumped storage with batteries and flywheels for cost, lifetime, and simplicity.
4. Quantify round-trip efficiency and explain where the losses occur.

## Materials
- Two jerricans (or buckets) — one at floor level, one on a stool
- A small pump (bottle pump, hand pump, or the diaphragm pump from Unit 8)
- A length of tube
- A stopwatch
- A measuring jar
- A torch bulb and battery (for comparison)
- Notebook and pencil

## Vocabulary
- **pumped storage** (hifadhi ya pampu) — storing energy by lifting water to a high tank; releasing it through a turbine to recover the energy
- **round-trip efficiency** (uhalali wa mwendo) — the fraction of the energy you put in that you get back out
- **battery** (betri) — a chemical energy store; common types: lead-acid (car), lithium (phone), dry cell (torch)
- **flywheel** (gurudumu la kuhifadhi) — a heavy spinning wheel that stores kinetic energy; slows down as it gives energy back

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You lift a full jerrican (10 kg) onto a shelf 2 m high. How much energy did you store?
2. If you let that water flow back down through a small turbine, do you get all the energy back? Why or why not?
3. Name one way to store energy that does not use water.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. E = mgh = 10 × 10 × 2 = 200 J. (Using g ≈ 10 m/s².)
2. No — some energy is lost to friction in the pipe, turbulence in the turbine, and heat in the generator. You get back less than you put in. The round-trip efficiency is less than 100%.
3. Any correct answer: a battery (chemical), a flywheel (kinetic), a spring (elastic), a compressed air tank, a stretched rubber band, a raised weight (gravitational, like pumped storage but with a solid mass).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a torch battery and a jerrican. Ask: *both store energy — which lasts longer? Which is cheaper?* The battery is small and convenient but dies and must be replaced. The jerrican of water on a shelf stores energy too (gravitational PE) and never wears out — but you need a pump and a turbine to store and recover it. Board: **pumped storage = lift water up when you have spare energy, let it flow down when you need it.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs build a pumped storage model (see Hands-On Build). They pump water from the lower jerrican to the upper one, timing the pump. Then they let the water flow back down through a tube (or through their turbine from Lesson 319 if available) and time how long it takes to empty. They measure the flow rate and compute the power recovered. They compare with the pumping time and estimate the round-trip efficiency. Results go on a class table: pumping time | pumping power | return time | return power | round-trip efficiency.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate. Pumped storage is the simplest large-scale energy storage: when you have spare wind or solar (or cheap night-time grid power), pump water uphill to a high tank. When demand peaks, let the water flow down through a turbine to generate electricity. The energy stored is E = mgh — a 1000-litre tank raised 5 m stores 1000 × 10 × 5 = 50,000 J = 50 kJ. At 50% round-trip efficiency, you recover 25 kJ — enough to light a 5 W LED bulb for 5 seconds (5 W × 5 s = 25 J... actually 25 kJ / 5 W = 5000 s ≈ 83 minutes). Confront the misconception that "pumped storage is free energy" — it is not. You put in more energy (pumping) than you get back (turbine), because friction, turbulence, and generator losses eat the difference. The round-trip efficiency of real pumped storage is 70–80%; a small school model might be 20–40%. Compare with batteries: a car battery stores about 1–2 MJ (100–200 kJ per kg), has a round-trip efficiency of 80–90%, but wears out after 500–1000 cycles and contains toxic lead. Pumped storage lasts decades with no chemical degradation — the water never wears out. Also address: "why not just use batteries?" — batteries are compact and convenient for small loads (phones, lights), but for large-scale storage (village water supply, irrigation pumping), pumped storage is cheaper, simpler, and longer-lasting. Flywheels are fast but expensive and lose energy to friction over hours.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *lift water up when you have spare energy, let it flow down when you need it — simple, cheap, lasts forever*. Homework: if your school had a 2000-litre tank on a 3 m tower, how much energy is stored? How long could it run a 10 W LED light at 50% efficiency?

## Hands-On Build: Two-Tank Pumped Storage Model
*Build a simple pumped storage system and measure round-trip efficiency.*

### Parts
- Two jerricans or buckets (one for the lower tank, one for the upper)
- A small pump (bottle pump, hand pump, or the Unit 8 diaphragm pump)
- A length of tube (to connect the tanks)
- A stopwatch
- A measuring jar
- A stool or shelf (to raise the upper tank)
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Place one jerrican on the floor (lower tank) and one on the stool (upper tank, about 1 m higher). Fill the lower tank with water. **Checkpoint:** the height difference is measured and recorded.
2. Pump water from the lower tank to the upper tank using the pump. Time how long it takes to lift a known volume (e.g., 2 litres). **Checkpoint:** the pumping time and volume are recorded.
3. Compute the pumping power: P_pump = (mgh) / t_pump, where m is the mass of water lifted, g = 10 m/s², h is the height, and t_pump is the pumping time. **Checkpoint:** the pumping power is computed in watts.
4. Let the water flow back down from the upper tank to the lower tank through the tube (or through the turbine from Lesson 319 if available). Time how long it takes to empty the same volume. **Checkpoint:** the return time is recorded.
5. Compute the return power: P_return = (mgh) / t_return. **Checkpoint:** the return power is computed in watts.
6. Compute the round-trip efficiency: η = P_return × t_return / (P_pump × t_pump) = (energy out) / (energy in). If the volumes are the same, η = t_pump / t_return (if the flow rates are different). A simpler estimate: η = (return power) / (pumping power). **Checkpoint:** the round-trip efficiency is computed and is less than 100%.
7. Repeat with a greater height difference (if possible) and see if the efficiency changes. **Checkpoint:** the efficiency is roughly constant — the losses are proportional to the flow rate, not the height.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not measuring the height** — the height difference (h) is critical for the energy calculation. Measure from the water surface of the lower tank to the water surface of the upper tank.
- **Confusing power and energy** — power is energy per second (W = J/s). The energy stored is mgh regardless of how fast you pump; the power depends on the pumping rate.
- **Expecting 100% efficiency** — real systems always lose energy to friction, turbulence, and heat. A school model might be 20–40% efficient. Real pumped storage plants achieve 70–80%.
- **Not using the same volume** — to compare pumping and return, lift and release the same volume of water. Measure with a measuring jar.
- **Letting the water flow freely** — if the water just pours out of the tube without a turbine or restriction, you are not extracting useful energy. The water flows out but does no work. To extract energy, the water must drive a turbine or push a piston.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. You lift a full jerrican (10 kg) onto a shelf 2 m high. How much energy did you store? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If you let that water flow back down through a turbine, do you get all the energy back? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Name one way to store energy that does not use water. (mirrors pre-test)
4. A 1000-litre tank is raised 5 m. How much energy is stored? If the round-trip efficiency is 50%, how much useful energy do you recover?
5. Challenge: compare pumped storage with a lead-acid battery for a village micro-hydro system. Give one advantage of each.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. E = mgh = 10 × 10 × 2 = 200 J.
2. No — friction in the pipe, turbulence in the turbine, and heat in the generator all take a share. The round-trip efficiency is less than 100%.
3. Any correct answer: battery (chemical), flywheel (kinetic), spring (elastic), compressed air, raised weight.
4. E = mgh = 1000 × 10 × 5 = 50,000 J = 50 kJ. At 50% efficiency, useful energy = 25 kJ.
5. Pumped storage advantage: lasts decades with no chemical degradation; no toxic materials; simple to build and maintain with local materials. Battery advantage: compact; high energy density (stores more energy per kg); can deliver electricity directly (no turbine needed); faster response time. For a village: pumped storage is cheaper and longer-lasting for large-scale storage (irrigation, water supply); batteries are better for small, instant loads (lights, phone charging).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A school has a 2000-litre tank on a 5 m tower. The pump runs on solar panels that produce 100 W for 6 hours per day. How long does it take to fill the tank? If the water is released through a turbine at 50% round-trip efficiency, how long can it run a 10 W LED light?
   **Worked Answer:** Energy to fill the tank: E = mgh = 2000 × 10 × 5 = 100,000 J = 100 kJ. Pumping power available: 100 W (assuming the pump is 100% efficient — in reality, the pump might be 50% efficient, so 50 W of hydraulic power). Time to fill: t = E / P = 100,000 / 100 = 1000 s ≈ 17 minutes (at 100% pump efficiency). At 50% pump efficiency: t = 100,000 / 50 = 2000 s ≈ 33 minutes. But the solar panels produce 100 W for 6 hours = 3600 Wh = 3600 × 3600 J = 12.96 MJ per day. The energy needed to fill the tank is 100 kJ (at 100% pump efficiency) or 200 kJ (at 50%). So the panels can fill the tank many times over in a day. Energy recovered from the tank at 50% round-trip efficiency: E_recovered = 100 kJ × 0.5 = 50 kJ. Time to run a 10 W LED: t = E_recovered / P_LED = 50,000 / 10 = 5000 s ≈ 83 minutes. So a full tank at 5 m height can run a 10 W LED for about 83 minutes.
2. **Q:** A flywheel stores 10 kJ of kinetic energy at 3000 rpm. It loses 2% of its energy per hour to friction. How much energy remains after 8 hours? Compare with a pumped storage system that loses 0.1% per hour.
   **Worked Answer:** Flywheel: after 8 hours, the remaining energy is E = 10,000 × (0.98)^8 = 10,000 × 0.851 = 8510 J. The flywheel loses about 15% of its energy in 8 hours. Pumped storage: E = 10,000 × (0.999)^8 = 10,000 × 0.992 = 9920 J. The pumped storage loses only 0.8% in 8 hours. The flywheel is good for short-term storage (minutes to hours) but loses energy quickly to friction. Pumped storage is good for long-term storage (hours to months) because the water just sits in the tank — the only loss is evaporation, which is very slow. This is why pumped storage is used for grid-scale energy storage (store solar by day, release at night) while flywheels are used for short-term power quality (ride through voltage dips, stabilise frequency).
3. **Q:** Design a pumped storage system for a village with a 50 m hill. The village needs 1 kWh (3.6 MJ) of electricity per evening. Assuming 60% round-trip efficiency, how much water must be pumped uphill each day?
   **Worked Answer:** Energy needed at the output: 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ. At 60% round-trip efficiency, the energy that must be stored is E_stored = 3.6 / 0.6 = 6.0 MJ. The energy stored in raised water: E = mgh. So m = E / (gh) = 6,000,000 / (10 × 50) = 6,000,000 / 500 = 12,000 kg. Since 1 litre of water = 1 kg, the village needs 12,000 litres = 12 m³ of water pumped uphill each day. That's a tank of about 2 m × 2 m × 3 m — a reasonable size for a village. The pump must lift 12,000 kg by 50 m each day: E_pump = 12,000 × 10 × 50 = 6 MJ. If the pump runs on solar for 6 hours, the average pumping power is 6,000,000 / (6 × 3600) = 278 W — a modest solar pump. This is a realistic design for a village with a hill.

## Safety Notes
- Do not overfill the upper jerrican — it can tip and spill. Fill to 3/4 maximum.
- The tube can slip off the pump — secure it with clamps or wire.
- Do not lift heavy jerricans above shoulder height without help — use a stool, not a high shelf.
- Water on the floor is a slip hazard — clean up spills immediately.
- The pump motor can overheat if run dry — always have water in the lower tank before pumping.

- The key insight is that pumped storage is the simplest and longest-lasting form of energy storage — no chemicals, no degradation, just water and gravity. The trade-off is low energy density: you need a lot of water and a big height to store significant energy. For a village with a hill, it's practical; for a flat school compound, a raised tank works but stores less.
