# Lesson 326: Community Wind & Water Survey

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-4.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** resource-survey

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Conduct a structured field survey of local wind and water resources.
2. Measure wind speed (using the anemometer from Lesson 303), slope (using the water level from Unit 7), and stream flow (using the float method).
3. Interview a local fundi or elder about existing pumps, water sources, and energy needs.
4. Plot the data on a resource map and identify the three best sites for wind or water power.

## Materials
- The cup anemometer (from Lesson 303) and its calibration card
- The water level (from Unit 7, Lesson 267 or equivalent)
- A tape measure (30 m)
- A stopwatch
- An orange or leaf (as a float for stream flow measurement)
- Notebooks and pencils
- A sketch map of the school area (or draw one on the board)
- A clipboard or stiff board for writing in the field

## Vocabulary
- **resource survey** (uatifu wa rasilimali) — a systematic walk to identify and measure the natural resources in an area
- **site assessment** (tathmini ya eneo) — evaluating a specific location for a wind or water installation
- **stream flow** (mtiririko wa mto) — the volume of water passing a point per second; measured in litres per second
- **head** (kimo) — the vertical distance the water falls; measured with a water level
- **prevailing wind** (upepo mkuu) — the most common wind direction at a site; found from a wind sock log

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You need to choose between a wind site and a water site for a school pump. What two measurements tell you which has more power?
2. How do you measure the flow of a small stream using an orange and a stopwatch?
3. Why might a local elder know more about the best water source than a single site visit reveals?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Wind: measure wind speed (with the anemometer). Water: measure head (with the water level) and flow (with the float method). Wind power ∝ v³ (speed cubed). Water power = ρgQh (flow × head × g). Both measurements are needed to compare the two sites.
2. Measure a known length of stream (e.g., 5 m). Drop the orange upstream of the start line. Time how long it takes to float from the start to the end. Flow speed = distance / time. To get the flow rate (L/s), multiply the speed by the cross-sectional area of the stream (width × depth, measured with a tape). Q = area × speed.
3. An elder knows the seasonal variation — which streams dry up in August, which springs are reliable year-round, where the wind is strongest in the afternoons, and where floods come in the long rains. A single visit only shows one moment; the elder's knowledge covers years.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the sketch map of the school area. Ask: *where is the windiest spot? Where is the nearest stream? Where does water already flow?* Mark candidates on the map. Board: **today we walk the area and measure — wind speed, slope, stream flow — and map the resources.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teams of 3–4 walk the school compound and surroundings (see Hands-On Build). Each team visits 2–3 sites: a windy ridge, a stream or spring, an existing pump or tank. At each site, they measure wind speed (anemometer), slope/head (water level), and stream flow (float method). They note the distance from the site to the school (tape measure or pacing). They interview a fundi or elder if available (prepare 3 questions). Results go on the resource map.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw the resource map with all team data. Mark the top three sites. For each site, compute the available power: wind sites — P = ½ρAv³ (using the rotor area the windmill would have); water sites — P = ρgQh. Compare the sites: which has the most power? Which is closest to the school? Which is most reliable (year-round)? Confront the misconception that "the closest site is always best" — a distant but powerful site may be better than a nearby but weak one, because the power grows with the cube of wind speed (doubling the distance to a windier site is worth it if the wind is 30% stronger). Also address: "one visit is enough" — no, resources vary by season. The survey must be repeated in different seasons (long rains, dry season, short rains) to be reliable. The elder's knowledge fills in the seasonal picture.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *measure, don't guess — wind speed, stream flow, and head tell you which site has the most power*. Homework: complete the resource map with all team data and write one paragraph recommending the best site.

## Hands-On Build: Community Resource Survey
*Walk the school area, measure wind and water resources, and map the results.*

### Parts
- The cup anemometer (from Lesson 303) and its calibration card
- The water level (from Unit 7)
- A tape measure (30 m)
- A stopwatch
- An orange or leaf (as a float)
- Notebooks and pencils
- A sketch map of the school area
- A clipboard or stiff board

### Workflow
1. Divide into teams of 3–4. Each team gets a zone: (a) the wind ridges and open areas, (b) the streams and springs, (c) the existing pumps and tanks. **Checkpoint:** every team knows its zone and has the right tools.
2. At each wind site: hold the anemometer at 2 m height (head height). Count turns for 30 seconds. Convert to wind speed using the calibration card. Repeat 3 times and average. Note the direction (use the wind sock or a wet finger). **Checkpoint:** wind speed and direction are recorded for each wind site.
3. At each water site: measure the head (vertical drop) using the water level. Measure the stream width and depth with the tape. Measure the flow speed: mark a 5 m section, drop the orange upstream, time it from start to end. Flow speed = 5 m / time. Flow rate Q = width × depth × speed. **Checkpoint:** head, width, depth, speed, and flow rate are recorded for each water site.
4. Measure the distance from each site to the school (tape measure or pacing — 1 pace ≈ 0.7 m). **Checkpoint:** distances are recorded.
5. If a fundi or elder is available, ask: (a) Which streams dry up in the dry season? (b) When is the wind strongest — morning or afternoon? (c) Where do people currently get water? **Checkpoint:** interview answers are recorded.
6. Return to the classroom. Plot all data on the sketch map. For each site, compute the available power. Rank the top three sites. **Checkpoint:** the resource map shows all sites with measurements and power estimates.

### Common Mistakes
- **Measuring wind at ground level** — wind speed increases with height. Measure at 2 m (head height) at minimum. A real windmill would be at 3–5 m, where the wind is stronger.
- **Measuring flow in a single spot** — streams vary in width and depth. Measure at a representative section (straight, uniform depth). Avoid pools (too slow) and rapids (too fast — the surface speed is higher than the average).
- **Forgetting seasonal variation** — a stream that flows in May may be dry in September. Note the date and ask the elder about seasonal reliability.
- **Not converting anemometer turns to speed** — the raw turn count is not useful without the calibration card from Lesson 303. Always convert.
- **Not computing power** — the raw measurements (speed, flow, head) are only useful if you compute the power. P_wind = ½ρAv³ and P_water = ρgQh. Without the power, you can't compare sites.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. What two measurements tell you whether a wind site or a water site has more power? (mirrors pre-test)
2. How do you measure stream flow with an orange and a stopwatch? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why might a local elder know more than a single site visit? (mirrors pre-test)
4. A stream is 1 m wide, 0.3 m deep, and the orange travels 5 m in 10 seconds. What is the flow rate in L/s?
5. Challenge: two sites — Site A has wind at 4 m/s, Site B has wind at 5 m/s. Site B is twice as far from the school. Which has more wind power, and by what factor?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Wind: wind speed (anemometer). Water: head (water level) and flow (float method). Wind power ∝ v³. Water power = ρgQh.
2. Mark a 5 m section of stream. Drop the orange upstream. Time from start to end. Speed = 5/time. Flow rate = width × depth × speed.
3. The elder knows seasonal variation — which streams dry up, when wind is strongest, where floods come. A single visit shows one moment; the elder's knowledge covers years.
4. Speed = 5 m / 10 s = 0.5 m/s. Cross-section = 1 × 0.3 = 0.3 m². Flow rate = 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15 m³/s = 150 L/s. (1 m³ = 1000 L.)
5. Wind power ∝ v³. P_A ∝ 4³ = 64. P_B ∝ 5³ = 125. Site B has 125/64 = 1.95× the wind power of Site A — almost double. Even though Site B is twice as far, the power is almost double, so the extra piping or transmission is worth it. This is the cube law of wind power: a small increase in wind speed gives a large increase in power.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A stream has a flow of 2 L/s and a head of 8 m. What is the available hydraulic power? If a micro-turbine is 50% efficient, what electrical power is delivered?
   **Worked Answer:** Available hydraulic power: P = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 0.002 × 8 = 160 W. (2 L/s = 0.002 m³/s.) At 50% turbine efficiency: P_electrical = 160 × 0.5 = 80 W. That's enough to light 8 LED bulbs (10 W each) or charge 16 phones simultaneously (5 W each). Not enough for a school computer lab (300+ W), but enough for lighting and phone charging.
2. **Q:** Score three sites with a weighted matrix. The criteria are: head (weight 3), flow (weight 3), distance to school (weight 2), demand (weight 2). Site A: head 5 m, flow 1 L/s, distance 200 m, demand high. Site B: head 15 m, flow 0.5 L/s, distance 500 m, demand medium. Site C: head 2 m, flow 5 L/s, distance 100 m, demand high. Score each criterion 1–5 and compute the weighted total.
   **Worked Answer:** First, score each criterion on a 1–5 scale (5 = best):
   - Head: A = 2 (5 m, modest), B = 5 (15 m, excellent), C = 1 (2 m, low).
   - Flow: A = 2 (1 L/s, low), B = 1 (0.5 L/s, very low), C = 5 (5 L/s, excellent).
   - Distance: A = 4 (200 m, close), B = 2 (500 m, far), C = 5 (100 m, very close).
   - Demand: A = 5 (high), B = 3 (medium), C = 5 (high).
   Weighted totals:
   - A: (2×3) + (2×3) + (4×2) + (5×2) = 6 + 6 + 8 + 10 = 30.
   - B: (5×3) + (1×3) + (2×2) + (3×2) = 15 + 3 + 4 + 6 = 28.
   - C: (1×3) + (5×3) + (5×2) + (5×2) = 3 + 15 + 10 + 10 = 38.
   Site C wins with 38 points — the high flow and close distance outweigh the low head. Site A is second (30), Site B is third (28) — the high head doesn't compensate for the low flow and long distance. The weights matter: if head were weighted 5 (for a high-pressure application), Site B would score higher.
3. **Q:** A survey finds that the wind at the school ridge averages 3 m/s in the morning and 6 m/s in the afternoon. How much more wind power is available in the afternoon? If the school needs water for the garden (200 L/day), and the windmill pumps 0.5 L per rotation at 60 rpm, how many minutes of afternoon wind are needed?
   **Worked Answer:** Wind power ∝ v³. Afternoon vs morning: (6/3)³ = 2³ = 8. The afternoon has 8× the wind power of the morning. Pumping rate: 0.5 L/rotation × 60 rpm = 30 L/min. Time to pump 200 L: 200 / 30 = 6.7 minutes. So the windmill needs only about 7 minutes of afternoon wind to pump the daily garden water. Even if the afternoon wind only lasts 30 minutes, that's 4× the needed water (900 L). The school could pump 200 L for the garden and 700 L for handwashing or the toilet tank. The cube law means the afternoon wind is dramatically more productive than the morning.

## Safety Notes
- Walking outside the school compound requires teacher supervision and a clear boundary. Do not cross roads or enter private land without permission.
- Stream banks can be slippery — approach from a stable footing. Do not enter fast-flowing water.
- The anemometer has sharp pins — hold it by the pencil, not the cups.
- The water level tube can trip people — coil it when not in use.
- Do not drink from streams during the survey — water quality is unknown.
- Sun protection: hats and water bottles for the field walk.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 301–304 (wind measurement) and 317–320 (water power) introduced the measurement tools and power formulas. This lesson applies them in a real field survey.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the field walk, using the anemometer and float method, and marking the map. Ages 14–17 should compute power at each site, build the weighted matrix, and defend their site recommendation with quantitative reasoning.
- Local resource tips: the anemometer (Lesson 303), the water level (Unit 7), and a tape measure are the only tools needed. The sketch map can be drawn on the board or printed from OpenStreetMap. A local fundi or school caretaker can be invited for the interview portion.
- The weighted matrix is a powerful decision-making tool. It forces learners to articulate what matters (the weights) and score each site objectively. Different teams may choose different weights, leading to different recommendations — this is realistic and should be discussed.
- This lesson directly feeds the design brief (Lesson 327), where teams use the survey data to design a wind or water solution for the school. The quality of the survey determines the quality of the design.
- If the school compound has no streams or windy ridges, the survey can focus on existing resources: the school water tank, the roof catchment area, the prevailing wind direction, and the nearest water source (even if it's a municipal supply).
