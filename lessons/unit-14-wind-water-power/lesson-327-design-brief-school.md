# Lesson 327: Design Brief: Power for Our School

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-4.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** school-power

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Translate a real school need (garden watering, handwash station, toilet tank) into an engineering design brief with measurable targets.
2. Select an appropriate energy source (wind, water, or gravity) based on the community survey data from Lesson 326.
3. Produce a design drawing with dimensions, materials list, and a budget using local prices.
4. Present an energy budget: daily demand, source power curve, storage, and worst-month shortfall plan.

## Materials
- The resource survey map from Lesson 326
- Design sheets (A4, with sections: Need, Site, Source, Design Drawing, Materials, Budget, Energy Budget)
- Pencils, rulers, erasers
- Graph paper
- Local price list (mabati, poles, pipe, fittings, pump, wire — teacher prepares or learners research)
- Calculators
- The anemometer calibration card and stream flow data from Lesson 326

## Vocabulary
- **design brief** (muhtasari wa ubuni) — a short document that states what you are building, for whom, and what it must achieve
- **energy budget** (bajeti ya nishati) — a table comparing daily energy demand with daily energy supply and storage
- **shortfall** (kasoro) — the gap between what the system can supply and what is needed in the worst month
- **specification** (maelezo ya kiufundi) — the measurable targets the design must meet (e.g., 200 L/day, 5 m head, budget KSh 5000)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Your school garden needs 200 L of water per day. What information from the survey do you need to decide between wind and water power?
2. What is a design brief, and why must it have measurable targets?
3. If your windmill pumps 30 L per minute but the wind only blows strongly for 2 hours per day, how much water can it deliver daily?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Wind: average wind speed and hours per day of usable wind (from the anemometer survey). Water: stream flow (L/s) and head (m) from the survey. Also: distance from the site to the garden, and seasonal reliability (from the elder interview). The power formulas — P_wind = ½ρAv³ and P_water = ρgQh — convert the survey data into available power.
2. A design brief is a short document stating what you are building, for whom, and what it must achieve. Measurable targets (e.g., 200 L/day, budget KSh 5000) let you check whether the finished design actually meets the need — without targets, you can't tell success from failure.
3. 30 L/min × 2 h = 30 × 120 = 3600 L. But the wind doesn't blow at full strength for the whole 2 hours — it ramps up and down. A realistic estimate might be 50% of the peak rate, so about 1800 L. Either way, 200 L/day is easily met with 2 hours of wind.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — present the design challenge: *Your school has a real need — garden watering, a handwash station, or a toilet tank. Using the survey data from Lesson 326, design a wind or water power system to meet that need.* Board: **Need → Site → Source → Design → Budget → Energy Budget.** Show a completed example design sheet on the board (a simple sketch with dimensions, materials, and a budget).
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teams of 3–4 work through the design sheet (see Hands-On Build). They choose a school need, select a site from the survey map, pick an energy source (wind or water), and sketch the system with dimensions. They list materials and look up prices. They compute the energy budget: daily demand (L/day or Wh/day), source power curve (power vs time of day), storage (tank or battery), and the worst-month shortfall. **Checkpoint:** each team has a completed design sheet with a sketch, materials list, budget, and energy budget.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — teams present their design to the class in 1-minute pitches. The class asks one question each. Consolidate on the board: compare the designs. Which chose wind? Which chose water? Which has the lowest budget? Which has the most reliable supply? Confront the misconception that "bigger is always better" — a small, reliable system that meets the need is better than a large, ambitious system that fails in the dry season. Also address: "the design is finished when the drawing is done" — no, the energy budget is the critical test. If the worst-month shortfall is negative (demand exceeds supply), the design needs more storage, a bigger rotor, or a backup source. The energy budget is where physics meets reality.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a design brief turns a need into a plan with measurable targets — and the energy budget is the test that tells you if the plan works*. Homework: complete the design sheet with a final energy budget and a one-paragraph justification.

## Hands-On Build: School Power Design Brief
*Produce a complete design sheet for a real school power need.*

### Parts
- Design sheet (A4 with sections: Need, Site, Source, Design Drawing, Materials, Budget, Energy Budget)
- Pencils, rulers, erasers, graph paper
- The resource survey map from Lesson 326
- Local price list
- Calculators

### Workflow
1. **Choose a need.** Each team picks one school need: (a) garden watering — 200 L/day, (b) handwash station — 50 L/day, (c) toilet tank — 100 L/day, (d) school lighting — 100 Wh/day. Write the need and the daily demand on the design sheet. **Checkpoint:** the need and demand are written.
2. **Choose a site.** From the survey map, pick the best site for the chosen need. Write the site name, distance to the school, and the measured resource (wind speed or stream flow + head). **Checkpoint:** the site and resource data are written.
3. **Choose a source.** Wind or water? Use the power formulas: P_wind = ½ρAv³ (A = rotor area, v = wind speed), P_water = ρgQh (Q = flow, h = head). Compute the available power and the daily energy (power × hours of operation). **Checkpoint:** the source, available power, and daily energy are written.
4. **Sketch the design.** Draw the system: windmill or turbine, pipe or wire run, storage tank or battery, and the end use (garden tap, handwash basin, light). Label dimensions. **Checkpoint:** the sketch has dimensions and labels.
5. **List materials and budget.** List every component with local prices: blades, hub, tower poles, pipe (per metre), pump, tank, wire, fittings. Total the budget. **Checkpoint:** the materials list and total budget are written.
6. **Compute the energy budget.** Make a table: Daily Demand | Daily Supply | Storage Capacity | Worst-Month Supply | Shortfall. If the shortfall is negative, note what you would change (bigger rotor, more storage, backup source). **Checkpoint:** the energy budget table is complete with a shortfall analysis.

### Common Mistakes
- **Not using the survey data** — the whole point of Lesson 326 was to collect data for this design. If teams invent wind speeds or stream flows, the design is fiction. Use the real numbers.
- **Forgetting the worst month** — the design must work in the dry season, not just the rainy season. The worst-month supply is the critical number. If the stream dries up in August, the design needs a backup (rainwater tank, or a wind-only mode).
- **Not costing the pipe or wire run** — the distance from the source to the school matters. A 500 m pipe run at KSh 200/m is KSh 100,000 — more than the windmill. Distance is a major cost driver.
- **Ignoring storage** — wind and water are intermittent. Without a storage tank, the water only flows when the wind blows. The tank must be sized for the longest calm period (typically 1–3 days).
- **Designing beyond the budget** — a school project has a real budget limit. If the design costs KSh 50,000 and the school has KSh 10,000, the design is not feasible. Scale down or phase the project.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual.*

1. Your school garden needs 200 L/day. What survey data do you need to choose between wind and water? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is a design brief, and why must it have measurable targets? (mirrors pre-test)
3. If your windmill pumps 30 L/min but the wind only blows strongly for 2 hours per day, how much water can it deliver? (mirrors pre-test)
4. Your energy budget shows: daily demand 200 L, daily supply 150 L in the dry season. What is the shortfall, and what are two ways to fix it?
5. Challenge: why is the worst-month supply more important than the average annual supply in the energy budget?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Wind: average wind speed and hours per day. Water: stream flow and head. Also: distance to the garden and seasonal reliability.
2. A design brief states what you are building, for whom, and what it must achieve. Measurable targets let you check whether the finished design meets the need.
3. 30 L/min × 2 h = 30 × 120 = 3600 L at peak. Realistically about 1800 L accounting for variable wind. Either way, 200 L/day is easily met.
4. Shortfall = 200 − 150 = 50 L/day in the dry season. Fix 1: add a rainwater harvesting tank (Lesson 323) to supplement the windmill in the dry season. Fix 2: increase the storage tank so surplus water from windy days is stored for calm days. Fix 3: increase the rotor area to pump more water when the wind does blow. Fix 4: reduce demand (drip irrigation instead of overhead watering).
5. The worst-month supply determines whether the system fails. If the average annual supply is 300 L/day but the dry-season supply is 100 L/day, the system fails in the dry season even though the annual average looks fine. People need water every day, not just on average. The system must be designed for the worst month, not the average month. A system that works in the worst month will work in all months.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A school garden needs 200 L/day. The windmill pumps at 30 L/min when the wind is above 3 m/s. The anemometer log shows: 6 hours/day above 3 m/s in the windy season (3 months), 3 hours/day in the calm season (9 months). Size the storage tank so the garden never runs out.
   **Worked Answer:** Windy season: 30 L/min × 6 h × 60 min/h = 30 × 360 = 10,800 L/day. Far more than 200 L — surplus is 10,600 L. Calm season: 30 L/min × 3 h × 60 = 30 × 180 = 5400 L/day. Still far more than 200 L. The windmill produces 5400 L/day in the calm season — 27× the demand. The storage tank only needs to cover calm periods within a day: if the wind blows for 3 hours in the afternoon, the tank must hold enough water for the other 21 hours: 200 L × (21/24) = 175 L. A 200 L tank (one jerrican) is sufficient. But if there are days with no wind at all, the tank must cover 1–2 calm days: 200 × 2 = 400 L. A 500 L tank provides a 2-day buffer. The tank is small because the demand is small relative to the supply.
2. **Q:** Design a combined wind + rainwater system for a school handwash station needing 50 L/day. The school roof is 100 m², annual rainfall is 800 mm, and the dry season is 3 months. The windmill is the primary source; rainwater is the backup. Size the rainwater tank for the dry season.
   **Worked Answer:** Dry season demand: 50 L/day × 90 days = 4500 L. Rainwater in the dry season: the dry season has little rain (assume 50 mm over 3 months). Roof catchment: 100 m² × 0.05 m = 5 m³ = 5000 L. But the dry-season rain comes in a few storms — most of it falls in 2–3 events. The tank must store enough to bridge the gaps between storms. If the longest dry spell is 30 days: 50 × 30 = 1500 L. A 2000 L tank provides a 40-day buffer, which covers the longest dry spell. The windmill supplies 50 L/day when the wind blows; the rainwater tank fills the gaps. Total system: windmill + 2000 L rainwater tank + gutters on the school roof. The tank also fills during the rainy season, providing a full buffer for the start of the dry season.
3. **Q:** A team's energy budget shows: demand 200 L/day, windmill supply 250 L/day in the windy season but only 80 L/day in the calm season. The budget is KSh 8000. A second windmill would cost KSh 5000 and boost the calm-season supply to 160 L/day. A rainwater tank (2000 L) would cost KSh 3000 and supplement 40 L/day in the calm season. Which option closes the shortfall, and which is cheaper?
   **Worked Answer:** Calm-season shortfall: 200 − 80 = 120 L/day. Option A: second windmill. New supply: 160 L/day. Shortfall: 200 − 160 = 40 L/day. Not fully closed — still 40 L/day short. Cost: KSh 5000. Option B: rainwater tank. New supply: 80 + 40 = 120 L/day. Shortfall: 200 − 120 = 80 L/day. Not closed either — still 80 L/day short. Cost: KSh 3000. Option C: both. New supply: 160 + 40 = 200 L/day. Shortfall: 0. Cost: KSh 8000. Option C fully closes the shortfall and costs exactly the budget. But option C uses the entire budget with no margin for error. Option A + partial rainwater (a 1000 L tank for KSh 1500): supply = 160 + 20 = 180 L/day. Shortfall: 20 L/day. Cost: KSh 6500. This leaves KSh 1500 for contingencies and reduces the shortfall to 20 L/day — manageable with minor demand reduction (drip irrigation). The best engineering answer is Option A + a small rainwater tank, leaving margin in the budget.

## Safety Notes
- Design work is classroom-based — no special safety risks.
- If teams visit the site to check dimensions, follow the field safety rules from Lesson 326.
- Do not climb on roofs to measure catchment area — estimate from the ground or use the building plan.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 301–326 (all of Unit 14 up to this point). This lesson is the capstone design exercise — it pulls together wind measurement, water power, rainwater harvesting, energy storage, and the community survey.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the design drawing, materials list, and simple energy budget (demand vs supply). Ages 14–17 should do the full energy budget with worst-month analysis, compare options, and justify their choice with quantitative reasoning.
- Local resource tips: the price list should reflect real local prices. Ask a fundi or visit a hardware shop to get current prices for mabati, poles, pipe, fittings, and pumps. The design is only as good as the cost data.
- Invite a jua kali fabricator or a local engineer to review the designs if possible. Real-world feedback from a practitioner is invaluable.
- The design brief feeds directly into the showcase (Lesson 328), where teams present their designs alongside their windmill and turbine builds.
- If the school has a real need (garden, handwash station, toilet tank), this lesson can produce a real design that the school could implement — making the physics tangible and the learning permanent.
