# Lesson 328: Unit Review: Energy Projects Showcase

**Unit:** 14 — Wind & Water Power Projects  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-4.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Present their windmill, turbine, and design brief to an audience of peers, teachers, and visitors.
2. Explain the physics behind each project using the correct vocabulary (head, flow, power, efficiency, round-trip efficiency).
3. Answer audience questions about design choices, data, and failures.
4. Complete a unit quiz covering all 28 lessons.

## Materials
- All Unit 14 builds: windmills, turbines, anemometers, wind socks, design sheets
- Tables for displays
- Poster paper and markers for labels
- The unit quiz (12 questions, printed or on the board)
- Stopwatch (for timed presentations)
- A bell or whistle (for rotation)
- Prizes or certificates (optional)

## Vocabulary
- **showcase** (maonyesho) — a public display of work for an audience
- **performance curve** (mstatili wa utendaji) — a graph of output vs input (e.g., water pumped vs wind speed)
- **cut-in speed** (kasi ya kuanza) — the minimum wind speed at which the windmill starts to pump
- **round-trip efficiency** (uhalali wa mwendo) — the fraction of energy put in that is recovered from storage

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What is the cut-in speed of a windmill, and why does it matter?
2. Name two energy sources covered in this unit that do not require fuel.
3. What does a performance curve show, and what axis labels does it need?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The cut-in speed is the minimum wind speed at which the windmill starts to turn and pump water. Below this speed, the wind does not have enough power to overcome friction in the bearings and the pump. It matters because it tells you how many hours per day the windmill is productive — if the cut-in is 3 m/s and the wind averages 2 m/s, the windmill never runs.
2. Any two: wind, flowing water (stream, river), rainwater (gravity-fed), solar (mentioned in comparisons), gravity (for water distribution). All are renewable — they do not require fuel like petrol or diesel.
3. A performance curve shows the output of a system as a function of the input. For a windmill: y-axis = water pumped (L/min) or power output (W), x-axis = wind speed (m/s). For a turbine: y-axis = power output (W), x-axis = flow rate (L/s) or head (m).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — welcome visitors (if any). Frame the showcase: *today we show what we built, what we measured, and what we learned about wind and water power.* Each team has 2 minutes to present at their station, then the class rotates. Board: **show your build, tell your data, answer questions.**
3. **Explore (15 min)** — showcase stations. Each team sets up their best build (windmill, turbine, or design brief poster) at a table. Half the class presents while the other half visits; then they swap. Visitors try the anemometer, see the windmill pump, watch the turbine spin under a tap. Presenters explain: the design, the data (performance curve, cut-in speed, efficiency), the failures and fixes, and the cost. **Checkpoint:** every team has presented and every learner has visited all stations.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw the big picture of Unit 14: wind (Lessons 301–316) → water (317–320) → storage and distribution (321–325) → survey and design (326–327). The thread: *measure the resource → build the machine → test and iterate → design for a real need.* Confront the misconception that "a showcase is just a show" — no, the showcase is the assessment. The presentations reveal whether learners can explain the physics, defend their design choices, and answer questions. The quiz confirms the vocabulary and formulas. Also address: "our windmill didn't work, so we failed" — no, a windmill that didn't work is a learning opportunity. The team that can explain *why* it failed (friction in the bearing, blades too flat, tower too short) has learned more than the team whose windmill worked but can't explain why.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer the unit quiz (12 questions, see below). Collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *wind and water are free, but the engineering to capture them is not — measure, build, test, iterate, and design for the worst month*. Homework: write one paragraph answering: *Is small wind worth it here?* using data from your windmill tests.

## Post-Test: Unit 14 Quiz
*10 minutes, individual. 12 questions.*

1. What causes wind? (a) The moon (b) Uneven heating of land and water by the sun (c) Ocean currents (d) Earth's rotation only
2. The Lake Turkana wind corridor is famous because: (a) It is always calm (b) It has steady strong winds year-round (c) It is near the equator (d) It has no rain
3. Wind power is proportional to wind speed raised to what power? (a) v (b) v² (c) v³ (d) v⁴
4. A cup anemometer measures wind speed by: (a) Measuring temperature (b) Counting rotations per minute (c) Measuring air pressure (d) Measuring humidity
5. Hooke's Law (F = kx) applies to a spring scale. If k = 2 N/cm and the spring stretches 3 cm, what is the force?
6. In a wind pump, the crank converts: (a) Rotation to reciprocation (b) Reciprocation to rotation (c) Electricity to motion (d) Heat to motion
7. The available hydraulic power from a stream is given by: (a) ½ρAv³ (b) ρgQh (c) mg h (d) F = kx
8. An overshot water wheel is more efficient than an undershot wheel because: (a) It uses less water (b) It harvests the weight of the water, not just its speed (c) It is bigger (d) It uses metal instead of wood
9. A stream has flow 2 L/s and head 5 m. What is the available hydraulic power? (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s²)
10. Round-trip efficiency of a pumped storage system is 70%. If you put in 1000 J, how much do you get back?
11. In a gravity water scheme, break-pressure tanks are installed on long drops to: (a) Store more water (b) Reduce the static pressure in the pipe below (c) Measure the flow (d) Filter the water
12. In one sentence: what is the single most important factor when choosing between a wind site and a water site for a school pump?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. (b) Uneven heating of land and water by the sun. Land heats faster than water → warm air rises over land → cool air flows in from the water → wind. The reverse happens at night.
2. (b) It has steady strong winds year-round. The Turkana channel funnels wind between the two rift valleys, creating one of the world's best wind resources.
3. (c) v³. Wind power = ½ρAv³. Doubling the wind speed gives 8× the power.
4. (b) Counting rotations per minute. The cups catch the wind and spin; the rotation rate is proportional to the wind speed.
5. F = kx = 2 × 3 = 6 N.
6. (a) Rotation to reciprocation. The crank on the rotor shaft converts the circular motion of the rotor into the up-and-down motion of the pump rod.
7. (b) ρgQh. ρ = water density, g = gravity, Q = flow rate, h = head.
8. (b) It harvests the weight of the water, not just its speed. The overshot wheel uses the gravitational potential energy of the water (mgh); the undershot only uses the kinetic energy (½mv²), which is much less for the same water.
9. P = ρgQh = 1000 × 10 × 0.002 × 5 = 100 W. (2 L/s = 0.002 m³/s.)
10. 1000 × 0.70 = 700 J. You get back 700 J; 300 J is lost to friction, turbulence, and heat.
11. (b) Reduce the static pressure in the pipe below. Without break-pressure tanks, a 120 m drop would create 12 bar of pressure at the bottom — far more than a plastic pipe can withstand. The tank vents to atmosphere, resetting the pressure to zero.
12. Any reasonable answer that uses data: "Compare the available power — P_wind = ½ρAv³ vs P_water = ρgQh — and choose the site with more power at the worst time of year." Or: "The site with the most reliable year-round resource, not the highest peak power." Or: "The one closest to the school with enough power to meet the daily demand."

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your windmill has a rotor diameter of 1 m. The average wind speed at the site is 4 m/s. The measured efficiency (from rotor to water delivered) is 15%. How much water can it pump per hour from a 2 m head? (ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s².)
   **Worked Answer:** Rotor area: A = π × (0.5)² = 0.785 m². Wind power: P_wind = ½ρAv³ = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.785 × 4³ = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.785 × 64 = 30.1 W. Hydraulic power delivered: P_hyd = P_wind × η = 30.1 × 0.15 = 4.5 W. Water lifted per second: P_hyd = ρgQh → Q = P_hyd / (ρgh) = 4.5 / (1000 × 10 × 2) = 4.5 / 20,000 = 0.000225 m³/s = 0.225 L/s. Per hour: 0.225 × 3600 = 810 L/h. That's a lot — in practice, the wind doesn't blow at 4 m/s constantly, and the efficiency might be lower. A realistic daily output might be 200–400 L.
2. **Q:** A panel question: "Is small wind worth it here?" Argue both sides using data from your windmill tests.
   **Worked Answer:** For: Small wind is worth it if (a) the site has consistent wind above the cut-in speed for several hours per day, (b) the demand is small and intermittent (garden watering, tank filling — not continuous power), (c) the windmill can be built and maintained locally with jua kali skills, and (d) the alternative (grid extension, diesel pump, or solar pump) is more expensive or unavailable. The data: if the windmill pumps 200 L/day and the alternative is carrying jerricans (200 L × 20 m distance = significant labour), the windmill pays for itself in saved labour within months. Against: Small wind is not worth it if (a) the wind is too variable — calm for days at a time, making the system unreliable without large storage, (b) the maintenance burden is high — bearings wear out, blades break in storms, the pump needs regular oiling, (c) a solar pump of the same cost produces more water per day in most Kenyan locations (solar is more predictable than wind in the tropics), and (d) the school is near the grid, where a grid-powered pump is cheaper and more reliable. The data: if the windmill produces 200 L/day but a solar pump of the same cost produces 400 L/day, solar is the better investment. The honest answer depends on the site. The Turkana corridor: wind wins. Most of Kenya: solar wins. The school's own data from Lessons 313–315 is the evidence.
3. **Q:** Design a combined wind + solar + rainwater system for a school needing 500 L/day. The windmill produces 200 L/day (average), a solar pump produces 300 L/day (average), and the rainwater tank provides 50 L/day (dry-season average). What is the total daily supply? Is there a shortfall? If the solar pump fails for 3 days (cloudy weather), does the system still meet demand?
   **Worked Answer:** Total daily supply: 200 + 300 + 50 = 550 L/day. Demand: 500 L/day. Surplus: 50 L/day — the system meets demand with a small margin. If the solar pump fails for 3 days: supply = 200 + 50 = 250 L/day. Shortfall: 500 − 250 = 250 L/day. Over 3 days: 750 L shortfall. The rainwater tank must hold at least 750 L to bridge the gap. If the tank is 2000 L (sized from Lesson 323), it can cover 3 days of solar failure with surplus. The system is resilient because it has three independent sources — if one fails, the other two plus storage cover the gap. This is the principle of redundancy: never rely on a single source. The windmill provides night-time and early-morning pumping (when solar is off); the solar pump provides midday pumping (when wind is often calm); the rainwater tank provides a buffer. The three sources complement each other temporally.

## Safety Notes
- No special safety risks — this is a classroom showcase and quiz.
- If visitors attend, ensure the display area is clear of trip hazards (tools, tubes, wires).
- Do not operate windmills indoors — they need wind. Display them static, with photos of test day.
- The turbine can be demonstrated under a tap, but the floor will get wet — have a mop ready.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: all of Unit 14 (Lessons 301–327). This lesson is the capstone review and assessment.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 present their build and answer simple questions about what it does and how. Ages 14–17 present their performance data, efficiency calculations, and design brief, and answer quantitative questions from the audience.
- The quiz covers the full unit: wind (Q1–4), Hooke's Law and cranks (Q5–6), water power (Q7–9), storage and distribution (Q10–11), and synthesis (Q12). It is designed to be completed in 10 minutes with no calculator (except Q5 and Q9, which require simple arithmetic).
- The showcase format (stations, rotation, peer visits) keeps all learners active. The 2-minute presentation limit forces concision. The class swap ensures everyone both presents and visits.
- If visitors attend (a jua kali fabricator, a local engineer, a parent, a teacher from another school), ask them to ask one question per team. Real-world questions from practitioners are the best assessment.
- The homework question — "Is small wind worth it here?" — is the unit's synthesis question. There is no single right answer; the quality of the answer depends on the quality of the evidence (the windmill data from Lessons 313–315). This is engineering reasoning: the answer depends on the data, and the data comes from building and testing.
- This unit is the culmination of the mechanics curriculum: it applies forces (Unit 12), elasticity and structures (Unit 13), energy, power, and efficiency — all in the service of a real engineering problem. The windmill and turbine projects are the most complex builds in the curriculum, and the design brief is the most open-ended task. By this point, learners have the tools to design, build, test, and justify a real energy system for their school.
