# Lesson 329: What Is Sound? Feel the Vibration

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that sound is produced by a vibration — a rapid back-and-forth movement of an object.
2. Feel and observe vibration in a drummed ngoma skin, a humming throat, and a struck ruler clamped on a desk.
3. Describe how the vibration reaches the ear through the air as a travelling wave.
4. Explain why a shorter vibrating ruler produces a higher pitch (faster vibration rate).

## Materials
- A drum (ngoma) or a sufuria turned upside down and a stick to strike it
- A few grains of dry maize or small pebbles to rest on the drum skin
- A wooden or plastic ruler, about 30 cm, one per pair
- A desk or table edge to clamp the ruler
- A long sisal rope or a piece of bicycle inner tube (to show a visible wave)
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **vibration** (mtetemo) — a rapid back-and-forth movement; what sound is made of
- **pitch** (sauti ya juu au chini) — how high or low a sound is; a faster vibration makes a higher pitch
- **wave** (wimbi) — a vibration that travels through a material, carrying sound from source to ear
- **source** (chanzo) — the object that vibrates and starts the sound
- **medium** (nyenzo) — the material the sound travels through, here the air

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. When a drum is beaten, what is actually moving — the drum, the air, or both?
2. Place your fingers on your throat and hum. What do you feel?
3. If you strike a ruler clamped on a desk, then push the ruler out so more of it hangs over the edge and strike again, will the new note be higher, lower, or the same?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Both — the drum skin vibrates, and that vibration makes the air particles next to it vibrate, passing the sound along.
2. A buzzing or trembling feeling — the vocal cords are vibrating.
3. Lower — a longer overhang vibrates more slowly, giving a lower pitch. (Accept predictions; the test will confirm.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before touching the instruments.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — strike the drum once and ask: *Where is the sound coming from?* Then rest a few grains of dry maize on the drum skin and strike again — let learners watch the grains jump. Say: *The skin is moving — that movement is the sound.* Now ask every learner to press a hand to their throat and say "aaaa". Ask: *What do you feel?* The throat buzzes. Tell learners that today they prove sound is vibration.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in pairs, each pair clamps a ruler on a desk edge, leaving about 15 cm overhanging, and flicks the free end. They watch the tip blur back and forth and listen. Then they press a fingertip lightly on the vibrating tip and feel it go numb — the vibration stops and so does the sound. Next they change the overhang: 10 cm, then 20 cm, and listen each time. They record overhang length and whether the note was higher or lower. The teacher walks between pairs checking that they press gently and do not snap the ruler.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board: draw a drum skin, an arrow up, an arrow down — that is one vibration. The skin bumps the air, the air bumps the next air, and the wave travels to the ear. Confront the misconception that *sound itself is a substance that flies through the air* — no object travels; only the vibration passes from particle to particle. Then link the ruler: shorter overhang vibrates faster → higher pitch; longer overhang vibrates slower → lower pitch. Pitch is how fast the source vibrates.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *sound is vibration that travels; faster vibration means higher pitch.* Homework: find three sound sources at home (a sufuria lid, a radio speaker, a chicken clucking) and for each, say what part is vibrating.

## Hands-On Build: The Vibration Station
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Set up three stations around the room.*

### Parts
- A drum or an upside-down sufuria with a stick
- A few grains of dry maize or small pebbles
- A 30 cm ruler per pair
- A desk or table edge
- A sisal rope or piece of bicycle inner tube, about 2 m

### Workflow
1. **Drum station:** rest three grains of maize on the drum skin. Strike softly and watch how high the grains jump; strike hard and watch them jump higher. **Checkpoint:** the grains clearly move more on the hard strike.
2. **Ruler station:** clamp a ruler on the desk edge with 15 cm overhanging. Flick the free end and watch the tip blur. Press a fingertip on the tip — the vibration and the sound stop together. **Checkpoint:** pressing the tip kills both the movement and the sound.
3. **Roverhang test:** flick the ruler at 10 cm overhang, then at 20 cm overhang. Listen and record which note is higher. **Checkpoint:** the shorter overhang gives the higher note.
4. **Rope wave:** lay the rope on the ground or stretch it between two learners. Flick one end up and down once and watch the bump travel to the other end. **Checkpoint:** a single bump travels the full length — a wave moving without the rope itself going anywhere.

### Common Mistakes
- **Pressing the ruler too hard at the clamp** — the ruler snaps or bends and gives no clear note. Clamp just firmly enough to hold it; flick gently.
- **Confusing pitch with loudness** — a harder flick makes a louder note, not a higher one. To change pitch, change the overhang length, not the flick force.
- **Letting the drum grains roll off** — use a flat drum skin or sufuria and only a few grains; too many scatter and the jump is hard to see.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. When a drum is beaten, what is moving — the drum skin, the air, or both? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Press a fingertip on the tip of a vibrating ruler clamped on a desk. What happens to the vibration and the sound? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A learner clamps a ruler with a short overhang and strikes it, then with a long overhang and strikes again. Which overhang gives the higher pitch, and why?
4. Sound is described as a "travelling wave." In one sentence, say what travels from the drum to your ear — the drum skin, the air particles, or the vibration.
5. Challenge: a mosquito's wings beat very fast and make a high whine; a bull's chest rumbles slowly and makes a low moo. Which sound comes from the faster vibration, and what does this tell you about pitch?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Both — the drum skin vibrates, and that vibration makes the air next to it vibrate, passing the sound to the ear.
2. Both the vibration and the sound stop — pressing the tip stops the back-and-forth movement, so there is nothing to pass on.
3. The shorter overhang gives the higher pitch because the shorter ruler vibrates faster (more back-and-forth movements per second).
4. The vibration travels — no air particle or drum skin travels all the way to the ear; the wave passes from particle to particle.
5. The mosquito's whine comes from the faster vibration. Faster vibration means higher pitch; slower vibration means lower pitch.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A learner clamps a ruler so that 12 cm overhangs and hears a note, then shortens the overhang to 6 cm and hears a note one octave higher. Use the idea that vibration frequency is inversely proportional to length (f ∝ 1/L) to explain the octave.
   **Worked Answer:** If f ∝ 1/L, then halving the length doubles the frequency: f₂/f₁ = L₁/L₂ = 12/6 = 2. A frequency ratio of 2 is exactly one octave, so the shorter ruler sounds one octave higher.
2. **Q:** The ruler vibrates at about 120 Hz when 10 cm overhangs. Predict the frequency when the overhang is 20 cm of the same ruler, and state whether the note is higher or lower.
   **Worked Answer:** Since f ∝ 1/L, doubling the length halves the frequency: f₂ = f₁ × (L₁/L₂) = 120 Hz × (10/20) = 120 × 0.5 = 60 Hz. The note is lower (half the frequency).
3. **Q:** Explain why the ruler's pitch rises as you shorten the overhang, linking the length to the vibration rate.
   **Worked Answer:** The ruler behaves like a vibrating reed. A shorter free length is stiffer and has less mass to move, so the restoring force snaps it back faster after each displacement. A faster back-and-forth means more cycles per second, that is a higher frequency, and the ear hears a higher pitch. Because f ∝ 1/L, shortening the overhang raises the frequency and the pitch together.

## Safety Notes
- A flicked ruler can sting a finger or fly off the desk. Flick gently, keep faces away, and do not point the free end at another learner.
- Striking a drum hard can send the stick flying or hit a hand. Strike the centre of the skin and keep hands clear of the stick's path.
- Do not clamp the ruler so hard it snaps — a snapped ruler has sharp edges; use a plastic ruler, not a steel one, for the overhang test.
- Maize grains on the drum can be a choking hazard for the youngest learners; supervise and collect them after the demo.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners will have seen drums and radios at home but may never have linked the sound to a visible vibration. This lesson makes that link concrete.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on feeling and watching the vibration and on the simple shorter-higher observation. Ages 14–17 work the f ∝ 1/L relationship in the Advanced Questions and explain the octave.
- Local resource tips: a ngoma, an upside-down sufuria, or even a thick book laid flat can serve as the drum surface. The ruler is standard classroom stock; a 30 cm wooden or plastic ruler works best. Sisal rope or a piece of old bicycle inner tube is available at any jua kali workshop.
- A common surprise: some learners will insist they "saw the ruler move" as a whole rather than vibrate. Slow the flick and ask them to watch only the tip — the tip blurs because it moves back and forth many times, but the clamped end does not move.
- CBE assessment note: the recorded overhang-vs-pitch table and the fingertip-on-tip observation are evidence for the "sound is vibration" competency under G10-PHY-2.1.
