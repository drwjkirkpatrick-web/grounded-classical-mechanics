# Lesson 330: Sound Needs a Medium

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that sound travels through solids (a desk, a string) and through air.
2. Describe a demonstration that shows sound weakening or dying as air is removed from a container.
3. State that sound cannot travel through a vacuum — there are no particles to pass on the vibration.
4. Explain why the Moon is silent even when rocks strike its surface.

## Materials
- A glass bottle or jar with a tight-fitting stopper or cork
- A small bell, a bead, or a few dry maize seeds inside the bottle
- A hand pump or bicycle pump (optional — if none, the teacher demonstrates with a tightly stoppered bottle and reasons with the class)
- String to tie the bell or bead inside the bottle so it hangs freely
- A desk or table for the "sound through solid" test
- A length of sisal string (about 2 m) for the "sound through string" test
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **medium** (nyenzo) — the material a sound wave travels through; air, water, wood, string, metal
- **vacuum** (utupu) — a space with no particles at all; sound cannot cross it
- **particle** (chembe) — a tiny piece of matter; sound needs particles to bump into each other to travel
- **transmit** (kuhamisha) — to pass sound from one place to another through a medium
- **collisions** (migongano) — particles bumping into each other; how a vibration passes through a medium

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Can you hear sound through a wall? Guess yes or no.
2. If all the air is pumped out of a bottle with a bell inside, do you think you would still hear the bell? Guess yes or no.
3. Astronauts on the Moon cannot hear each other speak even when they are close. Why do you think that is?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Yes — sound travels through solids. The wall vibrates and passes the sound. (Accept predictions; the test will confirm.)
2. No — without air there are no particles to carry the vibration, so the bell goes silent. (Accept predictions; the demo will confirm.)
3. The Moon has almost no air (it is nearly a vacuum), so there is no medium to carry the sound from one astronaut's mouth to another's ear.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before handling the bottle.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the bottle with the bell inside and shake it so the class hears the bell clearly. Ask: *What is carrying the sound from the bell to your ears?* (The air inside.) Now ask: *What if we took the air out?* Let learners guess. Tell them that today they test whether sound needs something to travel through — a medium.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — first, the "sound through solid" test: one learner presses an ear to the desk while another taps the desk lightly with a fingernail at the far end. The listener hears the tap clearly through the wood — a solid medium. Next, the "sound through string" test: two learners each hold one end of a taut sisal string; one plucks the string and the other feels the vibration at their end — the string carries the sound. Finally, the bell-in-a-bottle demo: the teacher shakes the bottle so all hear the bell; then, if a pump is available, pumps air out and shakes again — the bell sounds fainter or silent. If no pump, the teacher stoppers the bottle tightly, explains that a real vacuum pump would remove the air, and asks learners to predict what they would hear. The class records each result.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board: draw three particles in a row, the first bumps the second, the second bumps the third — that is how sound travels through a medium. Air, wood, water, and string all have particles that can bump. A vacuum has no particles, so there is nothing to pass the bump — sound dies. Confront the misconception that *sound can travel through "nothing" because it is a wave* — a wave is a vibration of something, and with nothing to vibrate, there is no sound. Then link to the Moon: the Moon's surface has almost no atmosphere, so rocks crashing or astronauts shouting make no sound that travels — the Moon is silent.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *sound needs a medium; no particles, no sound; that is why the Moon is silent.* Homework: at home, press your ear to a door and have someone knock on the other side; write one sentence on what carried the sound to your ear.

## Hands-On Build: The Bell-in-a-Bottle Demonstration
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. The teacher prepares the bottle before the lesson.*

### Parts
- A glass bottle or jar with a tight stopper or cork
- A small bell, a bead, or a few dry maize seeds
- String to hang the bell or bead inside the bottle so it does not touch the walls
- A hand pump or bicycle pump with a needle valve (optional)
- A desk or table for the solid-medium test
- About 2 m of sisal string for the string-medium test

### Workflow
1. Tie the bell or bead to the inside of the stopper with string so it hangs freely in the middle of the bottle. Stopper the bottle. **Checkpoint:** the bell hangs free and does not touch the glass when shaken.
2. Shake the bottle gently so the whole class hears the bell. **Checkpoint:** all learners can hear the bell clearly through the air inside.
3. **Solid test:** one learner presses an ear to the desk; another taps the far end with a fingernail. The listener reports what they hear. **Checkpoint:** the tap is heard clearly through the wood.
4. **String test:** two learners hold a taut sisal string; one plucks, the other feels the vibration. **Checkpoint:** the vibration is felt at the far end — the string carried it.
5. **Vacuum test (if a pump is available):** connect the pump to the bottle, pump air out for about a minute, then shake. The bell sounds faint or silent. If no pump, stopper the bottle tightly and explain what a vacuum pump would do; ask learners to predict. **Checkpoint:** learners state that with less or no air, the bell is fainter or silent.

### Common Mistakes
- **The bell touching the bottle wall** — if the bell rests on the glass, shaking the bottle makes the glass rattle and the bell sound is muddled. Hang the bell free with string.
- **A loose stopper** — air leaks back in and the vacuum test fails. Press the stopper firmly; if using a pump, check the seal around the valve.
- **Confusing "less loud" with "no sound"** — a school hand pump cannot make a perfect vacuum, so the bell may only get quieter. Stress that a perfect vacuum would silence the bell completely; the demo shows the trend.
- **Tapping the desk too hard** — a light fingernail tap is enough; a hard knock hurts the ear pressed to the desk.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. Can sound travel through a wall? Say yes or no and name the medium. (mirrors pre-test)
2. If all the air is pumped out of a bottle with a bell inside, can you still hear the bell? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A learner presses an ear to a desk and hears a tap at the far end. What carried the sound — the air, the wood, or both?
4. The Moon has almost no atmosphere. In one sentence, explain why two astronauts standing close on the Moon cannot hear each other speak.
5. Challenge: a learner argues that sound is a wave, so it should travel through empty space like light does. Using the idea of particles bumping, say why this argument is wrong.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Yes — the wall is the medium; the wall vibrates and passes the sound through the solid.
2. No — without air there are no particles to carry the vibration, so the bell is silent.
3. The wood carried the sound directly (the solid medium); the air also carries some sound, but the clearest path is through the desk.
4. With almost no atmosphere, there are no air particles to bump and carry the vibration from one mouth to the other's ear, so sound cannot travel.
5. Sound is a vibration of particles — each particle bumps the next. Empty space has no particles, so there is nothing to bump and the vibration cannot pass. Light is different — it is an electromagnetic wave that does not need a medium, so light crosses empty space but sound does not.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A bottle contains a bell in air. As air is pumped out, the bell sounds fainter. Argue from particle collisions why the sound dies, and state what remains in the bottle when a perfect vacuum is reached.
   **Worked Answer:** Sound travels by successive particle collisions — each air particle bumps the next, passing the vibration from the bell to the glass to the outside air. As air is removed, there are fewer particles per unit volume, so fewer collisions happen per second and less vibration energy reaches the glass; the bell sounds fainter. In a perfect vacuum there are no particles at all, so no collisions occur and no sound is transmitted. What remains is the bell, the string, the glass, and empty space — but no air, so the bell is silent even though it is still shaking.
2. **Q:** The speed of sound in air is about 340 m/s, in water about 1500 m/s, and in steel about 5000 m/s. Explain why sound travels faster in steel than in air, using the particle idea.
   **Worked Answer:** In steel the particles are packed close together and held rigidly, so when one particle is pushed it bumps its neighbour almost immediately — the collision passes on very quickly. In air the particles are far apart and move freely, so each particle must travel some distance before colliding, slowing the passage of the vibration. Closer packing and stiffer bonds mean faster transmission, so sound in steel is about 15 times faster than in air.
3. **Q:** A rock slide on the Moon sends boulders crashing together, but no sound is heard. A nearby astronaut with a radio hears a warning. Explain the difference: why is the crash silent but the radio audible?
   **Worked Answer:** The crashing boulders produce vibrations, but the Moon has almost no atmosphere, so there are no particles to carry the sound waves to the astronaut's ear — the crash is silent. The radio, however, transmits an electromagnetic signal (radio waves) that does not need a medium, so it crosses the vacuum between the Moon's surface and the astronaut's radio. The astronaut's radio then turns that signal into sound in the air inside the helmet, which the astronaut hears. Sound needs a medium; radio waves do not.

## Safety Notes
- Pumping air out of a glass bottle can cause the bottle to crack or implode if the glass is thin or flawed. Use a thick-walled jar or a plastic bottle; inspect for cracks before pumping; wear safety goggles during the vacuum test.
- Do not shake the bottle so hard that the bell strikes the glass and cracks it; shake gently.
- Pressing an ear hard to a desk can hurt the ear; press lightly and tap the desk softly with a fingernail, not a fist.
- If using a bicycle pump with a needle valve, the needle is sharp; handle by the barrel, not the point.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have covered that sound is a vibration (Lesson 329). This lesson asks what that vibration travels through.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the three tests (solid, string, air) and the simple rule "no air, no sound." Ages 14–17 work the particle-collision argument and the Moon example in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: a glass jar from a bought jam or pickle works well; a plastic water bottle is safer for the vacuum test. A small bell can be bought at a dukani; a bead or dry maize seeds work just as well. A bicycle pump is common in schools and homes; if none is available, the teacher demonstrates with a tightly stoppered bottle and reasons through the vacuum case — the prediction is the key learning, not a perfect vacuum.
- A common surprise: learners may expect the bell to sound the same after pumping. The fainter sound (or the prediction of silence) is the teachable moment — connect it to the Moon example before the post-test.
- CBE assessment note: the recorded results of the three tests and the one-sentence Moon explanation are evidence for the "sound needs a medium" competency under G10-PHY-2.1.
