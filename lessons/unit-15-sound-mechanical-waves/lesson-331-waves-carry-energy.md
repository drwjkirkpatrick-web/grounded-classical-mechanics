# Lesson 331: Waves Carry Energy, Not Stuff

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Show that a wave carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter along with it.
2. Demonstrate transverse waves on a rope and longitudinal waves on a spring or rubber tube.
3. Label the parts of a wave: crest, trough, wavelength, and amplitude on a sketch.
4. Distinguish the speed of the wave from the speed of the moving particles of the medium.

## Materials
- A shallow basin or a wide sufuria half-filled with water
- A dry leaf or a small piece of paper to float on the water
- A long sisal rope (about 3 m) for transverse waves
- A coiled spring or a length of rubber tube (bicycle inner tube) for longitudinal waves
- Chalk or a marker to mark points on the rope
- A ruler or tape measure
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **wave** (wimbi) — a vibration that travels and carries energy from one place to another
- **energy** (nguvu) — what the wave carries; the ability to do work, such as moving a leaf or an eardrum
- **transverse wave** (wimbi jembamba) — a wave where the particles move up and down while the wave travels along; a rope wave
- **longitudinal wave** (wimbi refu) — a wave where the particles move back and forth along the direction the wave travels; a spring wave and a sound wave
- **crest** (kilele) — the highest point of a transverse wave
- **trough** (beshte) — the lowest point of a transverse wave
- **wavelength** (urefu wa wimbi) — the distance from one crest to the next crest
- **amplitude** (kimo cha wimbi) — the height of the crest from the rest position; bigger amplitude means more energy

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you drop a stone into a basin of water with a leaf floating on it, does the leaf travel across the basin or just bob up and down?
2. When you flick one end of a rope up and down, does the rope itself move to the other end, or does only the wave shape move?
3. A wave carries something from one place to another. What does it carry — the material of the rope, or energy?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The leaf bobs up and down (or moves in a small circle) but does not travel across the basin — the wave passes through the water and the leaf stays roughly in place.
2. Only the wave shape moves along the rope; the rope itself stays where it is, each piece just going up and down.
3. The wave carries energy, not the material of the rope or water.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before handling the water and rope.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — float the leaf in the basin of water. Ask: *If I drop a stone in, will the leaf be pushed to the other side?* Let learners guess. Drop a small stone near one edge and watch the ripples spread. The leaf bobs up and down but stays where it is. Say: *The wave moved, the leaf did not — the wave carries energy, not stuff.* Tell learners that today they prove this with water, rope, and a spring.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners do three tasks. First, the basin test: each group floats a leaf in a shallow sufuria of water, drops a stone nearby, and records whether the leaf travels or stays. Second, the rope test: two learners stretch a sisal rope between them; one flicks one end up and down to send a wave along the rope; they mark a spot on the rope with chalk and watch whether the chalk mark travels to the other end or just goes up and down. Third, the spring test: with a coiled spring or rubber tube stretched on the ground, one learner squeezes a few coils together at one end and releases — a compressed pulse travels along the spring; they note that the squeeze moves along the spring while the coils themselves only shift back and forth. The teacher walks between groups checking the rope tension and the squeeze pulse.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board: draw a transverse wave and label crest, trough, wavelength (crest-to-crest), and amplitude (rest line to crest). Draw a longitudinal wave and label compression and rarefaction. Confront the misconception that *the wave carries the water or the rope to the far end* — the leaf stays put, and the chalk mark on the rope goes up and down but does not travel. The wave carries energy; the medium stays. Then distinguish wave speed (how fast the shape travels along) from particle speed (how fast each piece of rope moves up and down). The wave can race along the rope while each piece of rope moves slowly up and down.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a wave carries energy, not stuff; the medium stays in place.* Homework: sketch a rope wave and label crest, trough, wavelength, and amplitude; bring the sketch next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: The Wave Trio
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Set up three stations around the room.*

### Parts
- A shallow basin or a wide sufuria half-filled with water
- A dry leaf or a small piece of paper to float on the water
- A long sisal rope, about 3 m, with a chalk mark near one end
- A coiled spring or a length of bicycle inner tube (rubber tube)
- Chalk or a marker
- A ruler or tape measure

### Workflow
1. **Basin station:** float the leaf in the water. Drop a small stone near one edge. Watch the ripples spread and the leaf bob. **Checkpoint:** the leaf stays roughly in place while the ripples pass — the wave carried energy, not the leaf.
2. **Rope station (transverse):** two learners stretch the rope. One flicks one end up and down once and watches the bump travel to the other end. **Checkpoint:** a single bump travels the full length — a transverse wave moving, the rope staying.
3. **Rope chalk mark:** mark a spot on the rope with chalk. Send a wave and watch the mark. **Checkpoint:** the chalk mark moves up and down but does not travel to the other end — the rope itself did not move along.
4. **Spring station (longitudinal):** stretch the spring or rubber tube on the ground. Squeeze a few coils together at one end and release. **Checkpoint:** a compressed pulse travels along the spring — a longitudinal wave; the coils only shift back and forth, they do not travel to the far end.
5. **Sketch:** each learner draws the rope wave and labels crest, trough, wavelength, and amplitude. **Checkpoint:** all four labels are on the sketch.

### Common Mistakes
- **Confusing wave motion with matter motion** — learners may think the wave pushes the water to the far side. The leaf test shows the water stays; only the energy moves.
- **Slack rope** — a slack rope absorbs the flick and the wave does not travel. Pull the rope taut before flicking.
- **Squeezing too many coils** — a big squeeze makes a slow, muddy pulse. Squeeze only a few coils for a clean longitudinal pulse.
- **Mixing up wave speed and particle speed** — learners may think the rope pieces move as fast as the wave. The wave races along while each piece of rope moves only a short distance up and down. Use the chalk mark to show this.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. A leaf floats on water in a basin. A stone is dropped in nearby. Does the leaf travel across the basin or stay in place? (mirrors pre-test)
2. When a wave travels along a rope, does the rope itself move to the other end? (mirrors pre-test)
3. On a transverse wave sketch, what is the difference between the crest and the trough?
4. A learner marks a spot on a rope with chalk, then sends a wave along the rope. Describe what the chalk mark does while the wave passes.
5. Challenge: sound is a longitudinal wave in air. In one sentence, explain why a sound wave reaching your ear does not carry air from the speaker's mouth all the way to you.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The leaf stays roughly in place, bobbing up and down — the wave carries energy, not the leaf.
2. No — only the wave shape travels; the rope stays where it is, each piece moving up and down.
3. The crest is the highest point of the wave; the trough is the lowest point.
4. The chalk mark moves up and down (or back and forth) but does not travel to the other end — the rope itself did not move along.
5. A sound wave is a vibration of air particles; each particle bumps its neighbour and returns to its place, passing the energy along. No air particle travels all the way from the speaker to the listener — only the wave energy reaches the ear.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** On a rope sketch, label crest, trough, wavelength, and amplitude. If the wavelength is 2 m and the amplitude is 0.3 m, state what each quantity measures and distinguish wave speed from particle motion.
   **Worked Answer:** Wavelength (2 m) is the distance from one crest to the next crest — the length of one full cycle of the wave. Amplitude (0.3 m) is the height of the crest from the rest position — it measures the size of the vibration and the energy the wave carries. Wave speed is how fast the wave shape travels along the rope (in m/s); particle motion is how fast each piece of the rope moves up and down. The wave can travel along the rope at several metres per second while each piece of rope moves only fractions of a metre up and down — the two speeds are different.
2. **Q:** A transverse wave on a rope travels at 4 m/s. Each piece of rope moves up and down through a distance of 0.2 m (amplitude) at a frequency of 2 Hz. Distinguish the wave speed from the particle speed, and compute the distance the wave travels in 5 s.
   **Worked Answer:** Wave speed = 4 m/s is the speed of the wave shape along the rope. Particle speed is the speed of each piece of rope moving up and down — the particle travels 0.2 m up and 0.2 m down, twice per second (at 2 Hz), so the particle speed is roughly 0.8 m/s on average. The wave speed (4 m/s) is much faster than the particle speed (0.8 m/s). Distance the wave travels in 5 s = wave speed × time = 4 m/s × 5 s = 20 m. The wave moves 20 m along the rope while each piece of rope only bobs up and down in place.
3. **Q:** A sound wave is a longitudinal wave in air. Explain how a compression and a rarefaction form, and use the particle-collision idea to argue that air does not flow from the speaker to the listener.
   **Worked Answer:** When a speaker cone pushes forward, it squeezes the air particles in front of it into a region of higher pressure — a compression. When it pulls back, it spreads the particles apart into a region of lower pressure — a rarefaction. The compression and rarefaction then travel outward as the particles bump their neighbours: each particle shifts a little in the direction of the wave, bumps the next particle, and returns, so the compressions and rarefactions move along while the particles themselves only oscillate about fixed positions. No air parcel travels from the speaker to the listener — only the pattern of compressions and rarefactions (the energy) moves. That is why a sound wave carries energy, not stuff.

## Safety Notes
- Water spilled on the floor is a slip hazard; fill the basin only halfway and keep it on a table or the ground, not carried around; mop up spills at once.
- A stretched rope can snap back and sting if released under tension; release the rope gently, do not let go while flicking.
- A coiled spring under tension can pinch a finger or snap; stretch it gently and keep fingers clear of the coils when releasing the squeeze.
- Dropping a stone into the basin can splash water into eyes; drop small stones from a low height and lean back after releasing.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that sound is a vibration (Lesson 329) and that sound needs a medium (Lesson 330). This lesson generalizes the idea to all waves: they carry energy, not matter.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the three stations and the simple rule "the wave moves, the stuff stays." Ages 14–17 work the crest/trough/wavelength/amplitude labels and the wave-speed-vs-particle-speed distinction in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: a wide sufuria serves as the basin; a dry leaf or a scrap of paper floats on the water. A sisal rope of 3 m is available at any dukani or jua kali workshop. A bicycle inner tube makes a good substitute for a coiled spring for the longitudinal wave; a real coiled spring from a broken mattress or a jua kali hardware shop is better if available. Chalk marks on the rope are the key evidence that the rope itself does not travel.
- A common surprise: learners may insist the leaf "moved a little" and take that as the wave carrying matter. Acknowledge the small bobbing and the slight drift, but stress that the leaf did not travel across the basin — that is the difference between the wave and the water flow.
- CBE assessment note: the labelled wave sketch and the chalk-mark observation are evidence for the "waves carry energy, not stuff" competency under G10-PHY-2.1.
