# Lesson 332: Wavelength, Frequency, Amplitude and v = fλ

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Count the frequency of a rope wave by counting crests passing a point per second.
2. Measure the wavelength of a rope wave using chalk marks and a tape measure.
3. State the wave equation v = fλ and use it to find the speed of a rope wave and a sound wave.
4. Predict how wavelength changes when frequency changes (higher frequency means shorter wavelength) for a fixed wave speed.

## Materials
- A long sisal rope (about 3 m) stretched between two learners
- Coloured chalk or a marker to mark crests on the rope
- A tape measure or a metre rule
- A stopwatch or a phone clock (for counting waves per second)
- A drum or a sufuria and a stick (to link rope waves to sound waves)
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **frequency** (marudio) — the number of waves that pass a point each second; measured in hertz (Hz)
- **wavelength** (urefu wa wimbi) — the distance from one crest to the next crest; symbol λ (lambda)
- **amplitude** (kimo cha wimbi) — the height of the crest from the rest position; bigger amplitude means more energy
- **wave speed** (kasi ya wimbi) — how fast the wave shape travels along; symbol v, measured in metres per second (m/s)
- **hertz** (hertz) — the unit of frequency; one hertz means one wave per second

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you flick a rope up and down faster, do you make more waves per second or fewer?
2. The distance from one crest of a wave to the next crest is called the wavelength. Is a shorter wavelength produced by a faster or a slower vibration?
3. If a wave has a frequency of 2 waves per second and a wavelength of 3 m, what is its speed? (Hint: speed = frequency × wavelength)

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. More waves per second — flicking faster increases the frequency.
2. A shorter wavelength is produced by a faster vibration (higher frequency), because the waves are packed closer together.
3. Speed = frequency × wavelength = 2 × 3 = 6 m/s. (Accept any reasonable attempt; the lesson will confirm the method.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before handling the rope.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — stretch the rope taut and flick one end slowly, then fast. Ask: *What changed when I flicked faster?* Learners will see more waves along the rope and the waves shorter. Say: *Frequency is how many waves per second; wavelength is crest-to-crest. Today we measure both and find the wave speed with the formula v = fλ.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in pairs, learners stretch a sisal rope between them. First, they flick at a slow, steady rate for several seconds while a third learner counts the crests passing a chalk mark on the rope in a timed 5 seconds; they compute frequency as crests per second (Hz). Second, while one keeps the same steady flicking, another learner marks two adjacent crests with chalk and measures the distance between them with the tape — that is the wavelength λ in metres. Third, they repeat with a faster flick, recording a new frequency and a new wavelength. The teacher walks between pairs checking the timing and the chalk marks. Each pair computes v = fλ for both trials and checks that v is roughly the same (the wave speed depends on the rope tension, not the flicking rate).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board: write **v = fλ**, where v is wave speed (m/s), f is frequency (Hz), and λ is wavelength (m). Show that if the rope tension stays the same, v stays the same, so increasing f must decrease λ — faster flicking makes shorter waves. Then link to sound: a drum beat is a sound wave in air travelling at about 340 m/s. A higher-pitched note has a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength; a lower-pitched note has a lower frequency and a longer wavelength. Confront the misconception that *a faster wave means a higher frequency* — wave speed depends on the medium (tension, density), while frequency depends on the source (how fast you flick or vibrate). You can flick the same rope at different rates and change f without changing v.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *v = fλ; faster flicking means higher frequency and shorter wavelength for the same wave speed.* Homework: a drum beats at 85 Hz; the speed of sound is 340 m/s. Find the wavelength of the drum's sound.

## Hands-On Build: Measure the Rope Wave
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Each pair needs a rope, chalk, a tape, and a stopwatch.*

### Parts
- A long sisal rope, about 3 m, per pair
- Coloured chalk or a marker
- A tape measure or a metre rule
- A stopwatch or a phone clock
- A drum or a sufuria and a stick (for the sound-link demo)

### Workflow
1. Stretch the rope taut between two learners. **Checkpoint:** the rope is straight and does not sag.
2. One learner flicks the rope slowly and steadily while another counts the crests passing a fixed point in 5 seconds. **Checkpoint:** the count is recorded; frequency = count ÷ 5, in Hz.
3. While the flicking continues at the same rate, a third learner marks two adjacent crests with chalk and measures the distance between them with the tape. **Checkpoint:** the wavelength λ is recorded in metres.
4. Compute v = f × λ and record it. **Checkpoint:** a speed value in m/s is written down.
5. Repeat with a faster flick. Record the new f and the new λ, and compute v again. **Checkpoint:** the two v values are roughly equal — the wave speed did not change, only the frequency and wavelength.

### Common Mistakes
- **Counting the wrong thing** — learners may count half-cycles (crest to trough) instead of full cycles (crest to crest). Count only crests passing the mark, or count "up" motions.
- **Measuring the wrong distance** — wavelength is crest-to-crest, not crest-to-trough. Mark two adjacent crests and measure between them.
- **Flicking unevenly** — a jerky flick makes the wave messy and the count wrong. Practise a steady rhythm before timing.
- **Confusing frequency with speed** — flicking faster raises f, but v stays the same if the rope tension is unchanged. Compare the two v values to show this.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. If you flick a rope faster, what happens to the frequency and the wavelength? (mirrors pre-test)
2. State the wave equation v = fλ and define each symbol with its unit. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A rope wave has a frequency of 4 Hz and a wavelength of 1.5 m. What is its speed?
4. You flick the same rope faster but keep the tension the same. Does the wave speed increase, decrease, or stay the same? Explain in one sentence.
5. Challenge: a sound wave in air has a frequency of 170 Hz and a wavelength of 2 m. Compute the wave speed. Then predict the wavelength of a drum's sound at 85 Hz in the same air, assuming the speed of sound stays the same.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The frequency increases (more waves per second); the wavelength decreases (the waves are packed closer together).
2. v = fλ, where v is wave speed in metres per second (m/s), f is frequency in hertz (Hz), and λ is wavelength in metres (m).
3. v = f × λ = 4 × 1.5 = 6 m/s.
4. The wave speed stays the same — wave speed depends on the rope's tension and density (the medium), not on how fast you flick. Flicking faster changes f and λ, but v is unchanged.
5. Wave speed = f × λ = 170 × 2 = 340 m/s. For the drum at 85 Hz, with v still 340 m/s: λ = v ÷ f = 340 ÷ 85 = 4 m. The lower-frequency drum sound has a longer wavelength.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A sound wave in air has a frequency of 170 Hz and a wavelength of 2 m. Compute the wave speed. Then predict the wavelength of a drum's sound at 85 Hz in the same air, assuming the speed of sound stays the same.
   **Worked Answer:** Wave speed v = f × λ = 170 Hz × 2 m = 340 m/s. For the drum at 85 Hz, with v still 340 m/s: λ = v ÷ f = 340 ÷ 85 = 4 m. The drum's lower frequency gives a longer wavelength — half the frequency means twice the wavelength, since v is constant.
2. **Q:** A rope wave is flicked at 3 Hz with a wavelength of 2 m. The learner then doubles the tension in the rope, which increases the wave speed by a factor of √2 (about 1.41). If the learner keeps flicking at 3 Hz, what is the new wavelength?
   **Worked Answer:** Original speed v₁ = f × λ = 3 × 2 = 6 m/s. New speed v₂ = v₁ × √2 = 6 × 1.41 ≈ 8.49 m/s. With f still 3 Hz, the new wavelength λ₂ = v₂ ÷ f = 8.49 ÷ 3 ≈ 2.83 m. Increasing the tension raised the wave speed, so for the same frequency the wavelength grew longer.
3. **Q:** A learner claims that because the drum's wavelength is longer than the whistle's, the drum's sound travels faster. Using v = fλ and the fact that the speed of sound in air is about 340 m/s for both, explain why this claim is wrong.
   **Worked Answer:** The speed of sound in air depends on the air (its temperature and density), not on the source. For the drum: v = f × λ = 85 × 4 = 340 m/s. For the whistle (say f = 680 Hz, λ = 0.5 m): v = 680 × 0.5 = 340 m/s. Both give the same speed, 340 m/s, because the medium (air) is the same. The drum has a longer wavelength only because its frequency is lower — v is constant for a given medium, so f and λ change together to keep v fixed. The claim confuses wavelength with wave speed.

## Safety Notes
- A stretched sisal rope can snap back and sting if released under tension; release the rope gently and do not let go while flicking.
- Flicking the rope hard can whip the free end into a face; keep faces clear of the rope's path and flick with control.
- Chalk dust can irritate eyes; do not blow chalk off the rope, wipe it with a cloth.
- Do not stand on chairs to stretch the rope higher; keep both learners on the ground to avoid falls.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that waves carry energy (Lesson 331) and that sound is a vibration (Lesson 329). This lesson puts numbers to those waves.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on counting crests, measuring wavelength with the tape, and using v = fλ with the teacher's help. Ages 14–17 work the tension-changes-speed case and the constant-speed sound argument in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: a sisal rope of 3 m is available at any dukani. Coloured chalk is standard classroom stock. A tape measure or a metre rule is sufficient; a phone clock replaces a stopwatch. A drum or an upside-down sufuria with a stick links the rope wave to a sound wave in the same lesson.
- A common surprise: learners may expect the faster flick to produce a faster wave. The two v values being roughly equal is the teachable moment — wave speed depends on the rope (tension and mass), not on the flicking rate. If the two v values differ a lot, check that the rope tension did not change between trials or that the count was off.
