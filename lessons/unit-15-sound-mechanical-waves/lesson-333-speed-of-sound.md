# Lesson 333: Measuring the Speed of Sound with an Echo

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Measure the speed of sound by timing an echo over a known distance.
2. Average ten claps to reduce reaction-time error in a time measurement.
3. Compare a measured sound speed with the accepted value (~340 m/s) and express the difference as a percentage.
4. Identify the dominant source of error in the echo method and propose one improvement.

## Materials
- A tall, flat, hard wall or a cliff face at least 30 m away (school compound wall, gable end of a building, or a roadside cutting)
- A measuring tape (5 m or 10 m) or a known pace length (mark each 5 m with chalk or a stick)
- A stopwatch (phone stopwatch is fine) or a watch with a second hand
- A notebook and pencil for each pair
- Chalk or small stones to mark the standing spot and the distance count
- Two learners per pair: one clapper/timer, one recorder

## Vocabulary
- **echo** (sauti inayorudi) — sound that bounces off a hard surface and returns to the listener after a delay
- **speed of sound** (mbio za sauti) — the distance a sound wave travels in one second, about 340 m/s in air at room temperature
- **round trip** (safari ya kwenda na kurudi) — the sound travels to the wall and back, so the path length is twice the wall distance
- **averaging** (wastani) — adding several measurements and dividing by their count to reduce random error

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You stand 50 m from a wall and clap once. After what time do you expect the echo to return if sound travels at 340 m/s?
2. Why must you double the wall distance when calculating the speed of sound from an echo?
3. Why is timing one echo with a single clap likely to give an unreliable answer?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The sound travels to the wall and back, a round trip of 2 × 50 = 100 m. Time = distance ÷ speed = 100 ÷ 340 ≈ 0.29 s.
2. Because the sound goes to the wall and returns — the path is twice the wall distance. Using only the one-way distance would double the calculated speed.
3. Human reaction time (about 0.2 s) is a large fraction of the ~0.3 s echo delay, so a single measurement carries a big random error.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Have pairs ready with notebooks and tape.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — take the class outside to a flat wall at a measured distance (at least 30 m). Ask one learner to clap once, loudly. The whole class listens for the echo. Ask: *how long did that take?* (No one can say precisely.) Board the challenge: *we will measure the speed of sound with nothing but a clap, a wall, and a stopwatch.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs measure the wall distance with the tape (or by pacing, recording each learner's stride length first). Each pair does the echo method (see Hands-On Build): stand at the measured spot, clap rhythmically so the clap lands exactly on the returning echo, time ten claps, divide by ten, and compute the speed. Three trials per pair; swap roles between trials.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw the path: clap → wall → ear, and label 2d as the round-trip distance. Write v = 2d ÷ t. Confront the misconception that *the echo time is just the one-way travel time* — it is the round trip, so the path is 2d, not d. Then confront the misconception that *one good clap is enough* — reaction time (~0.2 s) is a big fraction of a ~0.3 s echo, so a single trial can be off by more than 50%. Timing ten clap-echo cycles and dividing by ten spreads that error over ten cycles, just as ten pendulum swings did in Unit 11.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *sound is fast but not instant — a wall and a clap can measure its speed.* Homework: find the tallest flat wall near your home, pace its distance, and write down how you would measure the speed of sound there.

## Hands-On Build: The Echo Method
*Do this outdoors. Pick the day before if the wall is far — scout the wall and check it is flat and hard.*

### Parts
- A flat, hard wall or cliff at least 30 m away
- Measuring tape (5–10 m) or a marked pace length
- Stopwatch (phone) — one per pair
- Chalk or stones to mark the standing spot
- Notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Measure the distance from the standing spot to the wall, using the tape end-to-end or by counting paces (multiply pace count by your measured stride length). Record d. **Checkpoint:** every pair has the same wall distance written down to the nearest metre.
2. Stand at the marked spot, facing the wall. Clap once and listen for the echo. Repeat until you can hear the echo clearly.
3. Now clap rhythmically: clap, wait for the echo, clap on the echo, wait for the next echo, and so on. Practise until the clap and echo are perfectly in step — the clap *becomes* the echo. **Checkpoint:** a partner standing beside you agrees the clap and echo are synchronised.
4. Once in rhythm, the clapper keeps clapping while the partner starts the stopwatch on a clap and stops it on the tenth clap after that. Record the total time for ten clap-echo cycles. **Checkpoint:** ten claps, not nine or eleven — count out loud.
5. The time for one echo = total time ÷ 10. The round-trip distance = 2d. Speed of sound = 2d ÷ (time per echo).
6. Repeat for three trials. Average the three speeds. Record each trial and the average in the notebook.
7. Compare the average with 340 m/s. Work out the percentage difference: (measured − 340) ÷ 340 × 100. **Checkpoint:** every pair has a speed, an average, and a percentage difference written down.

### Common Mistakes
- **Using the one-way distance instead of the round trip** — the sound goes to the wall and back, so the path is 2d. Always multiply the wall distance by two.
- **Counting the starting clap as "one"** — start the watch on the first clap, then count the next ten claps (the starting clap is "zero"). Otherwise you time only nine cycles.
- **Clapping too slowly so the rhythm is lost** — if the gap between clap and echo is long (wall far away), the rhythm is hard to hold. Choose a wall 30–60 m away so the echo is quick enough to keep a steady beat.
- **Wall too close or not flat** — below 20 m the echo is too fast to catch; a bushy or angled wall scatters the sound instead of reflecting it. Pick a flat, hard, vertical surface.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. You stand 50 m from a wall and clap once. After what time do you expect the echo to return if sound travels at 340 m/s? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Why must you double the wall distance when calculating the speed of sound from an echo? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A pair stands 40 m from a wall. They time ten clap-echo cycles in 2.4 s. Calculate the speed of sound they measure.
4. Their measured speed is 310 m/s. What is the percentage difference from the accepted value of 340 m/s?
5. Challenge: Give two reasons why timing a single clap-echo is less accurate than the ten-clap method, and state one further improvement beyond averaging.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Round trip = 2 × 50 = 100 m. Time = 100 ÷ 340 ≈ 0.29 s.
2. The sound travels to the wall and back, so the total path is twice the one-way distance. Using d instead of 2d would give double the true speed.
3. Time per echo = 2.4 ÷ 10 = 0.24 s. Round trip = 2 × 40 = 80 m. Speed = 80 ÷ 0.24 ≈ 333 m/s.
4. (310 − 340) ÷ 340 × 100 = −30 ÷ 340 × 100 ≈ −8.8%. The measured value is about 8.8% below the accepted value.
5. (a) Reaction time (~0.2 s) is a large fraction of a single ~0.3 s echo, so one trial carries a large random error. (b) A single clap gives no way to check repeatability — you cannot tell a fluke from a real measurement. Improvement beyond averaging: use a longer baseline (a wall farther away so the echo time is longer and reaction time is a smaller fraction), or use a phone audio recording and count the clap-to-echo gap on the waveform, removing reaction time entirely.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A wall is 85 m away. A learner times the echo of a single clap and gets 0.5 s. (a) Calculate the speed of sound from this measurement. (b) If the learner's reaction time is 0.2 s, estimate the percentage error this introduces. (c) Suggest an improvement that would cut the reaction-time error.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Round trip = 2 × 85 = 170 m. Speed = 170 ÷ 0.5 = 340 m/s. (b) The 0.5 s includes the reaction-time error. If the true echo time is t, the measured time is t + 0.2 (or t − 0.2, depending on whether the learner stops late or early). The reaction-time fraction = 0.2 ÷ 0.5 × 100 = 40%. So the single-clap measurement carries up to a 40% reaction-time error. (c) Use the ten-clap rhythm method: the 0.2 s reaction time is spread over ten cycles, shrinking the per-cycle error to 0.2 ÷ 10 = 0.02 s, or 0.02 ÷ 0.5 × 100 = 4%. Alternatively, record the clap and echo on a phone and read the gap from the audio waveform, removing reaction time entirely.
2. **Q:** Sound travels faster in warmer air because air molecules move quicker and pass the disturbance along faster. The speed of sound in air is approximately v = 331 + 0.6T m/s, where T is the temperature in °C. (a) What is the speed of sound at 20 °C? (b) A learner measures 340 m/s on a hot day. Estimate the air temperature.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) v = 331 + 0.6 × 20 = 331 + 12 = 343 m/s. (b) Set 340 = 331 + 0.6T → 0.6T = 9 → T = 15 °C. The air is about 15 °C. (If the learner measured 348 m/s, T ≈ 28 °C — a warm afternoon.)
3. **Q:** A learner stands 50 m from a wall and uses the ten-clap method. The stopwatch reads 2.9 s for ten cycles. (a) Calculate the speed of sound. (b) The wall distance was paced, not taped, and the learner's stride is 0.75 m but was assumed to be 0.80 m. Calculate the true speed and the percentage error introduced by the pacing mistake.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Time per echo = 2.9 ÷ 10 = 0.29 s. Round trip = 2 × 50 = 100 m (using the assumed distance). Speed = 100 ÷ 0.29 ≈ 345 m/s. (b) True distance: if the stride is actually 0.75 m and the learner counted, say, 67 paces, the true wall distance = 67 × 0.75 = 50.25 m — close. But if the learner assumed 0.80 m and recorded 50 m, the true distance = 50 × (0.75 ÷ 0.80) = 46.875 m. True round trip = 93.75 m. True speed = 93.75 ÷ 0.29 ≈ 323 m/s. Percentage error from the pacing mistake = (345 − 323) ÷ 323 × 100 ≈ 6.8%. The pacing error alone introduces nearly 7% error — a reminder that the distance must be measured accurately, not just the time.

## Safety Notes
- Work away from traffic. If the wall is beside a road, stand on the school side and face the wall; do not step into the road to reach a farther wall.
- Do not clap directly beside another learner's ear — a loud clap close to the ear can cause discomfort. Keep at least arm's length from the nearest person when clapping.
- If using a cliff or cutting, keep back from the edge; loose stones can fall. Choose a smooth, solid face.
- Sun and heat: if the wall is far, work in short bursts and carry water on hot days.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 332 introduced v = fλ and wave speed. This lesson applies the speed concept to sound itself and practises the averaging-for-precision technique from Lesson 245 (pendulum timing). If learners have not done Lesson 245, spend an extra minute on the "why ten" reasoning.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the clap rhythm, the round-trip idea (2d), and averaging three trials. Ages 14–17 should compute percentage error, distinguish random from systematic error (pacing error is systematic; reaction time is random), and tackle the temperature correction in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: a school compound wall, the gable end of a dormitory or hall, or a roadside cutting all work. Avoid rough, bushy, or angled surfaces — they scatter the sound. If no wall is long enough, two learners can stand 50–80 m apart facing each other and clap in a "relay" rhythm, though a single wall is simpler.
- The biggest practical pitfall is the wall distance: pacing without a measured stride is the main source of error. Have at least one group tape the distance and share it with the class, or calibrate every learner's stride against a 5 m tape before going out.
- Save the best class measurement on the board. In Lesson 343 (Echoes and Sonar) learners will use this same technique to compute distances from echo times — the same physics in reverse.
