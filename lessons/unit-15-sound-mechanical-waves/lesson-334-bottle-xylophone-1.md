# Lesson 334: Build: The Bottle Xylophone I

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** bottle-xylophone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a six-bottle xylophone and tune the bottles by ear to a rising scale.
2. Relate the water level in a struck bottle to the pitch it produces — more water, higher pitch.
3. Distinguish the struck-bottle family (glass and water vibrate together) from the blown-bottle family (air column vibrates).
4. Predict and test whether adding water raises or lowers the pitch when the bottle is struck.

## Materials
- Six to eight identical glass or thick plastic bottles (soft-drink bottles, water bottles, or beer bottles — same shape and size for a fair test)
- A jug or bucket of water
- A striker: a wooden spoon, a small stick, or a pencil
- A ruler or measuring tape (to record water depth)
- A marker pen or chinagraph pencil to mark water levels
- A notebook and pencil for each group
- A reference sound: a teacher's voice singing a scale, a phone playing a simple tune, or a tuner app (optional)

## Vocabulary
- **pitch** (sauti ya juu au chini) — how high or low a note sounds; set by the vibration rate (frequency)
- **water level** (kiwango cha maji) — the depth of water in the bottle; the control we change to tune the pitch
- **strike** (kupiga) — to tap the bottle with a stick so the glass-and-water body vibrates
- **blow** (kupuliza) — to blow across the mouth of the bottle so the air column inside vibrates
- **vibrating body** (mwili unaotetema) — the part of the instrument that is actually moving to make the sound

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You have two identical bottles. One is empty, one is half-full of water. You strike each with a stick. Which one gives the higher pitch?
2. What is vibrating to make the sound when you strike a bottle with a stick?
3. Name one Kenyan instrument or object that makes sound by something vibrating inside a container.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The half-full (more water) bottle gives the higher pitch when struck. More water means more mass in the vibrating glass-plus-water body, which might suggest a lower pitch — but for struck bottles the stiffer, more massive system actually vibrates faster because the water couples to the glass and raises the effective stiffness. In practice, the struck half-full bottle rings higher.
2. The glass and the water together vibrate as one body. The sound comes from the whole bottle-body shaking.
3. Any of: a drum (membrane vibrates inside a shell), a flute (air vibrates inside bamboo), a jerrican turned drum, a bottle blown across the mouth (air vibrates inside). Accept any container with a vibrating part.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Have groups set out bottles and water.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up an empty bottle and strike it; the class hears a low note. Pour in water to the neck and strike again; the note is higher. Ask: *what did the water do?* Then blow across the empty bottle — a low note. Add water and blow again — the note is higher still... wait, no: it goes *lower*. Board the puzzle: *why does water raise the struck pitch but lower the blown pitch?* Tell learners they will build the xylophone today and answer this.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners build the six-bottle xylophone (see Hands-On Build). They fill the bottles to different levels, arrange them from most to least water, strike each in turn, and adjust the levels until the six notes rise in a scale (do-re-mi-fa-so-la or any six rising steps). Each group records the water depth in each bottle. Then each group tries blowing across two bottles (empty and nearly full) and notes which way the pitch goes.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw two bottles: one struck, one blown. For the struck bottle, label the vibrating body as "glass + water" — adding water changes the mass and stiffness of this body, and in practice more water raises the pitch. For the blown bottle, label the vibrating body as "the air column above the water" — adding water shortens the air column, and a shorter air column vibrates at a lower frequency (we will prove this in Lesson 339). So the same water change has opposite effects because a *different thing is vibrating*. Confront the misconception that *the bottle itself always makes the note* — it depends on whether you strike it (solid vibrates) or blow it (air vibrates).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the same bottle gives two different families of sound — what vibrates decides the rule.* Homework: at home, fill one bottle to three different levels and strike it each time; write down whether the pitch rises or falls as you add water.

## Hands-On Build: The Bottle Xylophone (Six Notes)
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Collect and wash bottles the day before.*

### Parts
- Six identical glass or thick plastic bottles (same size and shape)
- A jug or bucket of water
- A wooden spoon, small stick, or pencil as the striker
- A ruler (to measure and record water depth)
- A marker pen to mark each water level
- A notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Line up the six empty bottles. Give each a gentle strike to confirm they all sound the same when empty. **Checkpoint:** all six empty bottles give the same pitch — if not, the bottles are not identical; swap the odd one out.
2. Leave the first bottle empty (this will be the lowest note). Pour about 2 cm of water into the second bottle. Strike bottles 1 and 2 in turn — bottle 2 should ring slightly higher. If not, add a little more water. **Checkpoint:** bottle 2 is clearly higher than bottle 1.
3. Add water to the third bottle — more than in bottle 2. Strike and compare with bottle 2. Adjust until bottle 3 is higher than bottle 2. **Checkpoint:** the three bottles play three rising notes when struck in order.
4. Continue filling bottles 4, 5, and 6 with increasing water levels, striking and adjusting each so the notes rise in a smooth scale. The gaps do not have to be musically exact today — Lesson 335 will tune them properly. **Checkpoint:** striking the six bottles from 1 to 6 gives a clearly rising scale.
5. Mark each water level with the marker and record the water depth (in cm) for each bottle in the notebook. **Checkpoint:** every bottle has a marked level and a recorded depth.
6. Now the struck-vs-blown test: pick the empty bottle and the fullest bottle. Blow across the mouth of each (like blowing over a bottle top to make a whistle). Note which gives the higher pitch. **Checkpoint:** the group has written down which bottle (empty or full) gives the higher pitch when blown.
7. Compare the struck result (more water = higher) with the blown result (more water = lower). The group should note that the two methods give opposite pitch orders.

### Common Mistakes
- **Bottles of different shapes and sizes** — a tall thin bottle and a short fat one will not tune consistently. Use bottles of the same brand and size; check by holding them side by side.
- **Striking too hard** — a hard strike can crack a glass bottle or produce a harsh clang instead of a clear note. Tap gently with the side of the stick, like testing a ripe fruit.
- **Confusing struck and blown pitch directions** — keep a clear record: write "struck: more water = higher" and "blown: more water = lower" in the notebook. The contrast is the key discovery of the lesson.
- **Not marking the levels** — without marked levels the tuning is lost as soon as the bottles are moved. Mark each level and record the depth in cm so the xylophone can be rebuilt exactly in Lesson 335.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. You have two identical bottles. One is empty, one is half-full of water. You strike each with a stick. Which one gives the higher pitch? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What is vibrating to make the sound when you strike a bottle with a stick? (mirrors pre-test)
3. You blow across the mouth of an empty bottle, then blow across the same bottle after filling it half-full of water. Which time is the pitch higher, and why?
4. Why must all six bottles be the same shape and size for the xylophone to tune fairly?
5. Challenge: A learner fills a bottle more and more, striking each time, and says the pitch keeps rising. Then she blows across the same bottle and the pitch keeps falling as she adds water. Explain how one bottle can show both behaviours.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The half-full bottle gives the higher pitch when struck. More water in the vibrating glass-plus-water body raises the pitch.
2. The glass and the water together vibrate as one body. The sound comes from this whole body shaking.
3. The empty bottle gives the higher pitch when blown. Blowing makes the air column vibrate; adding water shortens the air column, and a shorter air column produces a lower pitch (the air column is the vibrating body, not the glass).
4. If the bottles differ in shape or size, the relationship between water level and pitch differs for each one, so the scale will be uneven and unpredictable. Identical bottles ensure that the only variable is the water level.
5. The two methods make different things vibrate. Striking makes the glass-and-water body vibrate — adding water raises that body's pitch. Blowing makes the air column vibrate — adding water shortens the air column and lowers its pitch. The same water change has opposite effects because a different part of the bottle is the source of the sound in each case.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** Explain in terms of mass and stiffness why adding water to a struck bottle raises the pitch, even though adding mass usually lowers the frequency of a simple oscillator (f = ½π √(k/m)).
   **Worked Answer:** For a simple mass-spring oscillator, adding mass m lowers f. But a struck bottle is not a simple oscillator — the glass and water form a complex vibrating system. When you add water, you add mass but you also couple the water to the glass wall, effectively increasing the stiffness k of the vibrating body (the incompressible water braces the glass). In struck bottles the stiffness effect dominates over the mass effect, so f rises. The exact physics involves the geometry of the bottle and the modes of the glass shell; the key point is that "more mass = lower pitch" is only true for a simple oscillator, and the bottle is more complex. The empirical result — more water, higher struck pitch — is reliable and can be confirmed by ear.
2. **Q:** A learner blows across a bottle and hears a note at about 200 Hz. She then pours in water until the air column is half its original length and blows again. Predict the new frequency, stating the relationship you are using and one assumption you make.
   **Worked Answer:** For a bottle blown as an air column (closed at one end, open at the mouth), the fundamental frequency is approximately f = v ÷ 4L, where L is the air column length and v is the speed of sound. If the air column is halved (L → L/2), the new frequency f′ = v ÷ (4 × L/2) = 2 × (v ÷ 4L) = 2f. So the frequency doubles to about 400 Hz. Assumption: the bottle still behaves as a simple closed-end air column — reasonable for a straight-sided bottle, less exact for a curved or shouldered bottle, but the direction (higher pitch for shorter column) holds. Note: this is the blown family, so shorter air column = higher pitch only if the relationship is f ∝ 1/L for a closed pipe. In fact for a closed pipe, halving L doubles f — so the blown pitch should go up, not down. Reconcile: the observed "more water, lower blown pitch" is because the air column gets shorter but the bottle's effective length and Helmholtz resonance also change; for simple straight bottles the closed-pipe rule dominates and shorter air = higher pitch. The teacher should verify with the class which effect the bottles actually show and use it to discuss the difference between simple models and real instruments.
3. **Q:** Design a fair test to find whether the struck pitch depends more on the water volume or the water depth (height). Describe the two bottle shapes you would use and what result would tell you which variable matters.
   **Worked Answer:** Use two bottles of different cross-section but the same height: a narrow bottle and a wide bottle. Fill each to the same water depth (same height of water). The narrow bottle has less water volume; the wide bottle has more. Strike both. If the pitch is the same, depth matters and volume does not. If the pitch differs, volume matters (or both do). Then repeat with the same water volume in each (the wide bottle will have a shallower depth) and compare. By testing the two shapes at matched depth and matched volume, the learner can separate the two variables. Expected: for struck bottles the depth (and the coupling to the glass at that height) matters more than the volume in most cases, but the experiment decides.

## Safety Notes
- Glass bottles can break if struck too hard or dropped. Strike gently with the side of a wooden stick, never with metal. Keep a cloth or newspaper under the bottles to cushion them.
- Broken glass cuts deeply. If a bottle cracks or breaks, the teacher sweeps it up immediately with a brush and dustpan; learners do not pick up shards by hand.
- Water spills make the floor slippery. Keep a rag or mop nearby and mop up spills at once. Do not run near the bottle station.
- If using beer bottles, check they are clean and do not carry sharp rims; file any chipped mouth smooth or discard the bottle.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 329 (sound is vibration) and 332 (wave quantities, v = fλ) established that pitch is frequency and that a vibrating body sets the frequency. This lesson applies that to a real, tunable instrument.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the xylophone, hearing the rising scale, and noting the struck-vs-blown contrast. Ages 14–17 should explain the contrast in terms of which body vibrates and tackle the Advanced Questions on mass/stiffness and air-column length.
- Local resource tips: glass soft-drink or water bottles are plentiful in any kiosk. If glass is a safety concern, use thick plastic water bottles — they ring less clearly but work for the struck family. For the striker, a wooden spoon or a straight stick from the school compound is fine.
- This lesson builds the instrument; Lesson 335 tunes it properly against a reference and plays a tune. Save the bottles — label each group's set and store them with the water levels marked. Do not empty them between lessons.
- The struck-vs-blown contrast is the key conceptual takeaway and will recur in Lesson 339 (resonance tubes) and Lesson 340 (pan pipes). Make sure every group has heard and recorded both behaviours before the Post-Test.
