# Lesson 335: Build: The Bottle Xylophone II — Tune and Play

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** bottle-xylophone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Tune each bottle of the xylophone to match a reference pitch (phone tuner app, pitch pipe, or teacher's voice).
2. Play a short known tune on the tuned xylophone.
3. Record the water level for each tuned note so the xylophone can be rebuilt exactly.
4. Describe whether equal water-level steps produce equal musical note steps, and explain the result in terms of how pitch depends on the air column or the glass-water body.

## Materials
- The bottle xylophone built in Lesson 334 (six bottles with marked water levels)
- A jug or bucket of water for fine adjustments
- A striker: wooden spoon, small stick, or pencil
- A reference pitch source: a phone tuner or pitch-pipe app (several free ones exist), a keyboard app, or the teacher singing a scale
- A ruler or measuring tape (to record final water depths)
- A notebook and pencil for each group
- A simple tune written out as note names (e.g., do-re-mi-fa-so-la or the first line of a familiar Kenyan song like "Jambo Bwana" or a hymn)

## Vocabulary
- **tune** (kupanga sauti) — to adjust each bottle so its pitch matches a chosen reference note
- **reference pitch** (sauti ya kulinganisha) — a known note from a tuner app, pitch pipe, or voice, used to judge whether a bottle is too high or too low
- **semitone** (nusu hatua ya muziki) — the smallest step in the usual musical scale; twelve semitones make an octave
- **octave** (sauti ya pili) — the note that sounds "the same but higher"; it is double the frequency of the lower note

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Last lesson, how did the pitch of a struck bottle change as you added water — did it rise or fall?
2. Your bottle rings a little higher than the reference note. Should you add water or pour some out to lower the pitch?
3. If the relationship between water level and pitch were a straight line (linear), would equal water-level steps give equal musical steps? Briefly explain.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The pitch rose as water was added. More water in the struck bottle gives a higher pitch (the glass-and-water body vibrates faster).
2. To lower a struck bottle's pitch, pour some water out. Less water lowers the struck pitch.
3. Yes — if pitch were a linear function of water level, then equal steps in water level would give equal steps in pitch. (In practice, the relationship is not linear, so equal water steps do not give equal musical steps — learners will discover this in the lesson.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Have groups set out their xylophones and reference sources.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — play a short, familiar tune on the phone or sing it (e.g., the first line of "Jambo Bwana" or the school anthem). Then strike the six bottles from Lesson 334 in order and ask: *is this in tune?* The class will hear that the rough scale is close but not musically exact. Tell learners that today they tune each bottle to a real reference and then play a tune together.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners tune each bottle against the reference (see Hands-On Build). One learner strikes a bottle, another checks the tuner app (or compares with the teacher's sung note), and they add or pour water until the pitch matches. They record the final water depth for each tuned note. Then the group plays a short tune (do-re-mi-fa-so-la, or the first line of a chosen song) on the tuned xylophone. Each group also tests the "equal steps" question: they mark the water level for two adjacent notes and measure the difference, then compare it with the difference between two other adjacent notes.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw a graph of pitch (vertical) against water level (horizontal). If the relationship were linear, the points would lie on a straight line and equal water steps would give equal note steps. In practice the points curve — the pitch does not rise evenly with water level. The reason: for struck bottles the vibrating body (glass plus water) is complex, and the frequency depends on the stiffness and mass in a non-linear way; for blown bottles the air-column frequency follows f ∝ 1/L, which is also non-linear. So equal water steps do not give equal musical steps. Confront the misconception that *a scale means equal gaps in the thing you adjust* — musical scales are built from equal ratios of frequency, not equal linear steps in length or depth.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *tuning is matching a reference; equal water steps do not make equal musical steps because pitch depends on length non-linearly.* Homework: record the final water level for each bottle and label the bottles (1 to 6) so the xylophone can be reassembled for the class orchestra in Lesson 347.

## Hands-On Build: Tune and Play
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Use the xylophone from Lesson 334.*

### Parts
- The six-bottle xylophone from Lesson 334 (with marked levels)
- A jug or bucket of water
- A striker (wooden spoon or stick)
- A phone tuner app, keyboard app, or pitch pipe (or the teacher singing a reference scale)
- A ruler to measure and record final water depths
- A notebook and pencil
- A chosen short tune written as note names

### Workflow
1. Set the six bottles in a row, most water (highest pitch) to least water (lowest pitch), left to right or right to left as the group prefers. Strike each in turn to confirm the scale still rises from Lesson 334. **Checkpoint:** the six notes rise in order before fine-tuning begins.
2. Choose the reference scale. The simplest is do-re-mi-fa-so-la (six notes of the major scale). If using a tuner app, the app shows the nearest note name (e.g., C, D, E) and whether the bottle is sharp (too high) or flat (too low). **Checkpoint:** every group has a reference source and knows which six notes it is aiming for.
3. Start with the lowest note (least water). Strike the bottle and read the tuner. If the note is too high (sharp), pour a little water out. If too low (flat), add a little water. Re-strike and re-check. **Checkpoint:** the lowest bottle matches the first reference note within a small margin (the tuner shows the target note, not a neighbouring one).
4. Move to the next bottle. Tune it to the second reference note. Repeat for all six bottles, tuning each to its target note. Pour water in or out in small amounts — a teaspoon of water changes the pitch noticeably. **Checkpoint:** all six bottles match their target notes; striking them in order plays the reference scale.
5. Record the final water depth (in cm from the base) for each tuned bottle in the notebook. Mark each level on the bottle with the marker. Label the bottles 1 (lowest) to 6 (highest). **Checkpoint:** every bottle has a recorded depth and a label; the xylophone can be rebuilt exactly from the notebook.
6. Play the chosen tune. Start with do-re-mi-fa-so-la up and down, then try the first line of a familiar song. The group practises until the tune is recognisable. **Checkpoint:** the group can play the scale and the first line of the tune cleanly.
7. Test the "equal steps" question: measure the water depth of bottles 1 and 2; subtract to get the step. Measure the depth of bottles 5 and 6; subtract. Compare the two steps. **Checkpoint:** the group has written down both step sizes and noted whether they are equal or not.

### Common Mistakes
- **Adding or pouring too much water at once** — a big change overshoots the note and wastes time. Adjust in small sips; a teaspoon of water can shift the pitch by a semitone.
- **Forgetting which bottle is which note** — once tuned, label each bottle with its number and record the depth. Without labels the order is lost as soon as the bottles are moved.
- **Not re-striking after each adjustment** — the tuner reads the *last* strike, so always strike again after changing the water before reading the app.
- **Using a reference that drifts** — if the teacher is singing, the voice can tire and shift. A phone tuner or pitch pipe gives a stable reference. If using a phone, keep it at the same distance from the bottle for every check.
- **Assuming equal water steps give equal notes** — the "equal steps" test usually shows they do not. Record the actual step sizes; the non-equality is the finding, not a mistake.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. Last lesson, how did the pitch of a struck bottle change as you added water? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Your bottle rings a little higher than the reference note. Should you add water or pour some out to lower the pitch? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why is a reference pitch (tuner app or pitch pipe) better than tuning "by ear" alone when you want to play with other instruments?
4. A group measures the water-level gap between bottles 1 and 2 as 3 cm, and between bottles 5 and 6 as 7 cm. What does this tell you about the relationship between water level and pitch?
5. Challenge: If you wanted to add a seventh bottle one octave above the lowest, would you need to double the water level of the lowest bottle? Explain why or why not, using the idea that an octave is a doubling of frequency.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The pitch rose as water was added to the struck bottle.
2. Pour some water out. Less water lowers the struck pitch.
3. A reference pitch is stable and shared, so every group tunes to the same standard. Tuning by ear alone depends on each person's judgement, which can differ; if everyone tunes to a tuner, the xylophones will agree with each other and with other instruments in the orchestra.
4. The relationship between water level and pitch is not linear. Equal musical steps (the notes 1→2 and 5→6) do not correspond to equal water-level steps (3 cm vs 7 cm). The pitch changes more slowly at low water levels and more quickly (or vice versa) at high levels — the graph of pitch against water level curves.
5. No. An octave is a doubling of frequency, not a doubling of water level. Because the relationship between water level and pitch is non-linear, doubling the frequency does not require doubling the water level. The amount of water needed for an octave depends on the shape of the bottle and the pitch-versus-level curve, which the group can find only by tuning with a reference, not by a simple proportion.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A group tunes six bottles to the notes C, D, E, F, G, A (a major scale fragment). The frequencies are: C = 262 Hz, D = 294 Hz, E = 330 Hz, F = 349 Hz, G = 392 Hz, A = 440 Hz. (a) Compute the frequency ratio between each pair of adjacent notes. (b) Are the ratios equal? (c) What does this tell you about how musical pitch is built?
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Ratios: D/C = 294/262 = 1.122; E/D = 330/294 = 1.122; F/E = 349/330 = 1.058; G/F = 392/349 = 1.123; A/G = 440/392 = 1.122. (b) The ratios are not all equal — F/E (1.058) is smaller than the others (~1.122). The steps D–E, G–A are whole tones (ratio ~1.122); E–F is a semitone (ratio ~1.059). Within the whole tones the ratios are close but not identical because the listed frequencies are rounded; in equal temperament, a semitone is exactly 2^(1/12) ≈ 1.0595 and a whole tone is 2^(2/12) ≈ 1.1225. (c) Musical pitch is built from equal ratios of frequency (a geometric progression), not equal frequency differences. An octave is a ratio of exactly 2:1; each semitone is a ratio of 2^(1/12). This is why equal linear steps in water level do not give equal musical steps — the relationship between the physical variable (water level) and the perceptual/musical variable (pitch, which is logarithmic in frequency) is non-linear.
2. **Q:** If the pitch of a struck bottle depended linearly on the water depth (f = a + b·d, where d is depth and a, b are constants), (a) would equal water-depth steps give equal frequency steps? (b) Would equal frequency steps correspond to equal musical steps? Use the answer to Q1 to explain why a linear water-level model still would not produce a musical scale.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Yes — in a linear model f = a + b·d, equal steps in d give equal steps in f (each step adds b Hz). (b) No. Musical steps are equal ratios of frequency, not equal differences. Even if equal water steps gave equal frequency differences (say +30 Hz each), the ratios would shrink as the frequency rose: 30 Hz added to 262 Hz is a ratio of 292/262 = 1.115, but 30 Hz added to 392 Hz is a ratio of 422/392 = 1.077. These are different musical intervals. So even a perfectly linear water-level model would not produce a true musical scale. A musical scale requires equal frequency ratios, which means the water depth would have to follow an exponential (geometric) progression — each step multiplied by the same factor — not a linear one.
3. **Q:** Design an experiment to test whether the struck-bottle pitch is better described by a linear or a power-law relationship with water depth. Describe the measurements you would take, how you would analyse them, and what result would distinguish the two models.
   **Worked Answer:** Fill a bottle to ten different water depths (e.g., 1, 2, 3 ... 10 cm). At each depth, strike the bottle and read the frequency with a tuner app. Record (depth, frequency) for all ten. Plot frequency against depth. If the points lie on a straight line, the linear model f = a + b·d fits. If the points curve (e.g., a concave or convex curve), try a power-law model f = k·d^n: take the logarithm of both f and d and plot log(f) against log(d). If the power law holds, the log-log plot is a straight line with slope n. Compare the two fits (by eye or by computing the residual for each). If the log-log line is straight and the linear plot curves, the power law is the better model. The result distinguishes the models because a linear model predicts a straight f-vs-d plot, while a power-law model predicts a straight log(f)-vs-log(d) plot. Either way, neither model is guaranteed to produce equal musical steps, because musical steps require equal frequency ratios, not equal frequency differences.

## Safety Notes
- Glass bottles can break if struck too hard. Strike gently with the side of a wooden stick. Keep a cloth under the bottles.
- Broken glass is sharp — the teacher sweeps it up with a brush and dustpan; learners do not touch shards.
- Water spills make the floor slippery. Keep a rag or mop at the station and mop up at once.
- Keep phone tuners away from the water jug — a dropped phone in water is ruined. Set the phone on a dry stand a little back from the bottles.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lesson 334 built the xylophone and established that water level sets the struck pitch and that struck vs blown give opposite pitch directions. This lesson tunes the instrument and introduces the non-linear relationship between the physical control (water level) and the musical result (pitch).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on tuning each bottle to the reference, playing the scale and a short tune, and recording the water depths. Ages 14–17 should tackle the Advanced Questions on frequency ratios, the linear-vs-power-law test, and the logarithmic nature of musical pitch.
- Local resource tips: free phone tuner apps ("guitar tuner", "pitch pipe") are widely available; if no phone, a teacher or learner with a good ear can sing the reference scale, and a pitch pipe (sold in music shops) is a reliable alternative. If no reference at all is available, tune the bottles to equal-sounding steps and use the lesson to discuss why "equal-sounding" is not the same as "equal water steps".
- Save the tuned and labelled xylophone for the Class Orchestra (Lesson 347). Store bottles with water levels intact; top up any that evaporate before the orchestra lesson.
- The "equal steps" investigation is the bridge to Lesson 336 (one-string guitar, where f ∝ 1/L is also non-linear) and to the pan-pipe lesson (340), where lengths are cut to a geometric progression to make a scale. Emphasise that musical scales are built from frequency ratios, not length differences.
