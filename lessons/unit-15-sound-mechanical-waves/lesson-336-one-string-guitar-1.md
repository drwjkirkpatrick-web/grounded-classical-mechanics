# Lesson 336: Build: The One-String Guitar I

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** one-string

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a one-string guitar using a board, a wire or fishing line, a bottle bridge, and a hanging stone for tension.
2. Pluck the string and hear a clear pitch, and change the pitch by moving the bridge to shorten the vibrating length.
3. Measure the vibrating length and record the pitch for at least three different lengths.
4. State the relationship between vibrating length and pitch — shorter length gives higher pitch — and predict the pitch at half the length using f ∝ 1/L.

## Materials
- A flat board or plank about 60–80 cm long and 10–20 cm wide (offcut from a carpentry workshop or school bench)
- A length of wire or strong fishing line (nylon fishing line ~20 kg breaking strain, or thin galvanised wire from a jua kali fundi) — about 1.5 m
- A small bottle or short piece of pipe to act as the bridge (a small water bottle, a piece of bamboo, or a short metal tube)
- A stone or small weight (1–2 kg) to hang from the end of the wire and provide tension
- Two nails or screws to anchor the wire at each end of the board (and a hammer or screwdriver)
- A striker or pick: a pluck with the finger works, or a small piece of card or stiff plastic
- A ruler or measuring tape (to measure the vibrating length)
- A marker pen to mark fret positions
- A tuner app on a phone (optional, for reading the pitch) or the teacher's ear as a reference
- A notebook and pencil for each group

## Vocabulary
- **vibrating length** (urefu unaotetema) — the part of the string that is free to vibrate between the fixed end and the bridge
- **tension** (mvutano) — how tightly the string is pulled; set here by the hanging stone
- **bridge** (daraja) — the object that presses on the string and marks the end of the vibrating length
- **pitch** (sauti ya juu au chini) — how high or low the note sounds; set by the vibration rate (frequency)
- **f ∝ 1/L** (sauti inavyopatikana kwa urefu) — the frequency is inversely proportional to the length: halving the length doubles the frequency (raises the pitch by an octave)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. On a guitar, when a player presses a string against a fret closer to the body, the note goes higher. Does the vibrating length get shorter or longer?
2. If the vibrating length of a string is halved, what happens to the pitch — does it rise or fall?
3. Name one real instrument that changes pitch by changing the length of a vibrating string.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Shorter. Pressing the string against a fret nearer the body shortens the part that vibrates, and a shorter vibrating length gives a higher pitch.
2. The pitch rises. Halving the length roughly doubles the frequency (f ∝ 1/L), raising the note by about an octave.
3. Any of: guitar, violin, orutu (a Kenyan single-string fiddle), litungu (a Kenyan string instrument), nyatiti. Accept any stringed instrument that changes pitch by length.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Have groups lay out boards, wire, and stones.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the board and a length of wire. Ask: *can we make a guitar from this?* Pluck a loose wire — it buzzes, no clear note. Hang a stone on one end to tighten it, pluck again — a faint pitch. Press a pencil against the wire as a bridge, shortening the vibrating part, and pluck — a higher note. Ask: *what did the bridge do?* Board the challenge: *today we build a one-string guitar and find how length sets the pitch.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners build the one-string guitar (see Hands-On Build). They fix the wire to the board, hang the stone for tension, set the bottle bridge at a chosen length, and pluck. They measure the vibrating length, listen to the pitch, then move the bridge to shorten the length and pluck again. Each group records length and pitch (by ear or tuner app) for at least three lengths, and tests the half-length prediction.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw the string and label the vibrating length L between the fixed end and the bridge. Write f ∝ 1/L: frequency is inversely proportional to length. Halving L doubles f (one octave higher); doubling L halves f (one octave lower). Confront the misconception that *a tighter string is always a higher string* — tension does set the pitch, but in this lesson tension is held constant (same stone); we change only the length. The relationship f ∝ 1/L is the same one that governs the air column in a blown bottle (Lesson 334) and the pan pipes in Lesson 340 — shorter is higher.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *shorten the string, raise the pitch; halve the length, go up an octave.* Homework: on the one-string guitar, measure the full length and the half length, pluck each, and write down whether the half-length note sounds like "the same note but higher" (an octave).

## Hands-On Build: The One-String Guitar
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Prepare boards and wire the day before.*

### Parts
- A flat board, 60–80 cm long, 10–20 cm wide
- About 1.5 m of wire or strong fishing line (nylon ~20 kg or thin galvanised wire)
- A small bottle or short pipe as the bridge
- A stone or weight, 1–2 kg
- Two nails or screws (and a hammer or screwdriver) to anchor the wire at one end
- A way to hang the stone from the other end of the wire (a knot, or a small loop of wire)
- A ruler or measuring tape
- A marker pen
- A phone tuner app (optional) or the teacher's ear
- A notebook and pencil

### Workflow
1. Fix one end of the wire to the board near one end, using a nail or screw so the wire cannot slip. **Checkpoint:** the wire is firmly anchored — pull it hard and it does not move.
2. Run the wire along the full length of the board. At the far end, let the wire hang over the edge and attach the stone so the wire is under tension. The stone should hang freely and pull the wire taut across the board. **Checkpoint:** the wire is taut and straight along the board; the stone hangs clear of the floor.
3. Place the bottle bridge under the wire, somewhere along the board. The bridge presses the wire against the board and marks the end of the vibrating length. The vibrating length L is the distance from the fixed nail to the bridge. **Checkpoint:** the bridge holds the wire firmly and the wire on the far side of the bridge does not vibrate (it is damped by the board).
4. Pluck the wire between the nail and the bridge. Listen for a clear pitch. If the note is faint or buzzy, check that the bridge presses firmly and the stone is heavy enough to keep the wire taut. **Checkpoint:** a clear, sustained note is heard when the wire is plucked.
5. Measure the vibrating length L (nail to bridge) with the ruler and record it. Pluck and note the pitch (by ear or tuner app). **Checkpoint:** the length and the pitch are both recorded.
6. Move the bridge to shorten the vibrating length to about 2/3 of the first length. Pluck and record the new length and pitch. **Checkpoint:** the pitch is higher than before, and the new length is recorded.
7. Move the bridge to shorten the length to about half of the first length. Pluck and record. **Checkpoint:** the half-length note is clearly higher — about an octave above the full-length note.
8. Test the prediction: pluck the full length, then the half length. Does the half-length note sound like "the same note but higher"? If using a tuner app, check whether the half-length frequency is about double the full-length frequency. **Checkpoint:** the group has written down whether the half-length note is an octave above the full-length note.

### Common Mistakes
- **Wire not taut** — if the stone is too light or the wire slips at the nail, the string buzzes and has no clear pitch. Use a heavier stone (1–2 kg) and fix the wire firmly; nylon fishing line may need several wraps around the nail to grip.
- **Bridge not pressing firmly** — a loose bridge lets the wire slide and the note is unclear. Press the bridge down so the wire is pinned against the board; a cylindrical bottle or pipe works well because it grips the wire.
- **Plucking too hard** — a hard pluck makes the wire slap the board and produces a harsh clang, not a musical note. Pluck gently, like plucking a chicken feather.
- **Measuring to the wrong point** — the vibrating length is from the fixed nail (or the edge of the board if that is the anchor) to the bridge, not to the hanging stone. Always measure the part that is free to vibrate.
- **Changing the tension when moving the bridge** — if the stone swings or the wire slips as the bridge moves, the tension changes and the pitch change is due to tension, not length. Keep the stone hanging still and the wire fixed at the nail.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. On a guitar, when a player presses a string against a fret closer to the body, the note goes higher. Does the vibrating length get shorter or longer? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If the vibrating length of a string is halved, what happens to the pitch? (mirrors pre-test)
3. On the one-string guitar, a learner keeps the same stone (same tension) and moves the bridge to halve the length. The pitch rises. Why must the tension be kept the same for this test to be fair?
4. A learner measures a full vibrating length of 60 cm and a half length of 30 cm. The tuner app reads 165 Hz at 60 cm. Predict the frequency at 30 cm using f ∝ 1/L.
5. Challenge: The learner now wants to find the length that gives a note a fifth above the full-length note (a frequency ratio of 3/2). What length should the vibrating part be, as a fraction of the full length? Show your reasoning.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Shorter. The fret shortens the vibrating part, and a shorter length gives a higher pitch.
2. The pitch rises. Halving the length roughly doubles the frequency (f ∝ 1/L), raising the note by about an octave.
3. Tension also sets the pitch (a tighter string is higher). If the tension changes when the bridge moves, the pitch change is due to both length and tension, and we cannot isolate the length effect. Keeping the tension constant (same stone, wire not slipping) ensures that any pitch change is due to the length alone.
4. f ∝ 1/L, so f₂/f₁ = L₁/L₂ = 60/30 = 2. f₂ = 2 × 165 = 330 Hz.
5. For a fifth, the frequency ratio f₂/f₁ = 3/2. Since f ∝ 1/L, L₂/L₁ = f₁/f₂ = 2/3. So the vibrating length should be 2/3 of the full length.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** The frequency of a stretched string is given by f = (1/2L)√(T/μ), where L is the vibrating length, T is the tension, and μ is the mass per unit length of the string. (a) Show that if T and μ are constant, f ∝ 1/L. (b) A wire has a full vibrating length of 60 cm and a tension of 15 N. The tuner reads 165 Hz. Predict the frequency when the bridge is moved to (i) 40 cm and (ii) 30 cm. Verify the ratios.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) With T and μ constant, f = (1/2L)√(T/μ) = (constant)/L, so f ∝ 1/L. (b) (i) L = 40 cm = 0.40 m. f₂ = f₁ × (L₁/L₂) = 165 × (0.60/0.40) = 165 × 1.5 = 247.5 Hz. (ii) L = 30 cm = 0.30 m. f₃ = 165 × (0.60/0.30) = 165 × 2 = 330 Hz. Ratios: 247.5/165 = 1.5 (a fifth up), 330/165 = 2 (an octave up). These match the musical intervals (3/2 for a fifth, 2/1 for an octave).
2. **Q:** The same wire is now tightened by increasing the hanging stone from 1 kg to 4 kg, keeping the length at 60 cm. (a) If the tension is proportional to the hanging mass, by what factor does the frequency change? (b) The original frequency was 165 Hz. What is the new frequency? (c) Why does doubling the mass not double the frequency?
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Tension is proportional to mass: T₂/T₁ = 4/1 = 4. Since f ∝ √T, f₂/f₁ = √(T₂/T₁) = √4 = 2. The frequency doubles. (b) f₂ = 2 × 165 = 330 Hz. (c) Because f depends on the square root of tension, not on tension directly. Doubling the mass increases the frequency by √2 ≈ 1.41, not by 2. To double the frequency by tension alone, the tension must be quadrupled (as here: 1 kg → 4 kg gives √4 = 2).
3. **Q:** A learner wants to mark fret positions on the one-string guitar for a five-note (pentatonic) scale starting at the full length L₀ and ending an octave higher at L₀/2. The frequency ratios for a major pentatonic scale relative to the root are 1, 9/8, 5/4, 3/2, 2. (a) Calculate the five vibrating lengths as fractions of L₀. (b) Explain why the frets get closer together as you move toward the bridge.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Since f ∝ 1/L, the length ratio L/L₀ = 1/(frequency ratio). For ratios 1, 9/8, 5/4, 3/2, 2: L₁/L₀ = 1/1 = 1.000 (full length, the root); L₂/L₀ = 1/(9/8) = 8/9 ≈ 0.889; L₃/L₀ = 1/(5/4) = 4/5 = 0.800; L₄/L₀ = 1/(3/2) = 2/3 ≈ 0.667; L₅/L₀ = 1/2 = 0.500 (the octave). So the lengths are L₀, 0.889L₀, 0.800L₀, 0.667L₀, 0.500L₀. (b) The lengths shrink by smaller and smaller absolute amounts as you go up: from 1.000 to 0.889 (a gap of 0.111L₀), then to 0.800 (gap 0.089L₀), then to 0.667 (gap 0.133L₀), then to 0.500 (gap 0.167L₀). Wait — recheck: the gaps are 0.111, 0.089, 0.133, 0.167 of L₀. The frets do not simply get closer; the pattern depends on the scale. But in general, because the relationship is 1/L (inverse), equal musical steps (equal frequency ratios) correspond to lengths that shrink by ratios, so the absolute gaps between successive frets decrease as the length shortens — the frets crowd together toward the bridge. This is why guitar frets are closer together near the body.

## Safety Notes
- Wire under tension can snap and whip. Use undamaged wire or fishing line; inspect for nicks before tensioning. Wear eye protection if available, and do not lean over the wire when plucking. Keep faces back from the string.
- The hanging stone can fall and injure a foot. Set up on a stable surface, keep the stone close to the floor, and do not stand under the hanging weight. Place a folded cloth under the stone to cushion it if it falls.
- Nails or screws driven into the board can split the wood or fly out if hammered carelessly. The teacher supervises all nailing; drive nails in straight and not too close to the board edge.
- Wire ends are sharp. Tape or bend over any sharp end after fixing the wire; do not leave loose wire ends pointing outward.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: Lessons 334 and 335 (bottle xylophone) established that the physical control (water level) sets the pitch, and that the relationship is non-linear. This lesson introduces a second stringed family where the control is length and the law is f ∝ 1/L. Lesson 337 will build on this to find the octave, fifth, and fourth positions and play songs.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the guitar, plucking a clear note, shortening the length to raise the pitch, and testing the half-length octave. Ages 14–17 should derive f ∝ 1/L from f = (1/2L)√(T/μ), handle the tension dependence, and calculate fret positions in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: fishing line (nylon, ~20 kg breaking strain) is sold in any market or fishing shop and makes an excellent, safe string. Thin galvanised wire is available from jua kali workshops but is more likely to snap and whip — prefer nylon for younger learners. Boards are offcuts from any carpentry workshop. A small water bottle or a piece of bamboo makes a good bridge.
- Keep tension constant for the length investigation: the same stone, hanging still. If learners want to explore tension as well, they can add a second stone — but that is a separate experiment and should not be mixed with the length test.
- Save the one-string guitar for Lesson 337 (Ratios and Songs). Loosen the wire (remove the stone) for storage so the wire does not stretch or fatigue; re-tension at the start of the next lesson.
- The f ∝ 1/L relationship is the same one that governs the blown bottle (air column) and the pan pipes (Lesson 340). Emphasise the unifying idea: shorter vibrating length, higher pitch, whether the vibrating body is a string or an air column.
