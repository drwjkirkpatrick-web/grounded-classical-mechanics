# Lesson 337: Build: The One-String Guitar II — Ratios and Songs

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** one-string

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Find the octave, fifth and fourth positions on the one-string guitar by measuring fractions of the string length.
2. Mark frets for those intervals and play each note with the bottle xylophone ensemble.
3. State the relationship between string length and pitch (shorter length = higher pitch, f ∝ 1/L).
4. Predict the pitch change when a finger shortens the vibrating length to a given fraction.

## Materials
- The one-string guitar built in Lesson 336 (wood plank, fishing line or bicycle-brake wire, tuning peg or wedge)
- Ruler or tape measure (30 cm)
- Permanent marker or chalk to mark fret positions
- Phone tuner app (free: GuitarTuna, Pitched Tuner) or a tuning fork / bottle xylophone from Lesson 335 for pitch comparison
- Pencil and notebook

## Vocabulary
- **octave** (safu ya nane) — the note eight steps up that sounds "the same but higher"; happens at half the string length
- **fifth** (sauti ya tano) — a pleasing interval found at two-thirds of the string length
- **fourth** (sauti ya nne) — an interval found at three-quarters of the string length
- **fret** (freti) — a marked line on the stringboard where you press to shorten the vibrating length
- **frequency** (masikio mara ngapi) — how many times per second the string vibrates; higher frequency = higher pitch

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. You pluck a string and hear a note. If you press the string at its exact middle and pluck one half, will the new note be higher, lower, or the same?
2. What length of string vibrates when you press at the middle — half or the whole?
3. Shorter strings make higher pitches. Is this true or false?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Higher — and it is one octave above the open string.
2. Half the length vibrates.
3. True.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before touching the instruments.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — pluck the open one-string guitar, then press at the middle and pluck. The class hears the octave jump. Ask: *where on the string did I press?* Lay a ruler beside the string and show it was at the halfway mark.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — pairs measure the full vibrating length L (nut to bridge). They compute L/2, 2L/3, 3L/4 with the ruler, and mark each spot with the marker. They press-and-pluck at each mark and compare the note to the bottle xylophone (Lesson 335) to name the interval. They record the fraction, the measured distance, and "higher/lower/equal" for each.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board: frequency is inversely proportional to length, **f ∝ 1/L**. Half length → double frequency → one octave up. Two-thirds → a fifth (frequency ×3/2). Three-quarters → a fourth (frequency ×4/3). Confront the misconception that "pressing harder makes it higher" — it is the *shorter length*, not the force, that raises pitch. The same fraction on any string gives the same musical interval.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *musical intervals are length ratios*. Homework: find the octave mark on any string instrument at home or school (a neighbours' guitar, an orutu, a nyatiti) and write the measured distance.

## Hands-On Build: Fretted One-String Guitar — Ratios and Songs
*Build continues on the guitar from Lesson 336. Marking is reversible with chalk.*

### Parts
- One-string guitar from Lesson 336 — 1 per pair
- Ruler or tape measure — 30 cm
- Permanent marker or chalk — for fret marks
- Phone tuner or bottle xylophone — for pitch comparison

### Workflow
1. Measure the full vibrating length L from nut to bridge; record it in cm.
2. Compute three marks: L/2 (octave), 2L/3 (fifth), 3L/4 (fourth). **Checkpoint:** the L/2 mark must sit exactly halfway — verify by doubling the measurement.
3. Mark each spot lightly with chalk or marker; label "8", "5", "4".
4. Press the string firmly onto the board at each mark and pluck. Listen and compare to the bottle xylophone to confirm the interval.
5. Play a short pattern — open, fifth, octave, open — slowly, then faster, with the xylophone ensemble joining.
6. Check with the tuner: the octave note should read one octave above the open note.

### Common Mistakes
- **Measuring from the wrong end** — always measure from the nut (the high end), not the bridge; use the same zero point every time.
- **Pressing on the mark instead of behind it** — press the string down *just behind* the mark so the vibrating length ends at the fret, not your finger.
- **Loose string** — a slack string gives muddy notes; re-tension at the peg first (Lesson 336 skill).

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. You press a string at its middle and pluck. Compared to the open string, what happens to pitch and what fraction of the length vibrates? (mirrors pre-test)
2. True or false: a shorter vibrating length always gives a higher pitch. Explain in one sentence. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A string is 60 cm long. At what distance from the nut is the octave mark, and at what distance is the fifth?
4. The frequency of the open string is 220 Hz. What is the frequency when you press at the octave (L/2)?
5. Challenge: why does the same fraction of length give the same musical interval on every string, thick or thin?

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Pitch goes up one octave; half the length vibrates.
2. True — because f ∝ 1/L, shorter length means higher frequency and higher pitch.
3. Octave = 60/2 = 30 cm. Fifth = 2/3 × 60 = 40 cm.
4. f doubles: 2 × 220 = 440 Hz (one octave up).
5. The interval depends on the *ratio* of lengths, not the absolute length or thickness. Halving any string doubles its frequency, so the musical interval (octave) is the same everywhere.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A one-string guitar has a vibrating length of 64 cm and an open-string frequency of 196 Hz (the note G). Find the measured distance from the nut for the octave, fifth, and fourth, and the frequency at each.
   **Worked Answer:** Octave: L/2 = 32 cm, f = 2 × 196 = 392 Hz. Fifth: 2L/3 = 42.7 cm, f = (3/2) × 196 = 294 Hz. Fourth: 3L/4 = 48 cm, f = (4/3) × 196 ≈ 261 Hz. Each mark is closer to the nut than the next-lower interval.
2. **Q:** Using f ∝ 1/L, derive fret positions for a five-note pentatonic scale starting on the open string (frequency f₀) and including the octave. The pentatonic frequencies relative to f₀ are 1, 9/8, 5/4, 3/2, 5/3, 2. Give each length as a fraction of L.
   **Worked Answer:** Since f ∝ 1/L, L ∝ 1/f. So each fret length = L × (f₀/f). For f = f₀: L. For 9f₀/8: L × 8/9 = 0.889L. For 5f₀/4: L × 4/5 = 0.80L. For 3f₀/2: L × 2/3 = 0.667L. For 5f₀/3: L × 3/5 = 0.60L. For the octave 2f₀: L/2 = 0.50L. Measure these five fractions from the nut and mark them.
3. **Q:** A learner marks the fifth at 2L/3 but hears a note that is not in tune with the bottle xylophone's fifth. Give two physics reasons why the real note might be off.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) The string tension may have changed since measuring — a slack or stretched string shifts all frequencies (f ∝ √T), so the ratio stays right but the absolute pitch is wrong. (b) The string thickness or stiffness adds end correction so the effective vibrating length is slightly less than the marked length; press just behind the fret, not on it, and re-tune the open string first.

## Safety Notes
- The one-string guitar wire or fishing line is under tension and can snap — check for fraying before plucking; keep faces away from the line.
- Marker ink stains clothing and boards; use chalk if the board is shared.
- Do not press the string so hard the plank tips or the string cuts the finger; use the pad of the finger, not the nail.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 336 (One-String Guitar I) built the instrument, and Lesson 334–335 built the bottle xylophone. This lesson uses both together — set the xylophones out where pairs can reach them.
- Younger learners (10–13): focus on measuring the three fractions and hearing the intervals; do not require the f ∝ 1/L algebra. Older learners: complete the advanced fret-derivation and check frequencies with the tuner app.
- Local tip: a bicycle-brake inner wire from a jua kali fundi makes a durable, tunable string; fishing line (monofilament) is cheaper but stretches in heat — retune during the lesson.
- CBE assessment note: the marked fretboard and the played pattern are evidence for the "measure and compare" competency; record which learners reached the pentatonic derivation.
