# Lesson 338: Drums: The Physics of the Ngoma

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Describe how a drum skin vibrates to make sound and identify the drum as a vibrating membrane.
2. Show that a tighter drum skin produces a higher pitch and a larger drum produces a lower pitch.
3. Explain why warming a drum head by a fire raises its pitch (skin tightens).
4. Relate membrane tension and size to the wave speed on the skin and hence to pitch.

## Materials
- A real ngoma (drum) if the school or a neighbour has one; otherwise a tin can or debe with a balloon or inner-tube rubber stretched over the open end as a membrane drum
- A second small container (e.g. a sufuria or tin) with a membrane of different size for comparison
- A small heat source kept at a distance (a lit jiko or candle, or a lamp) — used only by the teacher to warm the drum head briefly
- Rubber bands or string to tension the membrane
- A mallet or stick to strike the drum (a short pencil or folded cloth works)
- Phone tuner app or a tuned bottle xylophone for pitch comparison

## Vocabulary
- **membrane** (kavu ya ngoma) — the stretched skin (or rubber) that vibrates as a sheet, not a string
- **tension** (mvutano) — how tightly the skin is pulled; more tension = higher pitch
- **pitch** (sauti ya juu/chini) — how high or low a note sounds
- **node** (kituo) — a point on the membrane that barely moves while the rest vibrates
- **mode** (mtindo wa mtikiso) — a pattern of vibration of the membrane (e.g. one ring up-and-down)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. When you beat a drum, what part of the drum is actually vibrating to make the sound?
2. Two drums are identical except one has a tighter skin. Which one sounds higher?
3. What do you think happens to a wet drum skin left to dry in the sun — does its pitch go up or down?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The skin (membrane) — the wood shell mainly amplifies.
2. The tighter-skinned drum sounds higher.
3. Pitch goes up — drying shrinks and tightens the skin.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before handling drums.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the teacher beats the ngoma once softly, then once hard, then pinches the skin tighter with one hand and beats again. The class notices the pitch change on the tightened beat. Ask: *what did I change?*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups of four work with a membrane drum (tin + balloon/inner-tube). They (a) tap the loose membrane, then twist the rubber band to tighten it and tap again; (b) compare their small drum with a larger membrane drum; (c) the teacher briefly warms one drum head near (not on) the heat source for 20 seconds and learners tap before and after. They record "higher / lower / same" for each change.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — board: a drum is a **membrane** — a 2-D vibrating sheet, not a 1-D string. Wave speed on the membrane is v ∝ √(tension / mass per area). Higher tension → faster waves → higher frequency (pitch). Bigger drum → longer waves fit → lower pitch. Warming dries and shrinks the skin → raises tension → raises pitch. Confront the misconception that "hitting harder makes a higher note" — force changes loudness, not pitch; the pitch comes from tension and size.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *pitch = tension and size; force = loudness*. Homework: find one drum or drum-like object at home (sufuria-and-balloon, a jerrican lid) and say whether it would sound high or low and why.

## Hands-On Build: Tin-Can Membrane Drum
*Build fits inside the Explore block; gather tins and rubber the day before.*

### Parts
- Tin can or debe — 1 per group — open at one end (smooth the rim with a stone or file)
- Balloon or inner-tube rubber — 1 per drum — cut to cover the open end
- Rubber band or string — to hold and tension the membrane
- Small stick, pencil, or folded cloth — as a mallet
- A larger container with membrane (e.g. bigger tin or sufuria) — for size comparison

### Workflow
1. Stretch the balloon or rubber flat over the open end of the tin.
2. Secure it with the rubber band; press out any wrinkles. **Checkpoint:** tap it — it should give a clear note, not a dull thud; if dull, retension.
3. Tap and listen; note the pitch (high/medium/low) on a phone tuner if available.
4. Twist the rubber band half a turn to tighten the membrane; tap again and compare. **Checkpoint:** the pitch should rise.
5. Compare with a group that has a larger membrane drum; note which is lower.
6. (Teacher only) warm the head near the heat for 20 seconds; the group taps immediately after and records the change.

### Common Mistakes
- **Wrinkled membrane** — dampens the vibration; smooth it flat and re-secure.
- **Hitting too hard** — dents the tin and splits the rubber; use a light tap with a soft mallet.
- **Warming too close or too long** — melts or burns the rubber; the teacher holds the drum at arm's length from the heat for a few seconds only.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. What part of a drum vibrates to produce sound? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Two drums differ only in skin tightness. Which sounds higher, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A learner says "if I hit the drum harder the note goes higher." Is this correct? Explain.
4. A large ngoma and a small ngoma have equally tight skins. Which has the lower pitch, and what is the reason in terms of waves?
5. Challenge: a drum head is warmed gently by a fire. Predict the pitch change and give the chain of causes.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The skin (membrane) — the shell mainly amplifies the vibration.
2. The tighter drum sounds higher — more tension gives faster waves on the membrane and a higher frequency.
3. Incorrect — hitting harder increases loudness (amplitude), not pitch; pitch comes from tension and size.
4. The larger ngoma has the lower pitch — a bigger membrane supports longer waves, which means lower frequency.
5. Pitch rises. Heat dries and tightens the skin → tension increases → wave speed on the membrane rises → frequency (pitch) rises.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** The wave speed on a membrane is v ∝ √(T/μ) where T is tension and μ is mass per unit area. A drum skin's tension is increased by 21% by tightening. By what factor does the fundamental frequency change, and does the pitch rise or fall?
   **Worked Answer:** Frequency f ∝ v ∝ √T (μ unchanged). New tension = 1.21T. √1.21 = 1.10. So the frequency rises by a factor of 1.10, i.e. +10%. Pitch rises.
2. **Q:** Two circular drums have the same skin tension and mass per area, but drum A has twice the diameter of drum B. Estimate how their fundamental frequencies compare.
   **Worked Answer:** The fundamental wavelength scales with the drum diameter, so λ_A ≈ 2λ_B. Since f = v/λ and v is the same (same tension and μ), f_A ≈ v/(2λ_B) = f_B/2. The larger drum sounds about one octave lower.
3. **Q:** A drum head is warmed so its tension rises by 44%. Predict the direction and approximate size of the frequency change, and state one assumption you make.
   **Worked Answer:** f ∝ √T, so new f = √1.44 × f₀ = 1.2 f₀ — a 20% rise in pitch. Assumption: the mass per area μ does not change much (the skin loses a little water but we treat μ as roughly constant), and the drum diameter is unchanged.

## Safety Notes
- Smooth all tin rims before stretching the membrane — a cut rim will slice the rubber and fingers.
- The heat-source warming is done by the teacher only, at arm's length, for a few seconds; never put rubber directly over a flame — it melts and gives toxic fumes.
- Striking with hard sticks can dent the tin and rebound; use soft mallets (pencil tip, folded cloth).
- Keep long hair and loose sleeves away from the jiko or candle.

## Teacher Notes
- Assumes Lesson 329 (What Is Sound?) introduced vibration-as-sound; this lesson extends the idea from strings to membranes. If a real ngoma is available from the music teacher or a local elder, borrow it for the engage demo — cultural authenticity matters.
- Younger learners (10–13): focus on the three observations (tighten raises pitch, bigger lowers pitch, heat raises pitch) in words; skip the √T algebra. Older learners: work the advanced questions and discuss why membrane modes are more complex than string harmonics (a teaser — no full derivation needed).
- Local tip: inner-tube rubber from a boda bodapuncture repair shop is cheap and durable; balloons split easily in the heat. A flat stone smoothed on a wall makes a safe rim file.
- CBE assessment note: the before/after pitch observations are evidence for the "investigate and explain" competency; record each group's three results.
