# Lesson 341: Build: The Debe Telephone

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** string-phone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Build a working string telephone from two tins or debes joined by a taut string.
2. Explain that sound travels along the string as a vibration, carrying energy from one tin to the other.
3. Test and compare how taut versus slack, long versus short, and string versus wire affect the loudness and clarity of the sound.
4. Predict and explain why a loose or touching string kills the call.

## Materials
- Two clean, empty tins or debes (mabati paint tins, powdered-milk tins, or milk packets — remove one end of each)
- About 10 m of strong string (sisal, cotton, or nylon twine)
- A sharp nail or awl to punch one hole in the bottom of each tin
- A short length of wire (fencing wire or bicycle-spoke wire) as an optional comparison medium
- Scissors or a knife
- Two small matchsticks or short sticks (to anchor the string inside each tin)
- Notebook and pencil for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **vibration** (mtetemo) — a rapid back-and-forth movement; what sound is made of
- **medium** (nyenzo) — the material a sound wave travels through — air, string, water, wood
- **taut** (imvivu) — pulled tight, so the string does not sag
- **transmit** (kuhamisha) — to pass energy or sound from one place to another
- **energy** (nguvu) — what the wave carries from the speaker's tin to the listener's tin
- **attenuate** (kupunguza) — to weaken a sound as it travels

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. When you speak, what actually moves from your mouth to a listener's ear?
2. If you and a friend each hold a tin can with a string between them, do you think you can hear each other through the string? Guess yes or no and say why.
3. If the string between the two tins is loose and sagging, do you think the call will still work? Guess yes or no.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Sound travels as a vibration through a medium (usually air). The air particles bump into each other, passing the vibration from your throat to the listener's ear. Nothing solid travels — only the wave energy.
2. Yes. The voice makes the bottom of the first tin vibrate; that vibration travels along the string as a wave and makes the bottom of the second tin vibrate, which the listener hears. (Accept any prediction with reasoning; the build will confirm it.)
3. No. A loose string cannot carry the vibration efficiently because it bends instead of pulling on the second tin. (Accept predictions; the test will confirm.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips; settle learners in pairs.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up two tins and a length of string. Ask: *Can two people talk secretly across a compound using only two tins and a string?* Let learners guess. Whisper into a tin while a helper listens at the other end across the classroom with a taut string. Watch the room go quiet. Tell learners that today they build and test the debe telephone and discover why it works.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in pairs, learners build the string telephone. They punch one small hole in the bottom of each tin, thread the string through, knot it around a matchstick inside each tin so it cannot pull out, then walk apart until the string is taut. One learner whispers; the other listens. They then test four conditions and record loudness (loud, medium, nothing): taut vs slack; 3 m vs 8 m; string vs wire; string touching the ground or a chair vs string hanging free. The teacher moves between pairs checking the knot and the string tension.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw two tins and a taut string. Show the voice making the first tin's base vibrate; the vibration travels as a wave along the string; the second tin's base vibrates and pushes air into the listener's ear. Confront the misconception that *sound travels through air inside the string* — there is no air column in the string; the wave rides the solid string itself, which transmits vibration faster and with less loss than air. Then explain why a slack string fails: it bends instead of pulling, so the vibration does not reach the second tin. A string touching a wall or the ground loses energy to that object and the call weakens.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *sound rides a taut solid string as a vibration; a loose or touching string wastes the energy.* Homework: at home, build a string telephone with a family member using any two tins and 5 m of string; report whether it worked and what happened when the string went slack.

## Hands-On Build: The Debe Telephone
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Gather parts the day before.*

### Parts
- Two clean tins or debes with one end removed (open end faces the ear/mouth)
- About 10 m of strong string (sisal or cotton twine)
- A nail or awl to punch one hole in the closed bottom of each tin
- Two matchsticks or short sticks
- Optional: a 3 m length of fencing wire for the string-vs-wire test

### Workflow
1. Punch one small hole in the centre of the closed bottom of each tin. **Checkpoint:** the hole is just big enough for the string — too large and the string pulls through, too small and the string frays.
2. Thread one end of the string through the hole from the outside into the tin. Tie a knot around a matchstick inside the tin so the string cannot pull back out. Repeat at the other tin. **Checkpoint:** pull the string hard from outside; the matchstick holds it inside the tin.
3. Two learners each take a tin and walk apart until the string is taut and off the ground. **Checkpoint:** the string is straight and does not sag; if you pluck it, it gives a clear note.
4. One learner holds the open end of the tin to the ear; the other speaks quietly into the open end of their tin. Test the call. **Checkpoint:** the listener hears the whisper clearly.
5. Test the four conditions (taut vs slack; 3 m vs 8 m; string vs wire; free-hanging vs touching). Record loudness as loud, medium, or nothing for each. **Checkpoint:** at least the taut-string condition is loud; the slack condition is nothing or very faint.

### Common Mistakes
- **String too slack** — a sagging string bends instead of pulling the second tin, killing the call. Walk further apart or pull the string tight before speaking.
- **Hole too big** — the string pulls through or the knot slips. Make the hole small and use a matchstick anchor inside the tin.
- **String touching the ground, a chair, or the wall** — the vibration leaks into the object it touches, weakening the sound. Hold the string clear of all surfaces, or stand on chairs if needed.
- **Speaking too loudly** — shouting makes the tin rattle and the sound distorts. A quiet, clear voice works better and tests the string's transmission, not the shouting.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. When you speak through a string telephone, what carries the sound from one tin to the other? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Does the call still work if the string is loose and sagging? Say yes or no and explain why. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why does the string need to be taut for the telephone to work well?
4. A learner lets the string rest against a desk while talking. The listener reports the sound is faint. Give one reason why.
5. Challenge: a pair tests string versus wire with the same length and tension. The wire sounds louder. Using the idea of energy transmission, suggest why the wire carries the vibration better than the string.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A vibration travels along the taut string as a wave, carrying sound energy from the speaking tin to the listening tin. The solid string transmits the vibration; no air column inside the string is involved.
2. No. A loose string bends instead of pulling on the second tin, so the vibration does not travel along it and the call fails or is very faint.
3. A taut string is under tension, so a vibration at one end pulls directly on the other end. A slack string absorbs the vibration by bending, so the energy never reaches the listener's tin.
4. When the string touches the desk, the vibration transfers into the desk (the desk absorbs energy), so less energy reaches the second tin and the sound is faint.
5. A metal wire is denser and stiffer than string, so it transmits the vibration with less energy lost to bending and internal friction. The stiffer medium carries the wave more efficiently, so more sound energy reaches the second tin.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** The speed of sound in air is about 340 m/s, but in a stretched steel wire it is about 5000 m/s. If a string telephone uses a 10 m wire, how long does the sound take to travel along it? Compare this with the time to travel 10 m through air, and comment on why the delay is imperceptible to the listener.
   **Worked Answer:** Time along the wire = distance / speed = 10 m / 5000 m/s = 0.002 s. Time through air = 10 m / 340 m/s ≈ 0.029 s. Both are far below the human perception threshold for delay (about 0.1 s), so the listener cannot tell the sound took any time to arrive. However, the wire delivers more energy with less spreading loss than air, which is why the call is clearer.
2. **Q:** Explain in terms of energy transmission why a loose string kills the call. Use the idea that the wave must do work on the second tin to make it vibrate.
   **Worked Answer:** For the second tin to vibrate, the wave arriving at it must do work — it must apply a force through a displacement on the tin's base. In a taut string, tension couples each segment to the next, so the wave energy propagates and arrives at the second tin with enough energy to vibrate it. In a loose string, the tension is near zero, so adjacent segments are poorly coupled; the wave energy goes into bending the string (moving it sideways) rather than transmitting a longitudinal pull. The energy dissipates as heat and motion of the string itself, so almost no energy reaches the second tin to do the work of vibrating it. The call dies.
3. **Q:** A pair uses a 3 m string and hears the call clearly; with 8 m of the same string the call is fainter. Give a quantitative reason for the loss, and suggest one way to improve the 8 m call without shortening the string.
   **Worked Answer:** The string has internal friction and some energy is lost to the air as the string vibrates along its length. Over a longer string, more energy is lost before the wave reaches the second tin, so the 8 m call is fainter. The fraction of energy arriving decreases with distance. To improve the 8 m call without shortening: increase the tension (pull the string tighter), which stiffens the string and reduces bending losses; or switch to a stiffer, smoother medium like wire, which has lower internal friction and transmits more energy over the longer distance.

## Safety Notes
- Punching holes in tin produces sharp edges. The teacher supervises all hole-punching; file the edges flat or cover with tape. Do not let young learners handle the nail point near their face.
- Taut string under tension can snap or recoil. Use strong string, not thin thread; inspect for fraying before pulling tight. Stand clear of the line when tensioning.
- Do not run while holding a stretched string telephone; the string can trip learners or catch on furniture. Walk slowly apart.
- Wire has sharp ends; bend the tips over with pliers so they cannot scratch.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have covered that sound is a vibration (Lesson 337) and that sound needs a medium (Lesson 338). This lesson shows a solid medium carrying sound better than air.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building, testing taut vs slack, and describing what they hear. Ages 14–17 tackle the energy-transmission reasoning and the wire-vs-string comparison in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: debes and powdered-milk tins are common in shops and homesteads; sisal twine is sold in any dukani. Fencing wire can be begged from a farmer or jua kali fundi. If tins are scarce, two paper cups work but transmit less energy — good for a comparison discussion.
- This is the first lesson of the string-phone project. Save the best pair of telephones for the project showcase later in the unit.
- A common surprise: thin nylon string can be too stretchy and damp the vibration; cotton or sisal usually works better. If a pair's telephone fails and the string is taut, check whether the string is stretchy nylon and suggest swapping.
