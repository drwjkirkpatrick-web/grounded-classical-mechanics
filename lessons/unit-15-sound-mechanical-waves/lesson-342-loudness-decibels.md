# Lesson 342: Loudness: Amplitude and the Decibel Idea

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that louder sound comes from a bigger vibration (greater amplitude).
2. Measure or estimate relative loudness in a Kenyan setting (drum, whisper, posho mill, classroom chatter).
3. Explain the decibel idea in simple terms: every 10 dB means 10 times more sound energy.
4. Identify sounds that can damage the ear and describe how to protect hearing.

## Materials
- A drum (ngoma) or a sufuria and a stick to strike it
- A sound-level meter app on a phone (optional; a free decibel app works well)
- A sheet of paper for each group to draw a simple loudness map of the school compound
- A ruler or small stick to show visible vibration
- Notebook and pencil for each learner
- Cotton wool or pieces of cloth to demonstrate ear protection (optional)

## Vocabulary
- **loudness** (nguvu ya sauti) — how strong a sound feels to the ear
- **amplitude** (muda kubwa) — the size of a vibration; bigger amplitude means louder sound
- **decibel** (desibeli) — the unit we use to compare sound levels; symbol dB
- **energy** (nguvu) — what a sound wave carries; more energy means a louder sound
- **hearing damage** (uharibifu wa masikio) — permanent harm to the ear from very loud or long sounds

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. If you hit a drum softly and then hit it hard, what changes about the sound besides the pitch?
2. Which is louder in your village or town: a whispered conversation or a running posho mill?
3. Have you ever had ringing in your ears after a loud event? Where were you?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The loudness changes — a hard hit makes a louder sound. The pitch (how high or low) stays about the same; the drum skin vibrates with a bigger amplitude.
2. The posho mill is much louder. (Accept any local loud source named — generator, matatu horn, preacher's speaker.)
3. Any personal answer is acceptable. Ringing (tinnitus) after loud sound is a sign the ear has been overworked. Use the answers to introduce ear safety.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — strike the drum softly, then hard, then very hard. Ask: *What changed?* Learners will say it got louder. Now strike the drum and rest a few grains of dry maize on the skin; strike soft, then hard, and let learners watch the grains jump higher on the hard strike. Say: *The bigger vibration is the louder sound — today we measure loudness and meet the decibel.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners map loudness around the school. They list six places or sources (quiet classroom, playground at break, near the road, the kitchen with a jiko, near a posho mill or generator if one is near, a whispered conversation) and rank them from quietest to loudest. If a phone with a decibel app is available, they take a reading at each spot and record the dB value. Without an app, they rank by ear and label each "whisper", "conversation", "loud", "very loud". The teacher walks between groups, helping them describe the vibration size they would expect at each source.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw a small wave and a tall wave with the same wavelength; label amplitude on each. State: *Loudness is about amplitude — the size of the vibration, not how fast it vibrates.* Then introduce the decibel: every 10 dB up means 10 times more sound energy. Show a simple scale: 30 dB (quiet room), 60 dB (conversation), 80 dB (busy matatu stage), 100 dB (posho mill close up), 120 dB (thunder, pain threshold). Confront the misconception that *100 dB is just a bit louder than 60 dB* — 100 dB is actually 10,000 times more sound energy than 60 dB. Explain that sounds above about 85 dB for long periods, or 120 dB even briefly, can damage hearing; cotton or fingers help for a short burst, and moving away from the source is the best protection.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *loudness = amplitude; 10 dB up = 10 times the energy; protect your ears from posho mills and loud speakers.* Homework: list three sounds at home and rank them quiet, loud, very loud; write one sentence on how to protect your ears from the loudest one.

## Hands-On Build: Loudness Map and Drum Amplitude Demo
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- A drum or a sufuria and a stick
- A few grains of dry maize or small pebbles
- A sheet of paper per group for the loudness map
- A phone with a decibel app (optional; one per class is enough)

### Workflow
1. Rest a few grains of maize on the drum skin. Strike softly and watch how high the grains jump. **Checkpoint:** the grains move a little.
2. Strike hard and watch the grains jump higher. **Checkpoint:** the grains jump clearly higher on the hard strike — the amplitude is bigger.
3. Walk to six places around the school (or name them). At each, rank the sound quiet, conversation, loud, or very loud, and record a dB reading if a phone app is available. **Checkpoint:** six entries are recorded with a rank and (if possible) a number.
4. Back in class, list the six sources from quietest to loudest on the map sheet. **Checkpoint:** the list is ordered and each source has its rank or dB value.
5. Circle any source above 85 dB (very loud) and write one protection method next to it. **Checkpoint:** each circled source has a protection note.

### Common Mistakes
- **Confusing loudness with pitch** — a high-pitched whistle can be quiet, and a low drum can be very loud. Ask learners: *did the pitch change when I hit harder, or only the loudness?* The pitch stayed; only the amplitude grew.
- **Standing too close to the loud source while measuring** — the reading is highest right at the source and drops with distance. Hold the phone at arm's length and at the same distance for each reading so comparisons are fair.
- **Reading the dB scale as a simple count** — learners may think 100 dB is "40 more than 60 dB". Stress that 10 dB is a ten-times step, so 40 dB more is 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000 times the energy.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. When you hit a drum hard instead of softly, what changes about the sound? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Which carries more sound energy: a whispered conversation or a running posho mill? (mirrors pre-test)
3. What does a bigger amplitude of vibration mean for the sound you hear?
4. A sound goes from 60 dB to 70 dB. Using the decibel idea, how many times more sound energy is this?
5. Challenge: a learner works near a posho mill all day (about 100 dB). Give two ways they can protect their hearing, and say which one is most effective and why.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The sound gets louder; the drum skin vibrates with a bigger amplitude. The pitch stays about the same.
2. The posho mill carries far more sound energy. (Accept any correct loud local source.)
3. A bigger amplitude means a louder sound — the vibration is larger, so it carries more energy and pushes the eardrum harder.
4. A 10 dB increase means 10 times more sound energy. So 60 dB to 70 dB is 10 times the sound energy.
5. Two methods: wear earplugs or stuff cotton in the ears; move further from the mill (sound energy drops with distance). Moving away is most effective because the sound energy falls rapidly with distance, and earplugs only block some of the energy. Best is to do both — move away and wear protection.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A posho mill produces a sound level of 100 dB and a normal conversation is 60 dB. How many times more sound energy does the posho mill put out than the conversation? Show your reasoning using the 10 dB = 10× rule.
   **Worked Answer:** The difference is 100 dB − 60 dB = 40 dB. Each 10 dB means 10 times the energy, so 40 dB = four steps of 10 dB = 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. The posho mill puts out 10,000 times more sound energy than a conversation.
2. **Q:** The sound level at a matatu stage is about 80 dB. How many times more sound energy is this than a whispered conversation at 30 dB? If the whisper carries 1 unit of energy, how many units does the matatu stage carry?
   **Worked Answer:** Difference = 80 − 30 = 50 dB. Each 10 dB is 10×, so 50 dB = five steps = 10^5 = 100,000 times. If the whisper is 1 unit, the matatu stage carries 100,000 units of sound energy.
3. **Q:** A learner sits 2 m from a loud speaker at a crusade and measures 100 dB. They move to 20 m away and the level drops to 80 dB. Did the energy drop by a factor of 10 or by more? Explain, using the inverse-square idea for spreading sound.
   **Worked Answer:** The level dropped by 20 dB, which is two steps of 10 dB, so the energy dropped by 10 × 10 = 100 times. Moving from 2 m to 20 m is a 10× increase in distance. By the inverse-square law, energy per unit area falls as 1/distance², so at 10× the distance the energy per area is 1/100 of the near value — a factor of 100. This matches the 20 dB drop. The learner's ears received 100 times less sound energy at 20 m, which is why the dB reading fell by 20 dB.

## Safety Notes
- Striking a drum or sufuria hard can send the stick flying or hit a hand. Strike the centre of the skin, not the edge, and keep hands clear.
- Do not hold a phone or ear close to a running posho mill or generator; loud sound can damage hearing in seconds. Stay several metres back for any measurement near machinery.
- Prolonged exposure above 85 dB causes hearing damage. If the class visits a loud source, keep the visit short (under a minute) and have learners cover their ears.
- Do not insert cotton, sticks, or anything small deep into the ear canal; ear protection sits over the ear opening, not inside it.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that sound is a vibration (Lesson 337) and that waves carry energy (Lesson 339). This lesson connects amplitude to loudness and introduces the decibel as a logarithmic scale, kept as a "10× per 10 dB" idea rather than full log mathematics.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the drum demo, the loudness map, and the idea that louder = bigger vibration and that very loud sounds hurt ears. Ages 14–17 work the decibel energy ratios in the Advanced Questions and reason about inverse-square spreading.
- Local resource tips: a posho mill is the classic Kenyan loud source; a matatu stage, a crusade or rally speaker, and a generator are equally good examples. A free phone decibel app is accurate enough for ranking; if no phone is available, the by-ear ranking is sufficient and teaches the same concept.
- The decibel is introduced as a comparison scale, not a formula. Avoid log base math with younger learners; the "10 dB = 10 times" rule is enough and is correct for sound energy.
- Ear safety is a serious public-health message in Kenya where posho mills, generators, and loud music are common. Repeat the protection advice (move away, cover ears, limit time) at every loud example.
