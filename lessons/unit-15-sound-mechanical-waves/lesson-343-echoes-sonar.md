# Lesson 343: Echoes and Sonar: Sound as a Ruler

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Explain that an echo is a reflected sound wave bouncing back from a hard surface.
2. Measure the distance to a wall or cliff by timing an echo and using distance = (speed × time) ÷ 2.
3. Describe how bats and fishermen's fish-finders use echoes (sonar) to find objects and depth.
4. Calculate the distance to a reflecting surface from a given echo time and the speed of sound.

## Materials
- A large open wall or a cliff at a known, safe distance (at least 20 m away and clear of traffic)
- A measuring tape or a measured piece of string (10 m or 20 m)
- A stopwatch (phone stopwatch is fine)
- A pair of wooden blocks or two stones to clap together loudly
- Notebook and pencil for each learner
- A picture or sketch of a bat and a fish-finder screen (drawn on the board if no picture is available)

## Vocabulary
- **echo** (sauti inayorudi) — a sound that bounces back from a hard surface so you hear it again
- **reflect** (kurudisha) — to bounce a wave off a surface, like light off a mirror
- **sonar** (sona) — using sound echoes to measure distance or find objects underwater
- **speed of sound** (mbio za sauti) — about 340 m/s in air; how fast sound travels
- **depth** (kina) — how deep the water is, which a fish-finder measures by echo time

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Have you ever shouted toward a wall or a cliff and heard your voice come back? What did you hear?
2. Why do you think you hear the shout a second time after the wall?
3. If sound takes time to travel, does the echo come back sooner from a near wall or a far wall?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Any personal answer is acceptable. The echo is the same shout heard again a moment later.
2. The shout hits the wall and bounces back (reflects), so the sound travels to the wall and back to your ear, arriving a moment after the original shout.
3. Sooner from a near wall. The sound has a shorter distance to travel to the wall and back, so the echo returns in less time.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — stand the class near the wall (or clap loudly in the room toward a hard wall if outside is not possible yet). Clap once with the blocks. Ask: *Did you hear the clap twice?* Most will hear the echo. Tell the story of a bat flying in the dark: it shouts tiny squeaks and listens for the echo to find a moth. Tell learners that today they will use sound the same way — as a ruler to measure distance.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — go to the open wall. Measure and mark a standing line 20 m from the wall with the tape or string. One learner stands at the line and claps the blocks hard, watching the stopwatch. They clap, start the watch, and stop it when they hear the echo. Repeat ten times and record each time; the group averages the results. Then move to 30 m and repeat. The teacher helps learners see that the time is the round trip — sound goes to the wall and back — so the distance to the wall is half of (speed × time).
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw a learner, a wall, and two arrows: one to the wall, one back, both labelled 340 m/s. Write: total distance = speed × time; distance to wall = (speed × time) ÷ 2. Show the bat example: a bat squeaks, the echo returns in 0.01 s, so the moth is (340 × 0.01) ÷ 2 = 1.7 m away. Then introduce the fish-finder (sonar): a boat sends a sound pulse into the water; the echo returns from the bottom; the time and the speed of sound in water (about 1500 m/s) give the depth. Confront the misconception that *the echo time is the one-way time* — it is the round trip; forgetting to divide by 2 doubles the distance and gives the wrong answer.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *an echo is sound bounced back; distance = (speed × time) ÷ 2 because the sound goes there and back.* Homework: clap toward a wall at home or near a shop and estimate its distance by counting "one, two" (each second ≈ 170 m one way) and halving.

## Hands-On Build: Echo Ranging with a Wall
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Pick the wall the day before and measure it is safe and clear of traffic.*

### Parts
- A large, flat, hard wall or cliff at least 20 m from a safe standing line
- A measuring tape or a pre-measured 20 m string
- A stopwatch (phone is fine)
- Two wooden blocks or two stones for a loud, sharp clap

### Workflow
1. Measure and mark a standing line exactly 20 m from the wall with the tape or string. **Checkpoint:** the distance from the line to the wall is 20 m — re-measure if unsure.
2. One learner stands at the line, claps the blocks once, starts the stopwatch at the clap, and stops it when the echo returns. Record the time. **Checkpoint:** a clear echo is heard; the time is between 0.1 s and 0.2 s.
3. Repeat the clap-and-time ten times. Record each time and calculate the average. **Checkpoint:** ten times are recorded; the average is written down.
4. Calculate the distance to the wall: distance = (340 × average time) ÷ 2. Compare with the measured 20 m. **Checkpoint:** the calculated distance is within 2–3 m of 20 m (human reaction time causes some error).
5. Move to 30 m and repeat steps 2–4. Compare the two calculated distances. **Checkpoint:** the 30 m time is clearly longer than the 20 m time, and the 30 m calculation is closer to 30 m than to 20 m.

### Common Mistakes
- **Forgetting to divide by 2** — the time measured is for the sound to go to the wall and back, so the one-way distance is half of (speed × time). Learners who skip the divide get 40 m instead of 20 m. Always ask: *is that the one-way or the round-trip distance?*
- **Slow stopwatch reaction** — human reaction time is about 0.2 s, which at 340 m/s is about 70 m of round-trip distance — a large error at 20 m. Average ten claps to reduce the error, and use a larger distance (30 m or more) so the echo time is bigger relative to the reaction time.
- **Clapping too softly** — a soft clap gives a weak echo that is hard to time. Clap the blocks hard and sharp; the wall needs a strong sound to reflect clearly.
- **Measuring to a sloping or soft surface** — a bush row, a wall of sacks, or a person does not reflect well. Use a flat, hard, solid wall or cliff.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. What is an echo, and what causes it? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Does the echo return sooner from a near wall or a far wall? (mirrors pre-test)
3. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. An echo returns in 0.20 s. What is the distance to the wall? Show your working.
4. How does a bat find a moth in the dark using sound? Give two steps.
5. Challenge: a fish-finder sends a sound pulse and the echo from the lake bottom returns in 0.10 s. The speed of sound in water is 1500 m/s. How deep is the water? Show your working and remember the sound goes down and back.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. An echo is a sound that bounces back (reflects) from a hard surface so you hear it again after the original sound. It is caused by the sound wave reflecting off the wall or cliff.
2. Sooner from a near wall. The sound has less distance to travel to the wall and back, so the echo returns in less time.
3. Total distance = speed × time = 340 × 0.20 = 68 m. This is the round trip. One-way distance = 68 ÷ 2 = 34 m. The wall is 34 m away.
4. The bat sends out a high squeak (a sound pulse); it listens for the echo that bounces off the moth. The shorter the echo time, the closer the moth. (Two steps: send the squeak, listen for the echo and judge the distance by the delay.)
5. Total distance = 1500 × 0.10 = 150 m. This is down and back, so depth = 150 ÷ 2 = 75 m. The water is 75 m deep.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** An echo from a cliff returns in 0.30 s. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. What is the distance to the cliff? Then, if the learner's reaction time adds 0.2 s to the measured time, what is the true distance and the error in the measurement?
   **Worked Answer:** Measured total distance = 340 × 0.30 = 102 m. One-way distance = 102 ÷ 2 = 51 m. If the true echo time is 0.30 − 0.20 = 0.10 s, the true total distance = 340 × 0.10 = 34 m, and the true one-way distance = 34 ÷ 2 = 17 m. The error is 51 − 17 = 34 m, an overestimate of 34 m (200% error). This shows why reaction time dominates at short distances and why averaging and using larger distances matter.
2. **Q:** Design an echo depth-sounder for a dugout canoe on Lake Victoria. State what sends the sound, what listens, the speed you must use, the time you must measure, and the formula for depth. If the bottom is 12 m down, what echo time should the device read?
   **Worked Answer:** Send a short sound pulse into the water from a transducer on the canoe hull (a small speaker or piezo buzzer mounted on the hull below the waterline). A microphone or hydrophone on the same hull listens for the echo from the bottom. Use the speed of sound in water, about 1500 m/s. Measure the time t between sending the pulse and hearing the echo. Depth = (1500 × t) ÷ 2. For a depth of 12 m, the round-trip distance is 24 m, so t = 24 ÷ 1500 = 0.016 s (16 ms). The device should read about 0.016 s for a 12 m depth.
3. **Q:** A fisherman's sonar shows fish at an echo time of 0.008 s and the lake bottom at 0.040 s. Using 1500 m/s, find the depth of the fish and the depth of the bottom. Is the fish above the bottom?
   **Worked Answer:** Fish depth = (1500 × 0.008) ÷ 2 = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 m. Bottom depth = (1500 × 0.040) ÷ 2 = 60 ÷ 2 = 30 m. The fish is at 6 m and the bottom is at 30 m, so the fish is above the bottom by 30 − 6 = 24 m.

## Safety Notes
- Use a wall or cliff clear of roads and traffic. Stand the class off any path or road; do the echo ranging on a school field or compound, not beside a highway.
- Clapping stones or blocks can pinch fingers. Show the correct grip; keep fingers clear of the striking faces.
- If using a cliff or riverbank for the outdoor echo test, keep learners back from the edge; do not approach a cliff base where loose stones may fall.
- Stopwatch use on a phone can distract; one learner times, the rest listen and watch, not play with phones.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should have measured the speed of sound with an echo in Lesson 340 (Measuring the Speed of Sound). This lesson reuses that skill and turns it around — using the known speed of sound to find an unknown distance.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the echo demo, the idea that sound bounces back, and the bat story. Ages 14–17 work the distance calculations, the reaction-time error analysis, and the sonar depth design in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: any large hard wall works — a school block wall, a mabati store wall, or a roadside retaining wall away from traffic. Fish-finder (sonar) is used by fishermen on Lake Victoria and at the coast; many learners will know the device. A bat is a good hook; any fruit bat around the school at dusk is a live demo of echolocation.
- The divide-by-2 error is the most common mistake. Drill it by asking at every step: *did the sound go one way or both ways?*
- The speed of sound in water (1500 m/s) is roughly 4.5 times the speed in air (340 m/s). Use this contrast to show that the medium changes the speed, which changes the time for the same distance.
