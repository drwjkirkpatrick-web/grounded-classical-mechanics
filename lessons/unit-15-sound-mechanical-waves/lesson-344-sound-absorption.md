# Lesson 344: Soaking Up Sound: Thatch, Soft Things and Quiet Rooms

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate that hard, smooth surfaces reflect sound while soft, rough surfaces absorb it.
2. Compare the sound of a clap in a bare hall versus a grass-thatched or cloth-lined space and describe the difference.
3. Explain that absorption reduces echo and reverberation by converting sound energy into a tiny amount of heat in the soft material.
4. Propose low-cost acoustic fixes for a noisy classroom using locally available materials.

## Materials
- A bare classroom or hall with hard walls (mabati, concrete, or plaster)
- A second space with soft surfaces (a grass-thatched room, a room with curtains and mats, or a corner lined with blankets)
- Two wooden blocks or two stones to clap loudly
- A few blankets, sisal mats, or old mattresses (mikeka)
- A piece of mabati or a hard wall section for a reflector comparison
- A notebook and pencil for each learner
- A pillow or a folded blanket for a single-object absorption test

## Vocabulary
- **absorb** (kumeza) — to take in sound energy so it does not bounce back; soft materials absorb
- **reflect** (kurudisha) — to bounce sound off a hard, smooth surface
- **reverberation** (mvurugo wa sauti) — the lingering of sound in a room because it bounces around many times before dying
- **echo** (sauti inayorudi) — a clear, separate repeat of a sound from one strong reflection
- **soft surface** (uso laini) — a material that soaks up sound, like cloth, thatch, or foam

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. When you clap in an empty classroom with hard walls, do you hear the sound linger or stop at once?
2. When you clap inside a room full of clothes, mattresses, or a grass-thatch wall, is the clap clearer or more muffled than in a bare room?
3. Why do you think people put curtains and carpets in a room — only for decoration, or for sound too?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The sound lingers — it bounces off the hard walls and floor and keeps going for a moment. This is reverberation.
2. The clap is more muffled and dies quickly. The soft things absorb the sound so it does not bounce around.
3. Not only for decoration. Curtains, carpets, mats, and soft furniture also absorb sound and reduce echo, making a room quieter and speech clearer. (Accept any answer that mentions sound; many learners will first say decoration, which the lesson will correct.)

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — clap once in the bare classroom and let everyone listen to the ring. Then clap into a pillow or a folded blanket held close to the blocks. Ask: *What was the difference?* The bare room rings; the pillow eats the sound. Tell learners that today they find out why some rooms shout back and others stay quiet, and how to fix a noisy classroom.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — in groups of 3–4, learners do the clap test in two spaces. First, clap in the bare classroom and rate the ring (how long the sound lingers) as long, medium, or short. Then go to the soft space (grass-thatch room, or a classroom corner with blankets hung on the wall and mats on the floor) and clap again; rate the ring. Back in class, each group places a few blankets or mats against one wall and claps toward it, then removes them and claps toward the bare wall, comparing the echo. They list soft versus hard surfaces found around the school (mabati, concrete, plaster, glass vs thatch, cloth, mikeka, mattresses, soil). The teacher moves between groups, asking them to describe the energy: where does the sound go when the wall is soft?
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Draw two walls: one hard and smooth (concrete), one soft and rough (thatch or cloth). Show arrows for sound hitting each. On the hard wall, the arrow bounces back — reflection. On the soft wall, the arrow goes in and dies — absorption. State: *soft materials absorb sound because their fibres and air pockets let the wave in and convert its energy into a tiny amount of heat; hard, smooth surfaces reflect sound because the wave cannot enter.* Explain reverberation: in a bare hall the sound bounces wall to wall many times, so it lingers and speech becomes muddy; in a soft room the sound is absorbed quickly, so speech is clear. Confront the misconception that *soft things "block" sound by being a wall* — they do not block it like a door; they soak it up by letting the wave enter and die in the fibres.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *hard surfaces reflect sound; soft surfaces absorb it and quiet the room.* Homework: find the noisiest room in your home and write one low-cost thing you could put on the wall or floor to make it quieter.

## Hands-On Build: Clap Test and Soft-Wall Comparison
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Arrange access to the second (soft) space the day before.*

### Parts
- A bare classroom or hall with hard walls
- A grass-thatched room, a curtained room, or a corner where blankets can be hung
- Two wooden blocks or stones for a loud, sharp clap
- A few blankets, sisal mats (mikeka), or an old mattress
- A pillow or folded blanket for the single-object test

### Workflow
1. In the bare classroom, stand at one end and clap the blocks once. The group listens and rates the ring as long, medium, or short. **Checkpoint:** the group agrees on a rating and writes it down.
2. Move to the soft space (or hang blankets along one wall and lay mats on the floor in a corner). Clap once from the same distance. Rate the ring. **Checkpoint:** the ring is clearly shorter or more muffled than in the bare room.
3. Back in class, hang one blanket on a hard wall. Clap toward the blanket, then clap toward the bare wall next to it. Compare. **Checkpoint:** the blanket-covered wall gives a softer, shorter echo than the bare wall.
4. List five hard and five soft surfaces found around the school. **Checkpoint:** the list has at least five hard (mabati, concrete, plaster, glass, floor tiles) and five soft (thatch, cloth, mats, mattresses, soil, people) surfaces.
5. Each group proposes one low-cost fix to make the classroom quieter (e.g., hang mats on the back wall, put a curtain over the door, lay mats on the floor). **Checkpoint:** the proposal names a real local material and says where it goes.

### Common Mistakes
- **Confusing "soft" with "thin"** — a thin bedsheet stretched tight reflects almost as well as a wall because it has no air pockets. Bunched cloth, blankets, and thatch work better because their fibres and air gaps absorb. Use thick, loose material, not a single tight sheet.
- **Covering only a small patch and expecting silence** — a single small blanket barely changes a big room. To hear a clear difference, cover a whole wall or a large section; absorption adds up with area.
- **Forgetting the floor and ceiling** — learners often focus on the walls. A bare concrete floor and a mabati ceiling also reflect. Mats on the floor and cloth or grass under the roof help as much as wall coverings.
- **Thinking absorption means soundproofing** — absorption reduces echo inside a room; it does not stop loud sound coming through the wall from outside. Different problem; do not promise learners the room will become silent to the street.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror the pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. When you clap in a bare classroom with hard walls, does the sound linger or stop at once? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Is a clap clearer or more muffled in a room full of cloth and mats compared to a bare room? (mirrors pre-test)
3. What does a soft surface do to sound that a hard surface does not?
4. Name two soft materials found in a Kenyan home that absorb sound, and two hard materials that reflect it.
5. Challenge: your classroom is very echoey and the teacher's voice is hard to hear at the back. Propose two low-cost fixes using local materials, and say how each one helps.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The sound lingers — it bounces off the hard walls and floor and keeps going for a moment. This is reverberation.
2. The clap is more muffled and dies quickly. The cloth and mats absorb the sound so it does not bounce around.
3. A soft surface absorbs sound — the wave enters the fibres and air pockets and its energy is converted into a tiny amount of heat, so little sound bounces back. A hard, smooth surface reflects the sound because the wave cannot enter.
4. Soft (absorb): thatch, blankets, mats (mikeka), mattresses, curtains, soil. Hard (reflect): mabati, concrete, plaster, glass, floor tiles. (Accept any two of each.)
5. Two low-cost fixes: (a) hang sisal mats (mikeka) or blankets on the back wall — the soft surface absorbs reflected sound and shortens the reverberation so the teacher's voice reaches the back clearly. (b) Lay mats on the bare concrete floor — the floor is a big reflector; covering it with soft mats absorbs sound from below and reduces the echo. (Accept any two fixes that use local materials and explain the absorption.)

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A bare classroom has a reverberation time of about 1.5 s (the sound takes 1.5 s to die away). After hanging mats on two walls, the reverberation time drops to 0.6 s. Explain in terms of sound energy why the time fell, and estimate what fraction of the sound energy is now absorbed before each bounce.
   **Worked Answer:** In the bare room, the sound bounces many times with little energy lost at each reflection, so it takes 1.5 s to die. With mats on the walls, each bounce delivers part of the sound energy into the mats, where it is converted to heat, so the sound loses energy faster and dies in 0.6 s. The reverberation time fell because more energy is absorbed per bounce. If the time fell from 1.5 s to 0.6 s, the sound now dies in 0.6/1.5 = 0.4 of the original time, meaning the energy decays much faster per bounce. A rough estimate: the mats now absorb a large fraction of the energy at each hit; the room keeps about 40% of its original ring time, so a good fraction (more than half) of the energy at each wall hit is absorbed rather than reflected.
2. **Q:** A studio has a wall area of 50 m². Bare concrete reflects about 97% of sound energy (absorbs 3%). If the wall is covered with a blanket that absorbs about 40% of the sound energy at each hit, what fraction of the original reflected energy now bounces back? By what factor does the reflected energy from this wall drop?
   **Worked Answer:** Bare wall reflects 0.97 of the energy. The blanket absorbs 0.40, so it reflects 0.60 of the energy. The reflected energy from the covered wall is 0.60 of the incoming energy compared with 0.97 for the bare wall. The factor drop = 0.97 ÷ 0.60 ≈ 1.6. So the reflected energy from this wall drops by a factor of about 1.6 (it is about 62% of the bare-wall reflection). Across many bounces, this adds up and shortens reverberation a lot.
3. **Q:** Propose three low-cost acoustic fixes for a noisy classroom and explain the physics behind each. Use materials available in a Kenyan market, homestead, or jua kali workshop.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Hang sisal mats (mikeka) or old blankets on the back and side walls — soft, thick fibres absorb sound energy into their air pockets, reducing reflected sound and shortening reverberation so speech is clearer. (b) Lay mats or a carpet on the bare concrete floor — the floor is a large reflector; a soft covering absorbs sound from below and cuts the up-and-down bounce path. (c) Hang a heavy curtain over the door and the windows, or stuff the space above a mabati ceiling with dry grass or cloth — these soft layers absorb sound that would otherwise reflect off the door, glass, or metal roof, reducing the ringing reverberation from the ceiling. Each fix works by adding soft, porous surface area that lets the sound wave enter and converts its energy to heat instead of reflecting it back into the room.

## Safety Notes
- Clapping blocks or stones can pinch fingers. Show the correct grip and keep fingers clear of the striking faces.
- Hanging blankets or mats on walls requires standing on a chair or desk. Use a stable chair, not a wobbly desk; have a partner hold it. Do not let young learners climb high.
- If using a grass-thatch room, check for wasps or bees before entering; thatch can host nests. Send a teacher in first.
- Do not block exits or doorways with hung materials; keep fire escape routes clear.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that sound reflects (echoes, Lesson 343) and that sound carries energy (Lesson 339). This lesson contrasts reflection with absorption.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the clap test, listing hard and soft surfaces, and proposing simple fixes. Ages 14–17 work the energy-absorption reasoning and the reverberation and absorption-fraction calculations in the Advanced Questions.
- Local resource tips: sisal mats (mikeka) are common and cheap in markets; old blankets and mattresses are found in most homes. Grass thatch is the classic rural absorber; a thatched room is a ready-made comparison space if the school has one. If no soft room is available, hang blankets along one wall and lay mats on the floor to create one in a corner.
- The key teaching point is energy conversion: absorption is not magic blocking; the sound wave enters the soft material and its energy becomes heat (a very small amount of heat — never measurable by hand, but real). This keeps the physics honest.
- A very common local problem is the mabati-roof classroom, which rings badly. Fixing the ceiling (cloth or grass under the roof) often helps more than fixing the walls. Mention this when discussing fixes.
- This is the last lesson of the unit's sound-absorption thread. Connect to real architecture: mosques, churches, and recording rooms use soft panels for the same reason the thatch room is quiet.
