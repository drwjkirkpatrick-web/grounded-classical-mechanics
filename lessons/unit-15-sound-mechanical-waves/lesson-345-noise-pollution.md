# Lesson 345: Noise Pollution in Our Town

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; G10-PHY-4.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** noise-map

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Identify loud and quiet zones around the school and nearby market using ear judgement and (optionally) a phone sound app.
2. Describe at least three health effects of long exposure to loud noise (hearing loss, stress, sleep loss, raised blood pressure).
3. Plot a simple noise map of a real route and mark danger zones on it.
4. Propose one realistic community action that lowers noise in a danger zone and explain the physics behind it.

## Materials
- A hand-drawn or printed sketch map of the area around the school (one per group of four)
- Clipboards or stiff card to lean on (jua kali hardboard offcuts work)
- Pencils and coloured pencils (red for loud, green for quiet)
- A smartphone with a free decibel-meter app (optional — one phone per group; if none, use ear judgement and a 1–5 loudness scale)
- Watch or phone clock for timing the walk

## Vocabulary
- **noise pollution** (uchafu wa sauti) — unwanted or harmful sound that disturbs people or wildlife
- **decibel** (desibeli) — the unit of sound level; a bigger number means louder sound
- **exposure** (kufichwa) — how long and how often a person hears a loud sound
- **hearing loss** (kupoteza usikivu) — permanent damage to the ear from very loud or long sound
- **buffer** (kinga) — something placed between a noise source and people to block or soak up sound

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Name one place near your home or school that is always very loud. What makes the sound?
2. How do your ears feel after standing next to a loud matatu horn or a posho mill for a long time?
3. If a sound is too loud, what is one thing a person can do to protect their ears?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Accept any real local source: matatu stage, posho mill, jua kali welding shed, generator, busy market, boda bodas, a nightclub. The key is naming a real place and the source of the sound.
2. Accept: ringing in the ears, pain, dullness (sounds seem muffled), headache, feeling tired or stressed. Any honest observation of the effect of loud sound.
3. Accept any one: move away from the source, cover the ears, wear earplugs or cotton wool, turn the volume down, limit the time spent near the loud sound.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect before going outside.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — clap once loudly, then whisper. Ask: *which one would you rather hear all day? Why?* Then tell the story: a WHO report estimates over 1 billion young people risk hearing loss from loud sound. Board the word **noise** and ask learners to name three loud spots in their town. Do not explain health effects yet.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups of four take their map, clipboard, and pencil and walk a short planned route (school gate, along the road, past the market or matatu stage, and back — stay on the footpath, teacher leads). At each stop they mark a dot and write the loudness: either the decibel reading from a phone app or a 1 (whisper-quiet) to 5 (painful) ear judgement, plus the source (engine, music, crowd, hammering). Back in class, each group colours loud spots red and quiet spots green. **Checkpoint:** every group has at least five marked points on the route before colouring.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Sound level is measured in decibels (dB). A whisper is about 30 dB, normal talk 60 dB, a matatu horn or posho mill about 90–100 dB, and anything above 85 dB for many hours can damage hearing. Three big health effects: (1) hearing loss — the tiny hair cells in the ear break and do not grow back; (2) stress and raised blood pressure — the body stays alert as if a threat is near; (3) sleep loss and poor learning — a noisy classroom or home makes concentration hard. Confront the misconception that "your ears get used to loud sound so it is safe." They do adapt (seem less loud) but the damage still happens — adaptation is not protection.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *loud sound is not just annoying — it is a health hazard, and distance and barriers are our best defence*. Homework: at home tonight, find the loudest and quietest minute of your evening. Write down the source and a 1–5 loudness for each. Bring it next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Community Noise Map
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; the teacher scouts the route and sketches the base map the day before.*

### Parts
- Base map of the school surroundings — 1 per group — hand-drawn or printed; mark the route with a dotted line
- Clipboard or hardboard offcut — 1 per group — jua kali scrap, for writing while standing
- Coloured pencils — red and green — to mark loud and quiet zones
- Phone with decibel app — optional, 1 per group — if none, use the 1–5 ear scale
- Watch or phone clock — to time stops and the walk

### Workflow
1. In class, mark your group's starting point and the dotted route on the base map. **Checkpoint:** everyone can point to where the route goes before leaving the room.
2. Walk the route with the teacher. At each chosen stop, stand still for 10 seconds and listen.
3. Mark a dot on the map at that spot. Beside it write: the loudness (dB reading or 1–5 scale) and the source (e.g. "matatu engine", "market crowd", "hammering").
4. Visit at least five stops along the route, including one you expect to be quiet (e.g. a side lane or the school garden).
5. Back in class, colour every dot: red for loud (4–5 on the scale, or above 75 dB), green for quiet (1–2, or below 55 dB), yellow for in-between. **Checkpoint:** the map shows a clear pattern of where sound is dangerous and where it is safe.
6. On the back, write one sentence proposing a fix for the worst red zone (move the source, add a wall or trees, limit the hours, wear protection).

### Common Mistakes
- **Measuring while walking** — footsteps and wind corrupt the reading; stand still for 10 seconds at each stop.
- **Only visiting loud spots** — a noise map needs quiet zones too, for comparison; include at least one.
- **Holding the phone near your own voice** — keep the phone at arm's length and do not talk while reading it; your breath and speech add decibels.
- **Forgetting the source** — a number without a source is useless for a noise plan; always write what made the sound.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. Name one place near your school that is always loud. What makes the sound? (mirrors pre-test)
2. How do your ears feel after a long time near a very loud sound, and what does that tell you? (mirrors pre-test)
3. On your noise map, what colour did you use for a spot above 85 dB, and why is that level the danger mark?
4. Give one health effect of living near a loud sound for many years, and explain in one sentence how it happens.
5. Challenge: your map shows a red zone at the matatu stage next to a classroom block. Propose one fix that uses a **buffer**, and say how it reduces the sound reaching the learners.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Accept any real local loud place with its source named (matatu stage/engines, posho mill, welding shed, market crowd, generator, boda bodas).
2. Ringing, pain, muffled hearing, headache, or tiredness. This tells us loud sound stresses and can damage the ear — the feeling is a warning, not something to ignore.
3. Red. 85 dB is the level above which long exposure begins to damage the tiny hair cells in the inner ear, so it marks the boundary between safe and harmful.
4. Accept one with a reason: hearing loss (hair cells break and do not regrow); stress/high blood pressure (body stays in alert mode); sleep loss (the brain cannot rest, so learning suffers). The reason must be stated.
5. Accept any buffer fix with the physics: plant a row of trees or build a mabati/earth wall between the stage and the classroom. The barrier blocks the direct sound path (absorbs or reflects it), so less sound energy reaches the learners; trees also scatter the sound. Moving the stage further away works too — doubling distance drops the level by about 6 dB in open air.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** In open air, sound level drops by about 6 dB each time the distance from the source doubles. A loud speaker at a matatu stage measures 100 dB at 1 m. How far away must a learner stand for the level to fall to 70 dB?
   **Worked Answer:** Each doubling of distance drops the level by 6 dB. We need a drop of 100 − 70 = 30 dB. Number of doublings = 30 / 6 = 5. Distance = 1 m × 2⁵ = 1 × 32 = 32 m. So the learner must stand about 32 m from the speaker for the level to reach 70 dB. (Note: 70 dB is still loud — above the 85 dB long-exposure limit applies to hours of exposure; 70 dB is safe for normal time periods but still clearly audible.)
2. **Q:** A posho mill runs at 95 dB. A worker stands beside it for 6 hours a day. A wall reduces the level at the worker's position to 75 dB. By what factor has the sound **energy** reaching the worker's ears been reduced? (Recall: every 10 dB is a factor of 10 in energy.)
   **Worked Answer:** The drop is 95 − 75 = 20 dB. Each 10 dB is a factor of 10 in sound energy, so 20 dB is 10 × 10 = 100. The wall reduces the sound energy reaching the worker's ears by a factor of 100 (to 1/100 of the original). This is why a barrier is far more effective than "getting used to it."
3. **Q:** A learner claims that because their ears "feel fine" after a loud matatu ride, no damage has occurred. Using the ideas of exposure and delayed hearing loss, argue why this claim is wrong.
   **Worked Answer:** Hearing loss from noise is often slow and cumulative, not felt at once. The hair cells in the inner ear bend under loud sound; some recover, but with repeated exposure they break permanently and do not regrow. The ear also adapts — after a few minutes loud sound seems less loud (temporary threshold shift), which hides the damage. So "feeling fine" right after a loud event does not prove safety. Damage shows up years later as difficulty hearing soft speech or ringing in the ears (tinnitus). The correct test is limiting exposure time and wearing protection, not trusting how the ear feels.

## Safety Notes
- The noise walk stays on the footpath; do not step onto the road. The teacher leads and the group crosses roads together at marked points.
- Do not hold phones or clipboards where passing matatus or boda bodas can snatch them; keep them close to the body.
- Do not stand closer than 1 m to a posho mill, generator, or welding shed for more than a few seconds — the sound level can exceed 100 dB and damage hearing. A quick reading at a safe distance is enough.
- If anyone feels ear pain or ringing during the walk, step away from the source immediately and cover the ears.
- In hot sun, carry water and keep the walk under 15 minutes to avoid heat exposure.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that sound is a wave and that loudness relates to amplitude, from Lessons 342 (Loudness and the Decibel Idea) and 331 (Waves Carry Energy). This lesson applies that to a real community health and climate-stress issue (G10-PHY-4.1 links noise and environmental quality).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the 1–5 ear scale, colouring the map, and naming one health effect and one fix. Ages 14–17 take real decibel readings, do the advanced calculations (6 dB per doubling, 10 dB per factor of 10), and write a one-paragraph community noise plan for the worst red zone.
- If no phone decibel app is available, the 1–5 ear scale works well — the lesson is about locating and comparing zones, not precise measurement. Consistency within a group matters more than absolute numbers.
- Local resource tips: base maps can be traced from a phone map screenshot or hand-drawn from memory — the route only needs to be recognisable. Clipboards can be hardboard offcuts from a jua kali furniture maker. The local public health officer (afisa wa afya) may have a real sound-level meter and can be invited to verify the class findings.
- The community-action angle is the heart of this lesson. Encourage fixes that are realistic for the area: asking the matatu stage to move, planting a hedge, limiting generator hours, or simply protecting ears with cotton wool or earplugs. Avoid fixes that need money the school does not have.
- CBE assessment note: the noise map is evidence for the "environmental stewardship" competency under G10-PHY-4.1. Photograph the maps and keep them for the learner portfolio.
