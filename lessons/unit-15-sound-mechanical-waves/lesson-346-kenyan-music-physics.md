# Lesson 346: The Physics of Kenyan Instruments

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Name at least five traditional Kenyan instruments and identify the part that vibrates to make the sound.
2. Classify instruments as string, membrane (drum), air-column, or idiophone (the whole body shakes).
3. Describe how each instrument controls pitch — by length, tension, or air-column length — using the wave ideas from this unit.
4. Write a short "physics profile" of one Kenyan instrument covering its vibrator, resonator, pitch control, and amplification.

## Materials
- Picture cards or printed photos of Kenyan instruments (ngoma drum, kayamba shaker, orutu, litungu, flute/peke, isukuti) — one set per group, or projected
- A notebook and pencil for each learner
- If available, one real instrument brought by a learner or a guest player (ngoma, orutu, or any local instrument)
- A short video or audio clip of each instrument playing (optional, on the teacher's phone)
- The board for the class survey table

## Vocabulary
- **vibrator** (kitu kinachotetema) — the part of an instrument that moves back and forth to make the sound
- **resonator** (kikarango) — the hollow body that makes the sound louder by adding its own vibrations
- **pitch** (sauti ya juu au chini) — how high or low a note is, set by how fast the vibrator moves
- **membrane** (membrane) — the stretched skin of a drum that vibrates when struck
- **idiophone** (idiofoni) — an instrument whose whole body vibrates, like a shaker or a struck piece of wood
- **tension** (mvutano) — how tightly a string or skin is pulled; tighter means higher pitch

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. When you hit a drum (ngoma), what part of the drum is actually moving to make the sound?
2. If you tighten a guitar string, does the note get higher or lower? Why do you think so?
3. Name one Kenyan instrument you have seen or heard, and say whether it is plucked, blown, struck, or shaken.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The stretched skin (membrane) on top of the drum vibrates when struck. The air inside the hollow body also vibrates and makes the sound louder.
2. Higher. Tightening a string increases the tension, which makes the string vibrate faster — and faster vibration means higher pitch. (Accept "higher" with any sensible reason.)
3. Accept any real instrument with a sensible action: ngoma/isisukuti (struck), orutu/litungu/nyatiti (plucked or bowed), flute/peke (blown), kayamba (shaken), njuga/leg bells (shaken/stamped).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — play one short clip (or, if a guest or learner can play, 30 seconds of a real ngoma or orutu). Ask: *what is moving to make that sound?* Take guesses. Do not correct yet — board every guess. Then reveal: every instrument has a **vibrator** (the thing that moves) and a **resonator** (the body that makes it loud).
3. **Explore (15 min)** — groups of four get a set of instrument cards (or share the projected pictures). For each instrument they fill a row in their notebook: Name | Vibrating part | How pitch is changed | Class (string/membrane/air/idiophone). The teacher moves between groups and asks: *if you made that string longer, what would happen to the pitch?* If a guest player is present, they demonstrate pitch change on the real instrument (tighten a string, cover a flute hole, strike the drum centre vs edge). **Checkpoint:** every group has completed the row for at least four instruments before the class table.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — build the class survey table on the board together, one row per instrument. Four families: **String** (orutu, litungu, nyatiti) — a stretched string vibrates; shorter or tighter string = higher pitch (faster vibration). **Membrane** (ngoma, isukuti) — a stretched skin vibrates; tighter skin or smaller drum = higher pitch. **Air column** (flute, peke, horn) — the air inside a tube vibrates; shorter tube or open holes = shorter air column = higher pitch. **Idiophone** (kayamba, njuga, clappers) — the whole solid body vibrates; pitch depends on the material's size and stiffness. Confront the misconception that "bigger instruments are always louder." Bigger usually means **lower** pitch (longer string, bigger drum, longer tube) — loudness comes from how hard you play and how well the body resonates, not size alone.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *every instrument is a wave machine — something vibrates, a body makes it loud, and length or tension sets the pitch*. Homework: pick one Kenyan instrument (from the cards or one you know from home) and write its full physics profile — vibrator, resonator, pitch control, how it is amplified — in four short lines. Bring it next lesson for the orchestra project.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. What part of a drum vibrates to make the sound? (mirrors pre-test)
2. If you tighten a guitar string, what happens to the pitch, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Classify an orutu (bow fiddle) into the correct instrument family and name its vibrating part.
4. A long flute and a short flute are played the same way. Which one gives the higher pitch, and what does this tell you about the air column inside?
5. Challenge: write the physics profile of one Kenyan instrument in four lines — vibrator, resonator, pitch control, amplification.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The stretched skin (membrane) on top; the air inside the hollow body also vibrates and adds loudness.
2. The pitch goes higher. Tightening increases the tension, so the string vibrates faster, and faster vibration means higher pitch.
3. String family. The vibrating part is the string (usually one or two strings bowed with a stick), and the hollow wooden body acts as the resonator.
4. The short flute gives the higher pitch. A shorter tube means a shorter air column, which vibrates faster — and faster vibration means higher pitch. (A longer air column vibrates more slowly, giving a lower note.)
5. Example profile (accept any real instrument with the four parts correct): **Ngoma** — vibrator: the stretched cowhide membrane; resonator: the hollow wooden body; pitch control: tighten the membrane (higher) or use a smaller drum (higher); amplification: the hollow body pushes the vibrating air out to the listener.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** An orutu has a single string of length 0.40 m. The player presses the string against the neck so only 0.30 m is free to vibrate. If the original note was 220 Hz (an A), find the new frequency, assuming tension and mass per length stay the same. (For a string, f is inversely proportional to length.)
   **Worked Answer:** For a vibrating string, f ∝ 1/L when tension and thickness are fixed. So f₂/f₁ = L₁/L₂ = 0.40/0.30 = 1.33. f₂ = 220 × 1.33 = 293 Hz. The new note is about 293 Hz (close to a D), higher than the original 220 Hz — shortening the string raises the pitch, as expected.
2. **Q:** A ngoma drum has a membrane diameter of 0.50 m. The drummer tightens the laces so the tension in the skin increases by 20%. By what factor does the fundamental frequency of the membrane change? (For a membrane, f ∝ √(T/m), where T is tension and m is mass per area.)
   **Worked Answer:** f ∝ √T, so the factor is √(new T / old T) = √1.20 = 1.095. The frequency rises by about 9.5% — a small but audible sharpening of the pitch. This is why a drummer retunes by pulling the laces: a 20% tension increase gives roughly a semitone sharpen.
3. **Q:** Write the full physics profile of a litungu (the eight-string lyre of the Abaluhya): name the vibrator, the resonator, how each string controls a different pitch, and how the sound reaches a listener 10 m away.
   **Worked Answer:** **Vibrator:** eight strings of different length and tension, each one a standing wave when plucked. **Resonator:** the hollowed-out wooden bowl with a skin face; the bowl's air and skin vibrate in sympathy with the strings, making the sound far louder than the thin strings alone. **Pitch control:** each string is tuned to a different pitch — shorter and/or tighter strings give higher notes, longer and/or looser strings give lower notes, giving the player a scale. **Amplification to a listener:** the resonator pushes the vibrating air outward as a spherical sound wave; the wave carries energy (not matter) to the listener 10 m away, spreading over a sphere so the level drops with distance, but the resonator's extra loudness keeps the notes audible.

## Safety Notes
- This is a survey and listening lesson — no build, so the main risk is handling a borrowed real instrument. Treat any guest's instrument with respect; do not touch strings, skins, or tuning pegs without the owner's permission.
- If a guest plays a drum or horn, keep fingers away from the striking or blowing area.
- Do not play audio clips at full phone volume near anyone's ears — keep the phone at arm's length.
- If instruments are borrowed, return them in the same condition; carry drums by the body, not by laces or straps that can stretch.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners should know that sound is a vibration (Lesson 329), that pitch is set by frequency (Lesson 332), and that a resonator makes a sound louder (Lesson 339, Singing Tubes and Bottles). This lesson surveys real Kenyan instruments through that physics lens.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on naming the vibrating part and the instrument family (string, drum, air, shaker) and on the idea that shorter or tighter means higher pitch. Ages 14–17 write the full physics profile and do the quantitative string-length and membrane-tension calculations.
- Local resource tips: instrument pictures can be printed from the web or drawn. Far better is a guest — a local player of ngoma, orutu, litungu, or a church choir drummer. Ask the local cultural association or a music teacher at a nearby school. A real 30-second demonstration beats any picture.
- The instrument families map directly to the unit's builds: bottle xylophone and one-string (string family), pan pipes (air column), ngoma and drums (membrane), and the kayamba or shaker idea (idiophone). Point this out so learners see the orchestra project (Lesson 347) as the performance of the same physics.
- If a guest is unavailable, the teacher can demonstrate pitch change on any available string (a rubber band stretched over a box) and any air column (blow across a bottle top — shorter water column = higher pitch).
- CBE assessment note: the homework physics profile is evidence for the "cultural heritage and applied science" competency (UP-FE). Collect the profiles; they become the program notes for the orchestra project.
