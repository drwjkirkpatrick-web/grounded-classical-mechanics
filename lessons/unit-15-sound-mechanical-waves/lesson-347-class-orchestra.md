# Lesson 347: Project: The Class Orchestra

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1; UP-FE  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** orchestra

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Assemble and tune the unit's instrument builds — bottle xylophone, one-string guitar, pan pipes, and drum — into a working ensemble.
2. Compose and perform a short group piece that uses at least two instrument families.
3. Explain in program notes how each instrument section makes its pitch (length, tension, or air-column length) using the wave vocabulary of the unit.
4. Cooperate as a section within an orchestra — listen, keep time, and follow a simple conductor's signal.

## Materials
- The bottle xylophones built in Lessons 334–335 (bottles, water, sticks)
- The one-string guitars built in Lessons 336–337 (box, string, tuning peg or stick)
- The pan pipes built in Lesson 340 (straws or bamboo cut to length, taped)
- A drum (ngoma) or any hand drum from Lesson 338 (or a debe and cloth skin)
- The string telephones from Lesson 341 (optional, for a "relay" sound effect)
- A simple conductor's beat (a pencil to tap, or a hand clap)
- Paper for program notes (one per section)

## Vocabulary
- **ensemble** (kikundi cha muziki) — a group of instruments playing together
- **section** (sehemu) — all the instruments of one family playing as a unit (strings, drums, pipes)
- **program notes** (maelezo ya programu) — the written explanation of the music and its physics, given to the audience
- **tempo** (mbio za muziki) — the speed of the beat
- **harmony** (sauti zinazokubaliana) — two or more different pitches sounded at the same time
- **resonator** (kikarango) — the body that makes each instrument's sound louder

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Name the four instrument families you have built in this unit (two string instruments, one air-column, one drum).
2. On a bottle xylophone, how do you make a higher note — add water or remove water? Why?
3. In an orchestra, why do the players need to listen to each other and follow one beat?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Bottle xylophone (struck bottles/air-water), one-string guitar (plucked string), pan pipes (blown air columns), drum/ngoma (struck membrane). Accept any order; the key is naming four distinct families.
2. Remove water. Less water means less mass in the bottle and a shorter effective air column, so the bottle vibrates faster and the pitch is higher. (Adding water lowers the pitch.) Accept "remove water" with the link to faster vibration.
3. So they stay together in time (the same tempo) and the notes sound as one piece, not a jumble. One beat keeps everyone aligned.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — the teacher taps a steady four-beat rhythm on a desk and asks the class to clap it back. Then split the class: half claps the beat, half claps twice as fast. The result is a simple rhythm — this is what an orchestra does. Board the word **ensemble** and the four sections: Bottles, Strings, Pipes, Drums.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — form four sections (Bottles, Strings, Pipes, Drums), each with its unit build. Each section has 2 minutes to tune and play a short riff (3–4 notes). Then the whole class learns one simple structure: Drums set a 4-beat pulse, Bottles play a low-high-low-high pattern on beats 1 and 3, Strings play a single open note on beats 2 and 4, Pipes add a short high note on beat 4 of every second bar. The teacher (or a learner conductor) taps the beat with a pencil. **Checkpoint:** every section can play its part alone, in time, before joining the full ensemble.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — stop and consolidate on the board: each section makes pitch a different way. **Bottles** — more water = slower vibration = lower pitch (mass of water-plus-glass controls the frequency). **Strings** — shorter or tighter string = higher pitch (tension and length control frequency). **Pipes** — shorter tube = shorter air column = higher pitch (air-column length controls frequency). **Drums** — tighter or smaller skin = higher pitch (tension and size of the membrane control frequency). Confront the misconception that "all the instruments work the same way." They share the wave idea (vibration → frequency → pitch) but the **control variable** differs — water level, string length, tube length, skin tension. That is why each section needs its own tuning.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *an orchestra is applied wave physics — each family controls pitch its own way, and one beat turns separate notes into music*. Homework: write the program notes for your section (2–3 sentences) explaining the pitch physics of your instrument, ready for the Sound Fair next lesson.

## Hands-On Build: Class Orchestra Performance
*Build time fits inside the Explore block; the instruments were built in earlier unit lessons — this lesson assembles and performs with them.*

### Parts
- Bottle xylophone — from Lessons 334–335 — 4–6 bottles tuned to different water levels, struck with a stick
- One-string guitar — from Lessons 336–337 — box, one string, tuning peg or sliding stick
- Pan pipes — from Lesson 340 — 4–6 straws or bamboo tubes of graded length, taped together
- Drum or ngoma — from Lesson 338 — a debe drum, a clay pot drum, or any hand drum
- Conductor's pencil or stick — 1 — to tap the visible beat
- Paper for program notes — 1 sheet per section

### Workflow
1. Assign each learner to one of the four sections. Each section collects its instrument build and tests it for 2 minutes. **Checkpoint:** every section can produce at least two clear different pitches.
2. Each section invents a short riff of 3–4 notes and plays it alone in time with a clapped beat.
3. The teacher sets a slow 4-beat pulse. Add sections one at a time: drums first, then bottles, then strings, then pipes. Listen after each addition. **Checkpoint:** the ensemble sounds like one piece, not a noise.
4. Run the piece twice. On the second run, the conductor (teacher or a learner) slows the tempo and then speeds it up — notice how the whole group must follow.
5. Each section writes 2–3 sentences of program notes: name the instrument, the vibrating part, and how pitch is changed.
6. If time allows, perform once for a neighbouring class or record 30 seconds on a phone. **Checkpoint:** the performance is in time and the program notes explain the physics.

### Common Mistakes
- **Sections starting at different tempos** — the piece falls apart; the conductor must give a clear "1-2-ready-play" count-in before every run.
- **Bottles out of tune** — water evaporates or gets spilled; check water levels and re-tune before the performance.
- **String section too loud or too soft** — the one-string is quiet; pluck firmly but not wildly, and let the box resonator do the work.
- **Pipes blown too hard** — over-blowing makes the note jump to a higher harmonic, not the intended pitch; blow a steady gentle breath across the top.
- **Drum drowning out everyone** — a drum is the loudest family; the drummer must play softly enough that the bottles and strings are still heard.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. Name the four instrument families built in this unit. (mirrors pre-test)
2. On a bottle xylophone, how do you raise the pitch — add or remove water? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Why does the one-string guitar and the bottle xylophone need different tuning methods? Name the control variable for each.
4. During the performance, what keeps the four sections playing together as one piece?
5. Challenge: your pipes section plays a note that is too low for the piece. Describe two different ways to raise the pitch using the physics of an air column.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Bottle xylophone (struck bottles), one-string guitar (plucked string), pan pipes (blown air columns), drum/ngoma (struck membrane).
2. Remove water. Less water means less mass in the bottle-and-water system, so it vibrates faster, and faster vibration means higher pitch.
3. They use different control variables. The one-string guitar controls pitch by string length and tension (shorten or tighten for higher). The bottle xylophone controls pitch by water level (less water for higher). So each needs its own tuning action.
4. A shared beat (tempo) set by the conductor and kept by the drum section. Everyone listens and follows the same pulse, so the notes line up in time.
5. Two ways, both using air-column length: (1) cut or block the pipe to make the air column shorter — shorter column = faster vibration = higher pitch; (2) open a side hole (like a flute) to shorten the effective vibrating air column, also raising the pitch. Blowing harder is not a correct answer — that changes loudness or jumps to a harmonic, not the fundamental pitch.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A bottle in the xylophone holds 300 mL of water and rings at 400 Hz. The players pour out water so it holds 150 mL, halving the mass of the bottle-plus-water system. Assuming frequency is inversely proportional to the square root of the vibrating mass, find the new frequency.
   **Worked Answer:** f ∝ 1/√m, so f₂/f₁ = √(m₁/m₂) = √(300/150) = √2 = 1.41. f₂ = 400 × 1.41 = 564 Hz. The pitch rises to about 564 Hz — roughly a tritone above the original 400 Hz, as expected when mass is halved.
2. **Q:** A pan pipe has a tube length of 0.15 m and sounds at about 570 Hz (open-end air column, v ≈ 340 m/s). The section needs a note one octave higher, about 1140 Hz. What tube length is required? (For an open pipe, f = v/2L.)
   **Worked Answer:** f = v/2L, so L = v/(2f) = 340/(2 × 1140) = 340/2280 = 0.149 m ≈ 0.075 m = 7.5 cm. The new pipe must be about 7.5 cm — half the original 15 cm. This confirms the rule: halving the length raises an open pipe one octave.
3. **Q:** Write the program notes for the string section of the class orchestra. Explain (in 4–5 sentences) the vibrator, the resonator, how the player changes pitch, and how the sound reaches the audience, using correct wave vocabulary.
   **Worked Answer:** The one-string guitar's **vibrator** is the stretched string, which forms a standing transverse wave when plucked. Its **resonator** is the hollow wooden box under the string; the box and its trapped air vibrate in sympathy, amplifying the thin string's sound many times over. The player changes **pitch** by pressing the string against the neck to shorten the free length, or by turning the tuning peg to change the tension — shorter or tighter means higher frequency and higher pitch (f ∝ (1/L)√(T/m)). The amplified sound leaves the box as a **longitudinal wave in air**, carrying energy (not matter) outward to the audience, spreading over a sphere so its level falls with distance. The four sections blend because they share one tempo while each family controls pitch through its own physical variable — length, tension, water mass, or membrane size.

## Safety Notes
- Broken bottles are a cut hazard — inspect all xylophone bottles for cracks before the performance; handle them by the body, not the neck, and sweep up any glass immediately.
- Striking bottles too hard can crack them — use a light wooden or plastic stick, not a stone or metal.
- Pan-pipe straws or bamboo splinters can scratch the mouth; smooth the cut ends with a knife or sandpaper before playing.
- The drum can be very loud — keep it at a playing distance, not pressed to anyone's ear; give the drummer a rest between runs.
- Keep the performance space clear of bags and chairs so learners can move between sections safely.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners must have completed the unit's instrument builds — bottle xylophone (Lessons 334–335), one-string guitar (336–337), pan pipes (340), and drum (338). If a build is missing, a simple substitute (rubber band over a box, a row of bottles with any water levels) still lets the lesson run.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on playing their section in time and naming the vibrating part. Ages 14–17 write the program notes with correct wave vocabulary and do the advanced pitch calculations for their own instrument.
- The performance is the assessment. Record 30 seconds on a phone if possible — it is powerful evidence for the learner portfolio and for the Sound Fair (Lesson 348).
- Local resource tips: if some builds are incomplete, jua kali substitutes work — a tin and string for the one-string, any graded bottles for the xylophone, a sufuria as a drum. The point is the ensemble and the physics, not perfect instruments.
- The conductor role can rotate so more learners try leading the beat. This builds the "collaboration" CBE competency.
- CBE assessment note: the program notes and the recording are evidence for the "creative arts and applied science" competency (UP-FE) and for the wave-properties strand (G10-PHY-2.1). Keep the program notes for the Sound Fair display.
