# Lesson 348: Unit Review: Sound Fair

**Unit:** 15 — Sound & Mechanical Waves  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Demonstrate mastery of the unit's core ideas — sound is a vibration, waves carry energy not matter, v = fλ, pitch and loudness, resonance, and the decibel idea — at three review stations.
2. Link the wave equation f = 1/L (or its string and air-column forms) to a real instrument and explain the pitch physics in their own words.
3. Write one well-formed quiz question with a correct mark scheme that tests a unit concept.
4. Self-assess their own growth from the pre-test to the post-test across the unit.

## Materials
- All the unit's instrument builds, set out on tables (bottle xylophone, one-string, pan pipes, drum, string telephone) — the "instrument petting zoo"
- A long wall or corridor for the echo game (from Lesson 343)
- Two string telephones and a 10 m string course for the telephone race (from Lesson 341)
- Blank paper and pencils for the quiz-writing task
- The unit's pre-test and post-test scores (teacher keeps these; learners see only their own)
- A simple score sheet for the three stations (one per learner)

## Vocabulary
- **review** (mapitio) — going back over the whole unit to fix the ideas firmly
- **petting zoo** (bustani ya viombo) — a station where learners handle and play each instrument again
- **echo** (sauti inayorudi) — sound reflecting back from a wall
- **mark scheme** (mfumo wa alama) — the written correct answers and how marks are awarded
- **self-assessment** (kujipima) — a learner judging their own growth against the unit outcomes

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. In one sentence, what is sound?
2. Write the wave equation linking wave speed, frequency, and wavelength.
3. Name one instrument from this unit and say how it changes pitch.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Sound is a vibration (a longitudinal wave) that travels through a medium (air, water, solid) and is heard when it reaches the ear.
2. v = fλ (wave speed = frequency × wavelength). Accept the equation in any order or words that show the same relationship.
3. Accept any unit instrument with a correct pitch mechanism: bottle xylophone (change water level), one-string (change string length or tension), pan pipes (change tube length / air column), drum (change skin tension or drum size). The pitch mechanism must be stated.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips; tell learners this is the unit review and fair, not a new topic.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — stand at the instrument petting zoo and pluck one string, blow one pipe, strike one drum, and tap one bottle — 10 seconds each. Ask: *one word for what they all share?* Answer: **vibration**. This fair reviews the whole unit through three stations: petting zoo, echo game, telephone race. Board the three station names and the rules — every learner visits all three.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — run three stations in rotation (5 minutes each, teacher calls the change). **Station A — Instrument Petting Zoo:** learners handle each build, play two notes, and tell a partner the vibrating part and how pitch is changed for that instrument. **Station B — Echo Game:** at a long wall, one learner claps, the partner times the echo return with a watch or phone stopwatch; they estimate the wall distance using d = (v × t)/2 with v ≈ 340 m/s (from Lesson 343). **Station C — Telephone Race:** two teams send a whispered word down a string telephone; the first team to repeat the word correctly wins, then they explain why the string carries sound better than air (Lesson 341). **Checkpoint:** every learner has visited all three stations and has a score-sheet stamp or tick for each.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — regroup and consolidate the unit's one big idea on the board: **sound is a wave that carries energy (not matter), its pitch is set by frequency, its loudness by amplitude, and every instrument controls frequency through a length or tension.** Recap the wave equation v = fλ and the instrument rules: string (shorter/tighter = higher), air column (shorter = higher), membrane (tighter/smaller = higher), bottle (less water = higher). Confront the misconception that "loudness and pitch are the same thing." They are independent — you can play any note softly or loudly. Pitch is frequency; loudness is amplitude (energy).
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *sound is a wave, and every sound you make is applied wave physics*. Homework: write one quiz question that links f = 1/L (or the equivalent for a string or air column) to a real Kenyan instrument, with a full mark scheme. Bring it next lesson — the best questions feed the next unit's pre-tests.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. In one sentence, what is sound? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Write the wave equation linking wave speed, frequency, and wavelength. (mirrors pre-test)
3. At the echo station, a clap returns after 0.40 s. Using v ≈ 340 m/s, find the distance to the wall and back. (mirrors the echo-game station)
4. Name two ways to raise the pitch of a one-string guitar, and say the physics behind each.
5. Challenge: write a quiz question (with its mark scheme) that links f = 1/L to a real instrument. The question should be answerable by a learner in this class.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Sound is a vibration (a longitudinal wave) that travels through a medium and is heard when it reaches the ear.
2. v = fλ (wave speed = frequency × wavelength).
3. Distance = v × t = 340 × 0.40 = 136 m there and back, so the wall is 136/2 = 68 m away. (Award full marks for 136 m with the round trip noted, or 68 m with the halving shown.)
4. Two ways: (1) shorten the free string length (press it against the neck) — shorter length means faster vibration, so higher pitch; (2) increase the tension (turn the tuning peg) — tighter string vibrates faster, so higher pitch. The physics: for a string, f is inversely proportional to length and proportional to the square root of tension.
5. Example question and mark scheme (accept any equivalent): **Q:** A pan pipe tube is 0.20 m long and sounds 850 Hz (open pipe, v = 340 m/s, f = v/2L). The player wants a note one octave higher (1700 Hz). What length should the new tube be? **Mark scheme:** (1 mark) state f = v/2L; (1 mark) rearrange L = v/2f = 340/3400; (1 mark) L = 0.10 m; (1 mark) note this is half the original length, so one octave higher. Total 4 marks.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** At the echo station, a learner stands 51 m from a school wall and claps once. How long should the echo take to return, and what does this confirm about the speed of sound? (Use v ≈ 340 m/s.)
   **Worked Answer:** The sound travels to the wall and back, so total distance = 2 × 51 = 102 m. Time = distance/speed = 102/340 = 0.30 s. The echo returns after about 0.30 s, confirming v = 2d/t = 102/0.30 ≈ 340 m/s. This matches the accepted speed of sound in air at about 20 °C, validating the echo method used in Lesson 343.
2. **Q:** A one-string guitar has a string of length 0.60 m tuned to 110 Hz. The player presses the string against the neck so only 0.40 m is free. Find the new frequency, assuming tension and mass per length are unchanged. Then state whether the pitch rises or falls, and by what factor.
   **Worked Answer:** For a string with fixed tension and thickness, f ∝ 1/L, so f₂/f₁ = L₁/L₂ = 0.60/0.40 = 1.5. f₂ = 110 × 1.5 = 165 Hz. The pitch rises (shorter free length = faster vibration). The frequency increases by a factor of 1.5, which is close to a perfect fifth in music — a natural interval, which is why pressing a string at simple length ratios gives pleasing notes.
3. **Q:** Write a quiz question (with a full mark scheme) that links the idea f = 1/L to a real Kenyan instrument, and that a learner aged 10–13 in this class could answer. State the instrument, the question, and the mark scheme.
   **Worked Answer:** **Instrument:** bottle xylophone. Question: You have two identical bottles. Bottle A has 200 mL of water; Bottle B has 400 mL of water. You strike each with the same stick. Which bottle gives the higher pitch, and why? **Mark scheme:** (1 mark) Bottle A gives the higher pitch; (1 mark) Bottle A has less water, so the bottle-and-water system has less mass; (1 mark) less mass means the bottle vibrates faster; (1 mark) faster vibration means higher frequency and higher pitch. Total 4 marks. The question is answerable by a younger learner because it uses only comparison (more/less water) and the link between vibration speed and pitch, with no calculation.
4. **Q:** A pan pipe has its longest tube at 20 cm, sounding a note at 850 Hz (open pipe, v = 340 m/s, f = v/2L). Design the tube lengths for a three-note rising scale: the same note, a fifth above (f × 3/2), and an octave above (f × 2). Verify each length with the wave equation.
   **Worked Answer:** For an open pipe, f = v/2L, so L = v/2f. Note 1 (same): f₁ = 850 Hz, L₁ = 340/(2 × 850) = 340/1700 = 0.20 m = 20 cm. Note 2 (fifth, f × 3/2 = 1275 Hz): L₂ = 340/(2 × 1275) = 340/2550 = 0.133 m = 13.3 cm. Note 3 (octave, f × 2 = 1700 Hz): L₃ = 340/(2 × 1700) = 340/3400 = 0.10 m = 10 cm. Verify: f₁ = 340/(2 × 0.20) = 850 Hz ✓; f₂ = 340/(2 × 0.133) = 1278 Hz ≈ 1275 Hz ✓ (rounding); f₃ = 340/(2 × 0.10) = 1700 Hz ✓. The three tubes are 20 cm, 13.3 cm, and 10 cm — each shorter tube gives a higher note, confirming f ∝ 1/L for open pipes.

## Safety Notes
- This is a review and games lesson using already-built instruments — the main risk is handling them. Inspect bottles for cracks before the petting zoo; broken glass is a cut hazard, sweep up any immediately.
- The echo game uses a wall or corridor — keep the area clear of traffic and do not run towards the wall; a clap is enough, no shouting needed.
- The telephone race string is under tension — do not pull it to the face; keep it at waist height between two learners.
- Pan-pipe and straw ends can splinter — smooth cut ends before anyone plays them.
- Keep the room noise at a manageable level; the petting zoo can get loud — remind learners to play softly so everyone's hearing is protected.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson reviews the whole unit (Lessons 329–347). Learners should know sound is a vibration, v = fλ, pitch vs loudness, resonance, the decibel idea, and how each unit instrument controls pitch. No new content is introduced.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the petting zoo (naming the vibrating part and pitch control for each instrument) and the echo and telephone games as concrete review. Ages 14–17 do the quantitative echo and string-length calculations in the advanced questions and write the f = 1/L quiz question with a rigorous mark scheme.
- The three-station rotation keeps a 45-minute review active and prevents the common review failure of passively re-reading notes. Five minutes per station is tight — the teacher calls the change sharply.
- If the full instrument set is unavailable (some builds missing), the petting zoo can run with any subset; the echo and telephone stations need only a wall and a string, so they always work.
- The quiz-writing homework is the unit's final assessment artifact. The best questions become pre-test items for the next unit, closing the assessment loop. Collect and sort them by quality.
- CBE assessment note: the post-test here measures unit-level growth against G10-PHY-2.1. Compare each learner's score to their Lesson 329 pre-test and their first-day expectations — the growth is the evidence for the learner record. Photograph the petting zoo and the string of quiz questions for the portfolio.
