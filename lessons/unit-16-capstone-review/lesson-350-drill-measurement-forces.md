# Lesson 350: Core Drill I: Measurement and Forces

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Convert base SI units confidently — metres, kilograms, seconds, newtons — and move between prefixes (cm, mm, km, g, kN) in one step.
2. Draw a correct free-body force diagram for a real Kenyan object (a panga at rest, a jerrican on the head, a mkokoteni being pushed) and state whether the forces balance.
3. Apply the friction model (F_friction ≤ μR) to decide whether a load slides, and identify static vs. kinetic friction in a given case.
4. Peer-mark a partner's drill answers against a printed mark scheme and give one specific improvement.

## Materials
- A set of about 24 problem cards, four rounds of six cards, written before the lesson:
  - Round 1 (units and conversions): e.g. "Convert 250 cm to m", "How many g in 1.5 kg?"
  - Round 2 (force diagrams): e.g. "Draw the forces on a jerrican resting on the ground"
  - Round 3 (friction cases): e.g. "A wooden box on a floor needs 30 N to start sliding and 20 N to keep sliding. Which is static, which is kinetic?"
  - Round 4 (mixed one-step): combining the above
- A printed mark scheme per pair for peer marking (one scheme per round)
- A mini-whiteboard or the chalkboard for the teacher's worked examples
- Blank paper and a pencil per learner for working
- A stopwatch or the classroom clock for the round timing
- A simple score sheet per learner (four rounds, a mark per round)

## Vocabulary
- **SI unit** (kituo cha SI) — the internationally agreed base unit for a quantity, e.g. metre for length, newton for force
- **conversion** (ubadilishaji) — changing a quantity from one unit to another without changing its size
- **free-body diagram** (ramani ya nguvu) — a sketch of one body showing only the forces acting on it, as labelled arrows
- **static friction** (msuguano tuli) — the friction that must be overcome to start a body moving
- **kinetic friction** (msuguano wa mwendo) — the friction that acts on a body already moving, usually smaller than static friction
- **mark scheme** (mfumo wa alama) — the written correct answers and how the marks are awarded

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Convert 400 cm to metres.
2. A jerrican of water rests on the ground. Name the two forces acting on it and say whether they balance.
3. Which is usually larger, the force needed to start sliding a box or the force needed to keep it sliding at a steady speed?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. 400 cm = 400 ÷ 100 = 4 m.
2. Weight (acting downwards) and the normal reaction from the ground (acting upwards). Yes, they balance — the jerrican is at rest, so the forces are equal and opposite.
3. The force needed to start sliding (the static friction) is usually larger than the force needed to keep it sliding (the kinetic friction).

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect after five minutes. Tell learners this is a drill lesson: four speed rounds, each about one big idea from Units 1 and 2, with peer marking at the end of each round.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a panga and a jerrican. Ask: *what is the SI unit of the force my hand uses to lift this jerrican?* Take the answer (newton). Then: *and if I drag the panga along the table, what kind of friction acts once it is already moving?* Take the answer (kinetic). The hook: today we drill the four core skills of Units 1 and 2 — units, force diagrams, friction, and mixed one-step problems — at speed, and we mark each other.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — run four rounds, about three to four minutes each. The teacher reads one card per round to the whole class; learners work the answer on paper in 60 seconds; pairs swap and peer-mark against the printed scheme in 60 seconds; the teacher posts the worked answer on the board. Repeat for the other cards in the round if time allows, or move on. **Round 1 — Units and Conversions:** convert between cm/m/km and g/kg, and name the SI unit for length, mass, time, force. **Round 2 — Force Diagrams:** draw the free-body diagram for a named object (jerrican on the head, panga at rest, mkokoteni pushed). **Round 3 — Friction Cases:** identify static vs. kinetic friction, and state which is larger. **Round 4 — Mixed One-Step:** a short problem combining a conversion and a force or friction idea. **Checkpoint:** every learner has a score for all four rounds on their score sheet.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate the four skills on the board, one line each. **Units:** to convert cm to m, divide by 100; km to m, multiply by 1000; the SI unit of force is the newton (N), of mass the kilogram (kg). **Force diagrams:** every free-body diagram shows the body as a dot and only the forces on it as arrows — weight down, normal reaction up, applied force and friction along the surface. **Friction:** static friction adjusts up to a maximum (μ_s R) to prevent motion; once sliding, kinetic friction (μ_k R) acts and is usually smaller. Confront the misconception that *friction always opposes motion* — strictly, friction opposes *relative sliding*; for a walking person or a driven wheel, friction is what pushes you forward. Point to the mkokoteni card: the pusher's feet push back on the ground, and the ground pushes them forward — that forward force is friction.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark using the printed scheme.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *units, force diagrams, and friction are the three tools you reach for first in every physics problem — drill them until they are automatic.* Homework: write the two hardest fair questions you can from Units 1 and 2, each with a full mark scheme. "Fair" means a classmate could answer it from what was taught. Bring them next lesson — the best questions feed the next drill.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. Convert 2.5 km to metres. (mirrors pre-test)
2. A mkokoteni is pushed at a steady speed along a flat path. Name the horizontal forces on it and say whether they balance. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A wooden box on a floor needs 35 N to start sliding and 25 N to keep it sliding at a steady speed. State which value is the static friction and which is the kinetic friction.
4. Draw the free-body force diagram for a jerrican of water being lifted straight up at a steady speed by a hand. Label every force.
5. Challenge: A box of mass 10 kg rests on a floor. The coefficient of static friction is 0.4 and g = 10 N/kg. Calculate the minimum horizontal force needed to start the box sliding.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. 2.5 km = 2.5 × 1000 = 2500 m.
2. The applied push forward and the kinetic friction backward (along the ground). At a steady speed the forces balance — the push equals the friction, so there is no acceleration.
3. 35 N is the static friction (the force needed to start sliding); 25 N is the kinetic friction (the force needed to keep sliding at a steady speed).
4. Two vertical forces: weight (W = mg, acting downwards from the dot) and the applied lift (F, acting upwards from the dot). At a steady speed the two forces are equal in size, so the arrows should be drawn the same length, pointing in opposite directions. The body is drawn as a single dot; only forces on the jerrican are shown.
5. Weight W = mg = 10 × 10 = 100 N. On a flat floor the normal reaction R = W = 100 N. Minimum force to start sliding = maximum static friction = μ_s × R = 0.4 × 100 = 40 N.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A learner says "the friction on a sliding box is always equal to the applied force, so friction does no work." Using a box pushed at a steady 2 m/s by a 25 N force across 4 m, calculate the work done by the push and the work done against friction, and explain why the box does not gain kinetic energy.
   **Worked Answer:** Work done by the push = F × d = 25 × 4 = 100 J. At a steady speed the push equals the kinetic friction, so the friction force is also 25 N. Work done against friction = F_friction × d = 25 × 4 = 100 J. The two works are equal and opposite: the push adds 100 J and friction removes 100 J, so the net work on the box is zero. By the work-energy theorem, zero net work means zero change in kinetic energy — the box keeps the same speed. So friction does do work (100 J), but it converts that work into heat at the surfaces; it does not accelerate the box.
2. **Q:** A box of mass 5 kg is on a ramp. The coefficient of static friction between box and ramp is 0.3 and g = 10 N/kg. (a) Calculate the normal reaction if the ramp is horizontal. (b) Calculate the maximum static friction on the horizontal ramp. (c) The ramp is now tilted until the box just begins to slide. Explain in one sentence why the box begins to slide even though the coefficient of friction has not changed.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) On a horizontal ramp the normal reaction R equals the weight: R = mg = 5 × 10 = 50 N. (b) Maximum static friction = μ_s × R = 0.3 × 50 = 15 N. (c) When the ramp is tilted, the component of weight along the ramp (mg sinθ) grows as the angle increases, while the maximum static friction (μ_s R, with R = mg cosθ) actually shrinks; once the downslope weight component exceeds the maximum static friction, the box can no longer stay at rest and begins to slide.
3. **Q:** Write the two hardest fair questions you can from Units 1 and 2, each with a full mark scheme. "Fair" means a classmate could answer from what was taught. Give the question, the mark scheme (showing how each mark is awarded), and state which misconception each question targets.
   **Worked Answer:** Question 1: "A girl pushes a table at a steady speed across a floor, then lets go. The table slows and stops. During the slowing, name the force that acts in the direction of motion, and explain why the table stops." Mark scheme: (1 mark) the force in the direction of motion is kinetic friction; (1 mark) before release, push and friction balanced at steady speed; (1 mark) after release, only friction acts along the ground; (1 mark) friction now opposes the motion, doing negative work, so the table loses kinetic energy and stops. Target misconception: "friction always opposes motion" — learners must see that while the table is being pushed, friction opposes the push but the table still moves; the table stops only because friction becomes a net decelerating force. Question 2: "A force of 30 N acts horizontally on a 6 kg box on a floor with kinetic friction 12 N. Calculate the acceleration of the box (g = 10 N/kg) and state the direction of the kinetic friction." Mark scheme: (1 mark) net force = applied force − friction = 30 − 12 = 18 N; (1 mark) F = ma, so a = F/m = 18/6 = 3 m/s²; (1 mark) direction of acceleration is the direction of the applied force; (1 mark) kinetic friction acts opposite to the motion, i.e. opposite the applied force. Target misconception: "use the applied force directly in F = ma" — learners must subtract friction to get the net force before applying Newton's second law.

## Safety Notes
- This is a paper-and-pen drill with no equipment handled by learners — the main risk is eye strain from fast reading; remind learners to look up between rounds.
- The panga and jerrican used in the Engage hook are for the teacher to hold only; do not pass them around — a panga is sharp and a full jerrican is heavy.
- Peer marking can cause arguments; the printed mark scheme is final, and the teacher adjudicates any dispute.
- Keep the pace brisk but not frantic — speed should build confidence, not panic.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this drill revises Unit 1 (Measurement and Science Skills) and Unit 2 (Forces in Everyday Life). Learners should already know SI base units, free-body diagrams, weight vs. mass, and static vs. kinetic friction. The drill is for speed and confidence, not first teaching — if a round exposes a widespread gap, slow the round and re-teach at the board.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the units and conversions round and the simple force diagrams (jerrican at rest, panga on a table) — concrete objects, one-step conversions. Ages 14–17 take the friction model (μR), the work-against-friction calculation, and the two-hardest-questions challenge in the Advanced Questions.
- The biggest pitfall in Round 2 is learners drawing forces that do not act on the chosen body (e.g. the pusher's force on the ground on the jerrican's diagram). Insist on the rule: a free-body diagram shows only the forces on the one body, drawn as a dot.
- If problem cards run short, the teacher can read fresh one-liners from the board; the format does not depend on printed cards.
- Peer marking with the printed scheme is part of the learning — learners see the expected working for every round, not just their own. Collect the score sheets to find which skill was weakest across the class.
- CBE assessment note: the four rounds map to G10-PHY-1.1 (measurement and units) and JS-FE (forces and friction). A learner who can convert units, draw a balanced force diagram, and state which friction is larger is at mastery level for these two strands.
