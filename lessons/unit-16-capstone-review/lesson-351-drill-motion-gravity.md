# Lesson 351: Core Drill II: Motion and Gravity

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Read a distance–time and a velocity–time graph, and extract speed, velocity, and acceleration from the slopes and areas.
2. Distinguish speed (scalar) from velocity (vector), and calculate each from d/t and s/t with the correct units.
3. Solve free-fall and simple projectile problems using the constant-acceleration equations with g = 10 m/s².
4. Answer a speed-round question under timed game-show conditions and justify the answer in one sentence.

## Materials
- A set of about 20 graph cards: printed or hand-drawn distance–time and velocity–time graphs on A5 cards (a flat line, a straight sloping line, a curve, a triangle area, a trapezium) — enough for four game rounds
- A set of about 20 short problem cards covering speed, velocity, acceleration, free fall, and projectiles
- Two "buzzer" markers per team — a small stone, a bottle cap, or a slap of the desk; write the simple buzzer rules on the board: first to slap answers, wrong answer loses a point
- A stopwatch or the classroom clock for the round timing
- A printed answer card per round for the teacher (the teacher is the quizmaster)
- Blank paper and a pencil per learner for working
- A simple team score sheet on the board (four teams, four rounds)

## Vocabulary
- **speed** (kasi) — distance travelled per unit time, a scalar (no direction)
- **velocity** (kasi yenye mwelekeo) — displacement per unit time, a vector (has direction)
- **acceleration** (ongezeko la kasi) — the rate of change of velocity, in m/s²
- **free fall** (kuanguka bure) — motion under gravity alone, with acceleration g ≈ 10 m/s² downwards
- **projectile** (chombo kinachorushwa) — an object thrown into the air moving under gravity alone
- **slope** (mteremko) — the gradient of a graph line; on a distance–time graph it gives the speed

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A matatu travels 120 km in 2 hours. Calculate its average speed.
2. On a velocity–time graph, what does the area under the line represent?
3. A stone is dropped from rest. After one second, what is its speed? (Take g = 10 m/s².)

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Average speed = distance ÷ time = 120 ÷ 2 = 60 km/h.
2. The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement (distance travelled in a straight line). Accept "distance" for younger learners.
3. v = u + at = 0 + 10 × 1 = 10 m/s downwards.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect after five minutes. Tell learners this is a drill lesson in a game-show format: four rounds of speed-round questions on motion and gravity, teams keep score, the teacher is the quizmaster.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up one graph card showing a straight sloping distance–time line. Ask: *what is the speed of this object, and how do you read it from the graph?* Take one or two answers. The hook: today every question is a speed-round question — read the graph or compute the motion in under a minute, slap to answer, and defend it in one sentence.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — run four rounds, about three to four minutes each, in teams of four. **Round 1 — Graph Reading:** the quizmaster holds up a graph card; the first team to slap answers "what does the slope/area give?" Correct answer = 1 point, wrong answer = the next team gets a try. **Round 2 — Speed and Velocity:** short problem cards — "a cyclist covers 600 m in 120 s, find the speed"; "give the velocity of a runner going 5 m/s north." **Round 3 — Acceleration:** "a boda boda accelerates from 0 to 20 m/s in 5 s, find the acceleration." **Round 4 — Free Fall and Projectiles:** "a mango falls for 3 s, find the impact speed (g = 10 m/s²)." The teacher writes each worked answer on the board after the round. **Checkpoint:** every team has a score for all four rounds on the board sheet.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate the four skills on the board, one line each. **Graphs:** the slope of a distance–time graph is the speed; the slope of a velocity–time graph is the acceleration; the area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement. **Speed vs. velocity:** speed is a scalar (size only); velocity is a vector (size and direction) — a matatu going 60 km/h east and one going 60 km/h west have the same speed but opposite velocities. **Acceleration:** a = (v − u)/t, in m/s²; a negative acceleration is a deceleration. **Free fall and projectiles:** with g = 10 m/s² and no air resistance, a falling body gains 10 m/s every second; a body thrown straight up loses 10 m/s every second until it stops, then falls back. Confront the misconception that *a heavier object falls faster* — in free fall all objects accelerate at g regardless of mass; a stone and a dropped feather (in a vacuum) land together. Point to the mango question: its mass was never needed.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark using the printed scheme.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *motion is read from graphs and computed with three equations — master the slope, the area, and g = 10, and you can solve any fall.* Homework: a ball is thrown straight up at 12 m/s from a height of 2 m. Find the time to land and the impact speed (take g = 10 m/s²). Bring the worked solution next lesson.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. A cyclist travels 30 km in 1.5 hours. Calculate the average speed. (mirrors pre-test)
2. On a velocity–time graph, what does the area under the line represent? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 25 m/s in 10 s. Calculate the acceleration.
4. A stone is dropped from rest and falls for 3 s. Calculate its speed just before it hits the ground (take g = 10 m/s²).
5. Challenge: a ball is thrown straight up at 12 m/s from a height of 2 m above the ground. Find the time taken to reach the ground and the speed at impact (take g = 10 m/s²).

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Average speed = 30 ÷ 1.5 = 20 km/h.
2. The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement (distance in a straight line). Accept "distance" for younger learners.
3. a = (v − u)/t = (25 − 0)/10 = 2.5 m/s².
4. v = u + at = 0 + 10 × 3 = 30 m/s downwards.
5. Taking up as positive: u = +12 m/s, a = −10 m/s², s = −2 m (final position is 2 m below start). Using s = ut + ½at²: −2 = 12t − 5t², so 5t² − 12t − 2 = 0. t = [12 ± √(144 + 40)]/10 = [12 ± √184]/10 = [12 ± 13.56]/10. Taking the positive root: t = 25.56/10 ≈ 2.56 s. Impact speed: v = u + at = 12 − 10 × 2.56 = 12 − 25.6 = −13.6 m/s. The minus sign means the ball is moving downwards, so the impact speed is about 13.6 m/s downwards. (Accept 2.6 s and 13.6 m/s with working shown.)

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A ball is thrown vertically up at 12 m/s from a height of 2 m above the ground. Take g = 10 m/s², and ignore air resistance. (a) Find the time to reach the highest point. (b) Find the maximum height above the ground. (c) Find the total time to land and the impact speed.
   **Worked Answer:** Take up as positive. u = +12 m/s, a = −10 m/s², s₀ = 2 m above ground. (a) At the highest point v = 0. v = u + at ⇒ 0 = 12 − 10t ⇒ t = 1.2 s. (b) Maximum height above the start: v² = u² + 2as ⇒ 0 = 144 + 2(−10)s ⇒ s = 144/20 = 7.2 m. Maximum height above the ground = 7.2 + 2 = 9.2 m. (c) From the start to the ground, s = −2 m (the ball ends 2 m below the start). Using s = ut + ½at²: −2 = 12t − 5t² ⇒ 5t² − 12t − 2 = 0. t = [12 ± √(144 + 40)]/10 = [12 ± √184]/10 = [12 ± 13.56]/10. Taking the positive root: t ≈ 2.56 s. Impact speed: v = u + at = 12 − 10 × 2.56 = −13.6 m/s. The negative sign means downward, so the impact speed is about 13.6 m/s. Note the impact speed is larger than the launch speed (12 m/s) because the ball lands 2 m below where it was thrown.
2. **Q:** The graph card shows a velocity–time graph that rises in a straight line from 0 to 20 m/s over 4 s, then stays flat at 20 m/s for 6 s, then falls in a straight line to 0 over 2 s. (a) Calculate the acceleration in each of the three stages. (b) Calculate the total displacement over the whole 12 s using the area under the graph.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Stage 1 (0 to 4 s): slope = (20 − 0)/4 = 5 m/s². Stage 2 (4 to 10 s): slope = (20 − 20)/6 = 0 m/s² (constant velocity). Stage 3 (10 to 12 s): slope = (0 − 20)/2 = −10 m/s² (a deceleration). (b) The area has three parts. Stage 1 is a triangle: area = ½ × 4 × 20 = 40 m. Stage 2 is a rectangle: area = 6 × 20 = 120 m. Stage 3 is a triangle: area = ½ × 2 × 20 = 20 m. Total displacement = 40 + 120 + 20 = 180 m.
3. **Q:** A learner claims "a stone dropped from a high building and a stone thrown horizontally from the same height land at the same time." Using the idea of independent vertical and horizontal motion, defend or correct this claim, and state what is the same and what is different about the two stones' motions.
   **Worked Answer:** The claim is correct, assuming air resistance is negligible. The vertical and horizontal motions are independent. Vertically, both stones start from the same height with zero vertical speed and accelerate at g = 10 m/s², so they take exactly the same time to fall — the horizontal throw does not change the vertical motion. So the time to land is the same for both, and they land at the same instant. What is the same: the vertical motion (zero initial vertical velocity, same acceleration g, same height, same time of fall). What is different: the horizontal motion — the dropped stone has no horizontal velocity and lands directly below the release point, while the thrown stone has a constant horizontal velocity throughout the fall and lands some horizontal distance away. The thrown stone also has a larger total speed at impact because its velocity has both a horizontal and a vertical component, while the dropped stone has only the vertical component.

## Safety Notes
- This is a paper-and-game drill with no falling objects handled by learners — the main risk is excitement leading to slapping the desk too hard; remind learners a gentle tap is enough to "buzz".
- Teams can get competitive; the teacher is the final judge of every answer and settles disputes immediately to keep the pace.
- Graph cards passed around can be torn; one set per team, handled at the desk.
- Keep the noise at a level where the quizmaster can be heard — a quick "quiet for the next question" between rounds keeps order.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this drill revises Unit 3 (Describing Motion) and Unit 4 (Gravity, Falling and Projectiles). Learners should already know speed = d/t, acceleration = (v − u)/t, the slopes and areas on distance–time and velocity–time graphs, and free fall with g = 10 m/s². The drill is for speed and confidence, not first teaching — if a round exposes a widespread gap, slow the round and re-teach at the board.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on reading graph slopes (speed from a distance–time line) and simple speed and free-fall one-step problems (mango drop). Ages 14–17 take the velocity–time area calculations, the projectile thrown up from a height, and the independent-motion argument in the Advanced Questions.
- The biggest pitfall is learners using the launch speed directly as the impact speed for a thrown-up ball. Push them: *where does the ball land relative to where it was thrown?* Because it lands lower, it gains extra speed on the way down — the impact speed must be computed, not assumed.
- If graph cards run short, the teacher can sketch a fresh graph on the board each round; the format does not depend on printed cards.
- The game-show format keeps a 45-minute review active; collect the team scores but weight the individual post-test for the learner record.
- CBE assessment note: the four rounds and post-test map to JS-FE (motion and gravity). A learner who can read a velocity–time graph, calculate acceleration, and solve a free-fall or simple projectile with g = 10 m/s² is at mastery level for this strand.
