# Lesson 353: Core Drill IV: Pressure, Fluids and Buoyancy

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.2  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Calculate pressure from P = F/A and P = ρgh, and choose the right formula for a given situation (surface pressure vs. fluid-depth pressure).
2. Reason through a siphon or a U-tube scenario by tracking pressure at the same horizontal level in a connected fluid.
3. Apply Archimedes' principle to decide whether an object floats or sinks, and calculate the upthrust from the displaced fluid.
4. Move between lab-practical style stations, read an apparatus, record data, and answer a short question at each station.

## Materials
- A station kit of four labelled stations set up before the lesson:
  - Station A — a jerrican of water, a ruler, and a drawn table of "depth (cm) vs. pressure (Pa)" to complete (P = ρgh, ρ = 1000 kg/m³)
  - Station B — two syringes of different sizes joined by a short tube (water-filled): a "small plunger" and a "big plunger" to test force transfer (hydraulic analogy)
  - Station C — a basin of water with a stone, a plastic bottle cap, and a maize cob to test float or sink and estimate displaced volume
  - Station D — a siphon diagram on a card (two containers at different heights joined by a tube) with two "explain why the water moves" questions
- A stopwatch or the classroom clock for station rotation
- A printed station answer sheet per learner (four boxes, one per station)
- A printed marking scheme per pair for peer marking at the end
- A mini-whiteboard or chalkboard for the teacher's consolidation diagram
- Pencils for each learner

## Vocabulary
- **pressure** (shinikizo) — force acting on a surface divided by the area of that surface, measured in pascals (Pa)
- **fluid depth pressure** (shinikizo la kina) — the pressure in a liquid that increases with depth, given by P = ρgh
- **siphon** (sifoni) — a tube that moves water from a higher container to a lower one over a hump, driven by the pressure difference
- **upthrust** (nguvu ya kuelea) — the upward force a fluid exerts on a body partly or wholly immersed in it
- **Archimedes' principle** (kanuni ya Archimedes) — the upthrust equals the weight of the fluid the body displaces
- **displaced volume** (ujazo wa kusukumwa) — the volume of fluid pushed aside when a body is placed in it

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A woman of mass 60 kg stands on one foot. The area of her shoe sole touching the ground is 0.015 m². Calculate the pressure she exerts on the ground (take g = 10 N/kg).
2. Water has a density of 1000 kg/m³. What is the pressure at a depth of 3 m in a tank of water (take g = 10 N/kg)?
3. A stone is dropped into a basin of water and sinks. What does Archimedes' principle say about the force that pushes up on the stone while it is underwater?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Force F = mg = 60 × 10 = 600 N. Pressure P = F ÷ A = 600 ÷ 0.015 = 40 000 Pa (40 kPa).
2. P = ρgh = 1000 × 10 × 3 = 30 000 Pa (30 kPa).
3. The upthrust on the stone equals the weight of the water the stone displaces. Since the stone sinks, the upthrust is less than the stone's weight, but it still equals the weight of the displaced water.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect after 5 minutes. Tell learners this is a drill lesson: four short stations, one big idea each — pressure on a surface, pressure in a fluid, buoyancy, and the siphon. They will rotate every few minutes and record an answer at each station.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the two syringes from Station B joined by the tube. Push the small plunger; the big plunger moves. Ask: *why does a small push here move a big load there?* Take one or two guesses; do not correct yet. The hook: today we drill the four pressure ideas that explain this and everything from a jerrican tap to a floating gourd.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — station rotation. Four stations (A, B, C, D), about 3 to 4 minutes each. In pairs, learners move from station to station, read the card, do the short task, and write one answer with working in the station answer sheet. The teacher keeps time and calls the rotation. Pairs that finish early re-check their working at the current station. **Checkpoint:** every pair has visited all four stations and has one answer box filled for each.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board, one line per station. Draw the jerrican and label P = ρgh at the surface (h = 0, P = 0 relative to surface) and at the bottom (h = depth, P = ρgh) — the pressure grows with depth, which is why a jerrican leaks harder at the bottom. Then draw the two-syringe hydraulic and label the two areas: F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂, so a small force on a small area gives a bigger force on a bigger area — but the big plunger moves a shorter distance (energy is conserved). Confront the misconception that *pressure only comes from pushing on a solid* — in a fluid the pressure at the same horizontal level is the same everywhere, which is what drives the siphon (the water at the lower outlet is at a lower pressure than the atmosphere at the higher container, so the water flows down and over). Confront the misconception that *a heavy object always sinks because there is no upthrust* — there is always an upthrust equal to the weight of displaced fluid; the object sinks only if its weight exceeds that upthrust.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark using the printed scheme.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *pressure is force spread over area, and in a fluid it grows with depth — that one idea explains taps, jacks, siphons, and floating boats.* Homework: find one example at home of a small force on a small area lifting or moving a bigger load (a car jack, a watering can tap, a wheelbarrow handle), and write one sentence naming the pressure idea it uses.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. A boy of mass 40 kg stands on both feet. The total area of his two shoe soles touching the ground is 0.04 m². Calculate the pressure he exerts (take g = 10 N/kg). (mirrors pre-test)
2. Water has a density of 1000 kg/m³. What is the pressure at a depth of 2.5 m in a swimming pool (take g = 10 N/kg)? (mirrors pre-test)
3. In a hydraulic jack the small plunger has an area of 0.0004 m² and the big plunger has an area of 0.02 m². A force of 80 N is applied to the small plunger. What force does the big plunger exert on the load?
4. A block of wood of volume 0.0008 m³ floats half-submerged in water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg). Calculate the upthrust on the block and explain why it floats.
5. Challenge: A siphon tube moves water from a jerrican on a table to a bucket on the floor. Explain, using the idea of pressure at the same horizontal level in a connected fluid, why the water flows from the higher container to the lower one and does not flow back.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Force F = mg = 40 × 10 = 400 N. Pressure P = F ÷ A = 400 ÷ 0.04 = 10 000 Pa (10 kPa).
2. P = ρgh = 1000 × 10 × 2.5 = 25 000 Pa (25 kPa).
3. Pressure is transmitted equally: P₁ = F₁ ÷ A₁ = 80 ÷ 0.0004 = 200 000 Pa. F₂ = P₂ × A₂ = 200 000 × 0.02 = 4000 N. The big plunger exerts 4000 N on the load.
4. Displaced volume = half of 0.0008 = 0.0004 m³. Upthrust = weight of displaced water = ρVg = 1000 × 0.0004 × 10 = 4 N. The block floats because the upthrust (4 N) equals the weight of the block — the forces balance, so it stays at the surface.
5. The tube is one connected fluid. At the same horizontal level the pressure must be the same throughout the connected water. The water column at the lower outlet (on the floor) is deeper than the surface of the water in the jerrican on the table, so the pressure at the lower outlet is higher than the pressure at the upper surface. The atmosphere pushes equally on both open surfaces, so the pressure difference inside the tube drives water from high to low — out of the lower end. It does not flow back because to go back uphill the water would have to move from low pressure to high pressure, which the pressure difference will not allow.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** You need to lift a load of 100 kg (about 1000 N) with a hydraulic jack. You can push the small plunger with a force of 50 N. (a) What is the ratio of the plunger areas needed? (b) If the small plunger has an area of 0.0005 m², what is the area of the big plunger? (c) To lift the load by 5 cm, how far must you push the small plunger, and why?
   **Worked Answer:** (a) For a hydraulic, F₂/F₁ = A₂/A₁. So the ratio A₂/A₁ = F₂/F₁ = 1000/50 = 20. The big plunger must have 20 times the area of the small one. (b) A₂ = 20 × A₁ = 20 × 0.0005 = 0.01 m² (a circle of about 11 cm diameter). (c) Energy is conserved (ignoring friction): work in = work out. F₁ × d₁ = F₂ × d₂. So d₁ = (F₂ × d₂)/F₁ = (1000 × 0.05)/50 = 1.0 m. You must push the small plunger 1 m to lift the load 5 cm. The small force is traded for a long stroke; the big force moves only a short distance.
2. **Q:** A solid steel ball of mass 0.6 kg and density 7800 kg/m³ is dropped into a basin of water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg). (a) Calculate the volume of the ball. (b) Calculate the upthrust on it when fully submerged. (c) Calculate the apparent weight of the ball underwater and explain why it sinks.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Volume V = m/ρ = 0.6 ÷ 7800 ≈ 7.69 × 10⁻⁵ m³. (b) When fully submerged the displaced volume equals the ball's volume. Upthrust = ρ_water × V × g = 1000 × 7.69 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 ≈ 0.77 N. (c) True weight = mg = 0.6 × 10 = 6 N. Apparent weight = true weight − upthrust = 6 − 0.77 = 5.23 N. The ball sinks because the upthrust (0.77 N) is much less than the weight (6 N); the apparent weight is still positive, so the ball accelerates downward through the water.
3. **Q:** A dam holds water to a depth of 12 m. (a) Calculate the pressure at the bottom of the dam wall (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg). (b) The dam wall is 20 m wide. Explain why the wall is built thicker at the bottom than at the top, using a force calculation for a 1 m-wide strip of wall at the bottom (assume the pressure acts on the full 12 m height of that strip).
   **Worked Answer:** (a) P = ρgh = 1000 × 10 × 12 = 120 000 Pa (120 kPa). (b) The pressure increases with depth, so the force on the wall is far greater near the bottom. For a 1 m-wide strip of wall the average pressure over the full height = (P_top + P_bottom)/2 = (0 + 120 000)/2 = 60 000 Pa. The area of the 1 m-wide, 12 m-high strip = 12 m². Force = P_avg × area = 60 000 × 12 = 720 000 N. That single strip carries about 720 kN of force, concentrated towards the bottom; a wider, thicker base resists that large moment without toppling. This is why dam walls are built thicker at the bottom — to withstand the higher pressure and the larger turning effect (moment) the water exerts near the base.

## Safety Notes
- The water basins at Station C can spill and make the floor slippery; keep a cloth nearby and mop up spills at once. Learners must not run between stations.
- Syringes at Station B are for pushing water only — never push a dry plunger hard or point a syringe at a person; a jet of water can injure an eye.
- Stones dropped into the basin can splash water into eyes; place stones in gently, do not throw. Keep fingers out of the basin when a stone is being lowered.
- Station rotation can cause bumping in a crowded room; lay out stations around the edges of the room with clear paths between them.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this drill revises Unit 7 (Pressure) and Unit 8–9 (Fluids and Buoyancy). Learners should already have met P = F/A, P = ρgh, the siphon, and Archimedes' principle. The drill is for speed and confidence, not first teaching — if a station exposes a widespread gap, slow the rotation and re-teach that idea at the board before continuing.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the four station tasks (compute P = F/A and P = ρgh, observe the syringe force transfer, decide float or sink, describe the siphon in one sentence). Ages 14–17 tackle the ratio reasoning in the hydraulic jack, the apparent-weight calculation, and the dam-wall moment argument in the Advanced Questions.
- If a station kit is short of syringes, Station B can be run as a drawn diagram with two labelled areas and a "predict which plunger moves more" question; the physics is the same.
- The biggest pitfall at Station D is learners saying "the water flows because of gravity" without naming the pressure argument. Push them: *gravity on what? what is the pressure at the top of the tube compared to the bottom?* The answer must name the pressure difference at the same horizontal level in the connected fluid.
- Peer marking with the printed scheme at the end doubles as revision — learners see the expected working for all four stations, not just their own. Collect the answer sheets for a record of which station was weakest across the class.
- CBE assessment note: the station tasks and post-test map directly to G10-PHY-1.2 (pressure and fluids). A learner who correctly chooses between P = F/A and P = ρgh, and who can state the upthrust equals the weight of displaced fluid, is at mastery level for this strand.
