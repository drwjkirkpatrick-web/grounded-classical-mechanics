# Lesson 354: Core Drill V: Circles, Oscillations and Momentum

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** JS-FE; G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Calculate the speed of an object in circular motion using v = 2πr/T and identify the force that keeps it on the circle (centripetal force).
2. Use the pendulum formula T = 2π√(L/g) to find the period, length, or value of g from given data.
3. Apply conservation of momentum (p = mv) to a collision to find one unknown velocity.
4. Take part in a mini-whiteboard showdown: hold up a written answer with working within 30 seconds and defend it.

## Materials
- Mini-whiteboards or laminated card and dry-erase markers — one per learner (or one per pair)
- A cloth or eraser per board
- A printed set of "showdown questions" on cards — at least 12 short problems covering v = 2πr/T, pendulum T = 2π√(L/g), and p = mv collisions
- A stopwatch or the classroom clock for the 30-second rounds
- A printed scoreboard on the wall for team totals
- A printed marking scheme for the teacher to read out after each round
- A calculator per learner or pair (or work to 2 significant figures by hand)
- Pencils and the learners' exercise books for the post-test

## Vocabulary
- **circular motion** (mwendo wa duara) — motion of a body along a circular path at a constant speed
- **period** (kipindi) — the time taken to complete one full circle or one full swing, in seconds
- **centripetal force** (nguvu ya kuelekea kituo) — the inward force that keeps a body moving in a circle
- **simple pendulum** (pendulamu rahisi) — a mass on a string swinging back and forth under gravity
- **momentum** (matendo) — mass times velocity (p = mv); a vector with the same direction as the velocity
- **conservation of momentum** (uhifadhi wa matendo) — in a collision the total momentum before equals the total momentum after, provided no outside force acts

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A stone on a string moves in a circle of radius 0.5 m. It takes 2 s to complete one circle. Calculate its speed (take π = 3.14).
2. A pendulum has a length of 1 m. Calculate its period using T = 2π√(L/g), with g = 10 m/s² and π = 3.14.
3. A 2 kg ball moving at 4 m/s hits a stationary 2 kg ball and they stick together. What is the momentum of the moving ball before the collision, and what is the combined speed after?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Circumference = 2πr = 2 × 3.14 × 0.5 = 3.14 m. Speed v = 2πr/T = 3.14 ÷ 2 = 1.57 m/s (≈ 1.6 m/s).
2. T = 2π√(L/g) = 2 × 3.14 × √(1/10) = 6.28 × √0.1 = 6.28 × 0.316 ≈ 1.99 s (≈ 2.0 s).
3. Momentum before = mv = 2 × 4 = 8 kg m/s. After sticking, total mass = 2 + 2 = 4 kg. By conservation, total momentum after = 8 kg m/s. Speed = p/m = 8 ÷ 4 = 2 m/s.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect after 5 minutes. Hand out mini-whiteboards and markers; tell learners today is a "showdown" — quick questions, hold up the board, fastest correct answer wins the round for the team.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — swing a stone on a short string in a circle (one swing, low to the ground, away from learners) and release it straight up so it does not fly at anyone. Ask: *what kept it on the circle while I held it?* (The tension in the string — the centripetal force.) Then let a stone on a string swing as a pendulum once and ask: *what decides how long one swing takes?* The hook: today we drill the three big formulas that answer these — v = 2πr/T, T = 2π√(L/g), and p = mv — in a fast showdown.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — mini-whiteboard showdown. The class is in teams of four. The teacher reads a showdown question card (e.g. "A boy on a merry-go-round radius 2 m takes 5 s for one turn — find his speed — go!"). Learners have 30 seconds to write the working and the answer on their board and hold it up. The teacher reads the marking scheme; each team with the correct answer and working gets a point. Run about 12 rounds spread across the three formula families (roughly four on circles, four on the pendulum, four on momentum collisions). **Checkpoint:** every learner has answered at least one round and the team scores are on the scoreboard.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board, one line per formula family. For circles: v = 2πr/T gives the speed, but the direction is always changing, so there is acceleration towards the centre — the centripetal force — provided by tension (string), friction (car on a bend), or gravity (planet in orbit). Confront the misconception that *circular motion at constant speed has no acceleration* — the speed is constant but the velocity direction changes, so there is centripetal acceleration. For the pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g) shows the period depends only on length and g, not on mass or amplitude (for small swings). Confront the misconception that *a heavier pendulum swings slower* — it does not; mass cancels out. For momentum: total momentum is conserved in a collision if no outside force acts; p = mv must use the correct signs for direction. Confront the misconception that *momentum is always conserved so the speeds never change* — speeds do change, but the total mv (with direction) stays the same.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *circular motion, pendulums, and collisions all obey one rule — a conserved quantity (mass-energy for the pendulum, momentum for collisions) and a formula for the rate.* Homework: write one showdown question of your own from any of the three families, with the worked answer, for the next lesson's warm-up.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. A stone on a string moves in a circle of radius 0.8 m. It takes 4 s to complete one circle. Calculate its speed (take π = 3.14). (mirrors pre-test)
2. A pendulum has a length of 0.4 m. Calculate its period using T = 2π√(L/g), with g = 10 m/s² and π = 3.14. (mirrors pre-test)
3. A car of mass 1000 kg goes round a circular bend of radius 25 m at a speed of 5 m/s. (a) Calculate the time it takes to complete one circle. (b) Name the force that provides the centripetal force on the car.
4. A 3 kg ball moving at 6 m/s catches up and sticks to a 1 kg ball moving in the same direction at 2 m/s. Find the combined speed after the collision.
5. Challenge: A pendulum on the Moon, where g is about 1.6 m/s², has a length of 1 m. (a) Calculate its period on the Moon. (b) Explain in one sentence why the same pendulum has a longer period on the Moon than on Earth.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Circumference = 2πr = 2 × 3.14 × 0.8 = 5.024 m. Speed v = 2πr/T = 5.024 ÷ 4 = 1.256 m/s (≈ 1.3 m/s).
2. T = 2π√(L/g) = 2 × 3.14 × √(0.4/10) = 6.28 × √0.04 = 6.28 × 0.2 = 1.256 s (≈ 1.3 s).
3. (a) Time for one circle = T = 2πr/v = (2 × 3.14 × 25) ÷ 5 = 157 ÷ 5 = 31.4 s. (b) Friction between the tyres and the road provides the centripetal force.
4. Momentum before = (3 × 6) + (1 × 2) = 18 + 2 = 20 kg m/s (same direction, so both positive). Total mass after = 3 + 1 = 4 kg. Speed after = 20 ÷ 4 = 5 m/s in the same direction.
5. (a) T = 2π√(L/g) = 2 × 3.14 × √(1/1.6) = 6.28 × √0.625 = 6.28 × 0.79 ≈ 4.97 s (≈ 5.0 s). (b) The period depends on √(L/g); on the Moon g is smaller, so √(L/g) is larger, so the period is longer. Gravity pulls the bob back more weakly, so each swing takes more time.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** A cart of mass 2 kg moving at 3 m/s catches a stationary cart of mass 1 kg, and they couple together (sticky collision). (a) Calculate the final speed of the combined carts. (b) Calculate the total kinetic energy before and after the collision. (c) State what has happened to the "missing" kinetic energy and whether momentum was conserved.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Momentum before = (2 × 3) + (1 × 0) = 6 kg m/s. Total mass after = 2 + 1 = 3 kg. Final speed = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m/s. (b) KE before = ½(2)(3²) + ½(1)(0²) = 9 + 0 = 9 J. KE after = ½(3)(2²) = ½(3)(4) = 6 J. (c) Momentum was conserved (6 kg m/s before and after). But 3 J of kinetic energy was lost. In a sticky (perfectly inelastic) collision, that energy goes into heat, sound, and the deformation of the coupling surfaces. Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not — this is an inelastic collision.
2. **Q:** A conical pendulum is a mass on a string that moves in a horizontal circle so the string sweeps out a cone. A 0.5 kg mass moves in a horizontal circle of radius 0.3 m at a constant speed of 2 m/s. (a) Calculate the centripetal acceleration. (b) Calculate the centripetal force. (c) Explain what provides this force.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Centripetal acceleration a = v²/r = (2)² ÷ 0.3 = 4 ÷ 0.3 ≈ 13.3 m/s². (b) Centripetal force F = ma = 0.5 × 13.3 ≈ 6.7 N. (c) The horizontal component of the tension in the string provides the centripetal force; the vertical component of the tension balances the weight of the mass so it does not fall.
3. **Q:** A pendulum has a period of 1.5 s on Earth (g = 10 m/s²). (a) Calculate its length. (b) If the same pendulum is taken to a planet where g = 25 m/s², what is its new period? (c) If you wanted a period of 4 s on the original planet (g = 25 m/s²), what length would the pendulum need to be?
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Rearrange T = 2π√(L/g): L = g(T/2π)² = 10 × (1.5/(2 × 3.14))² = 10 × (1.5/6.28)² = 10 × (0.239)² = 10 × 0.0571 ≈ 0.571 m (≈ 0.57 m). (b) New period T' = 2π√(L/g') = 2 × 3.14 × √(0.571/25) = 6.28 × √0.0228 = 6.28 × 0.151 ≈ 0.948 s (≈ 0.95 s). The period falls because g is larger. (c) For T = 4 s on the new planet: L = g'(T/2π)² = 25 × (4/6.28)² = 25 × (0.637)² = 25 × 0.405 ≈ 10.1 m. A 4 s pendulum on that planet would need a length of about 10 m.

## Safety Notes
- If a stone-on-a-string is used for the engage demo, do it low to the ground and release the stone straight up or let it wind onto the hand — never release it towards the class. Use a short string (under 0.5 m) so the radius is small.
- Mini-whiteboard markers can stain clothes; remind learners to cap markers when not in use and to keep the cloth away from their faces.
- The showdown is fast; remind teams not to grab boards or markers from each other. The 30-second timing is for working, not for arguing over the board.
- Keep the floor clear of bags so learners can hold up boards without tripping.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this drill revises Unit 10 (Circular Motion) and Unit 11 (Oscillations), plus the momentum part of Unit 3/4 (motion and collisions). Learners should already have met v = 2πr/T, T = 2π√(L/g), and p = mv. The showdown is for speed and recall, not first teaching — if a round shows a whole-team gap, pause and re-teach that one formula before the next round.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the direct substitutions in the showdown (v = 2πr/T, T = 2π√(L/g), simple sticky-collision momentum). Ages 14–17 tackle the energy accounting in the inelastic collision, the conical pendulum's force components, and the rearranged pendulum formula (solving for L) in the Advanced Questions.
- If mini-whiteboards are short, learners can use their exercise books and hold up the open page — the showdown works the same way. The point is a visible, fast answer.
- The biggest pitfall in momentum questions is sign (direction). For Q4 of the post-test, both balls move in the same direction, so both momenta are positive; for a head-on collision one must be negative. Insist that learners write the direction or the sign.
- The pendulum pitfall is using T = 2π√(L/g) as "time per swing" — it is the time for one full back-and-forth, not the time from one side to the other (that is half the period). Flag this in the consolidate step if any team trips on it.
- CBE assessment note: the showdown and post-test map directly to JS-FE (foundational enquiry — motion and oscillation) and G10-PHY-2.1 (circular motion, oscillations, and momentum). A learner who correctly substitutes and handles direction in the momentum question is at mastery level.
