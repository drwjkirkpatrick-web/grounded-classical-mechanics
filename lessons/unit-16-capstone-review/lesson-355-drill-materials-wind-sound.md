# Lesson 355: Core Drill VI: Materials, Wind/Water and Sound

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.3; G10-PHY-2.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Apply Hooke's law (F = kx) to a spring or beam problem and find the spring constant from a force-extension graph.
2. Read the year's own windmill data log, compute water delivered in a day from flow rate and time, and state one assumption and one uncertainty.
3. Use the wave equation v = fλ to find the wavelength, frequency, or speed of a sound from given data.
4. Relate a material's properties (stiffness, strength, brittleness) to a real Kenyan structure (a latrine slab, a school roof truss, a windmill blade).

## Materials
- The year's own data logs: the windmill daily log (wind speed, pump flow rate, hours of pumping) from Unit 12–14; the Hooke's law table (force, extension) from the spring or beam test; the wave data table (frequency, wavelength) from the bottle xylophone or pan-pipe build
- Printed problem sets — at least 10 short questions drawn from these three logs (four on Hooke's law, three on the windmill data, three on v = fλ)
- A graph sheet showing the year's force-extension plot for the spring or beam (on the wall)
- A printed marking scheme per pair for peer marking
- A calculator per learner or pair (or work to 2 significant figures by hand)
- A mini-whiteboard or the chalkboard for the teacher's consolidation
- Pencils and the learners' exercise books for the post-test

## Vocabulary
- **Hooke's law** (kanuni ya Hooke) — the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, up to the elastic limit
- **spring constant** (常数 ya spring) — the stiffness of a spring, the force needed per metre of extension (k = F/x), in N/m
- **elastic limit** (kikomo cha kujinyooka) — the maximum extension beyond which a spring or beam will not return to its original length
- **flow rate** (kiwango cha mtiririko) — the volume of water delivered per unit time, in litres per minute or m³ per second
- **wavelength** (wavelength) — the distance between two successive crests of a wave, in metres
- **frequency** (masikio) — the number of waves passing a point per second, in hertz (Hz)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A spring stretches by 0.05 m when a force of 2 N is applied. Calculate the spring constant.
2. A windmill pumps water at 8 litres per minute for 2 hours. How many litres does it deliver in that time?
3. A sound wave has a frequency of 170 Hz and a speed of 340 m/s. Calculate its wavelength.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. k = F/x = 2 ÷ 0.05 = 40 N/m.
2. 2 hours = 120 minutes. Volume = 8 × 120 = 960 litres.
3. v = fλ → λ = v/f = 340 ÷ 170 = 2 m.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect after 5 minutes. Hand out the year's data logs and the problem sets. Tell learners today's drill uses the class's own data — not made-up numbers — so every answer comes from the logs they kept during the year.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up the windmill daily log and point to one row (a real day with a flow rate and hours). Ask: *on that day, how much water did our windmill actually deliver?* Take one or two estimates. Then hold up the force-extension graph on the wall and point to the straight part and the bent part. Ask: *which part obeys Hooke's law and which part does not?* The hook: today we answer questions from our own year of physics — materials, the windmill, and the sound waves we built.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — paired problem-solving from the logs. Learners work through the printed problem set in pairs, using the year's data logs as the data source. The problem set has three blocks: (A) four Hooke's-law questions using the force-extension table and graph (find k, find the extension for a given force, identify the elastic limit on the graph, compare two springs); (B) three windmill-data questions using the daily log (water delivered in a day, a week, and an estimate for the year — with assumptions stated); (C) three wave questions using the bottle-xylophone or pan-pipe data (find λ from v and f, find f from v and λ, explain why a longer pipe gives a lower note). The teacher circulates and points learners to the right column of the log when they are stuck. **Checkpoint:** every pair has at least seven of the ten questions answered with working shown.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board, one block at a time. For materials: on the force-extension graph, the straight line is Hooke's law (F = kx), the slope is the spring constant k, and the point where the line bends is the elastic limit — beyond it the beam or spring will not return. Confront the misconception that *a stiffer spring stretches less because it is weaker* — a stiffer spring has a larger k, so it needs more force for the same extension; it is stronger, not weaker. For the windmill: water delivered = flow rate × time; to scale a day to a year you must assume the wind is steady (an assumption) and that the pump does not break (an assumption); the uncertainty is the day-to-day variation in wind. Confront the misconception that *one day's reading is the year's average* — wind varies by season, so one day over- or under-estimates the year. For waves: v = fλ means that for a fixed speed (sound in air) a longer wavelength means a lower frequency — which is why the long pan-pipe gives a low note. Confront the misconception that *a louder note has a longer wavelength* — loudness changes the amplitude, not the wavelength or frequency.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark using the printed scheme.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *the data you kept this year is real physics — your logs answered today's questions, not a textbook.* Homework: pick one row of the windmill log and write one sentence on what assumption you would change to improve the year estimate, and why.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. A spring stretches by 0.02 m when a force of 4 N is applied. Calculate the spring constant. (mirrors pre-test)
2. A windmill pumps water at 6 litres per minute for 3 hours. How many litres does it deliver in that time? (mirrors pre-test)
3. The year's force-extension graph for a beam shows a straight line up to 5 N (extension 0.01 m) and then bends. (a) Calculate the spring constant from the straight part. (b) State the value of the elastic limit force.
4. The windmill log shows a flow rate of 10 litres per minute for 4 hours on a windy day. (a) How many litres were delivered that day? (b) Give one assumption you must make to estimate the yearly total from this one day, and one reason the estimate is uncertain.
5. Challenge: A pan-pipe has a longest pipe of length 0.3 m. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. For an open pipe, the fundamental wavelength is twice the pipe length (λ = 2L). (a) Calculate the wavelength. (b) Calculate the frequency of the note it plays. (c) Explain why a shorter pipe plays a higher note.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. k = F/x = 4 ÷ 0.02 = 200 N/m.
2. 3 hours = 180 minutes. Volume = 6 × 180 = 1080 litres.
3. (a) k = F/x = 5 ÷ 0.01 = 500 N/m. (b) The elastic limit is at 5 N — the force where the line stops being straight.
4. (a) 4 hours = 240 minutes. Volume = 10 × 240 = 2400 litres. (b) Assumption: the wind blows at this rate on most days of the year (steady wind). Uncertainty: wind varies by season and by day, so this one windy day may over-estimate the yearly total; calm days deliver far less.
5. (a) λ = 2L = 2 × 0.3 = 0.6 m. (b) f = v/λ = 340 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 566.7 Hz (≈ 567 Hz). (c) A shorter pipe has a shorter L, so λ = 2L is shorter. With v fixed, f = v/λ is larger — a higher frequency means a higher note. So shorter pipes play higher notes.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** From the windmill log, estimate the annual water delivered. Use these real entries: the log shows an average flow rate of 7 litres per minute, pumping for an average of 5 hours per day, on about 250 windy days in the year. (a) Calculate the annual total in litres and in cubic metres. (b) State two assumptions behind this estimate. (c) Estimate a reasonable uncertainty range for the annual total.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Per day: 7 litres/min × (5 × 60) min = 7 × 300 = 2100 litres per day. Per year: 2100 × 250 = 525 000 litres. In cubic metres: 525 000 ÷ 1000 = 525 m³. (b) Assumption 1: the 7 L/min and 5 h/day averages hold across the 250 windy days (no day is far from the average). Assumption 2: the pump efficiency does not drop over the year (no wear, no blockage). Assumption 3: "250 windy days" is a fair count for the year (the dry season may have fewer). (c) A reasonable uncertainty: the flow rate could be ±2 L/min (5 to 9) and the hours ±1 h (4 to 6). Low estimate: 5 × (4 × 60) × 250 = 5 × 240 × 250 = 300 000 L (300 m³). High estimate: 9 × (6 × 60) × 250 = 9 × 360 × 250 = 810 000 L (810 m³). So the annual total is about 525 m³, with a plausible range of roughly 300–810 m³ — an uncertainty of about ±40%.
2. **Q:** The year's force-extension test on a steel wire gave these readings: 0 N → 0 mm; 10 N → 0.5 mm; 20 N → 1.0 mm; 30 N → 1.6 mm; 40 N → 2.4 mm; 50 N → 3.5 mm. (a) Plot or describe the graph and state which points lie on the Hooke's-law straight line. (b) Calculate the spring constant from the straight part. (c) Estimate the elastic limit and explain how you know.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) The first four points (0, 0), (10, 0.5), (20, 1.0), (30, 1.6) lie close to a straight line; the last two (40, 2.4) and (50, 3.5) curve upward, showing increasing extension per unit force. (b) From the straight part (0 to 20 N): extension goes from 0 to 1.0 mm = 0.001 m. k = F/x = 20 ÷ 0.001 = 20 000 N/m (20 kN/m). Using the 30 N point: k = 30 ÷ 0.0016 = 18 750 N/m — close, confirming the line is roughly straight up to 30 N. (c) The elastic limit is at about 30 N. Beyond 30 N the extension grows faster than the force (the wire is yielding — it will not return to its original length). The bend in the graph between 30 N and 40 N marks the elastic limit.
3. **Q:** A school roof truss uses steel beams. A beam of length 2 m supports a load and sags by 3 mm under 5000 N. (a) Calculate the effective spring constant of the beam. (b) The maximum safe sag is 5 mm. What is the maximum safe load? (c) Explain why a brittle material (like a ceramic tile) is a poor choice for a load-bearing beam, even if it is strong in compression.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Sag x = 3 mm = 0.003 m. k = F/x = 5000 ÷ 0.003 ≈ 1 666 667 N/m (≈ 1.67 × 10⁶ N/m, or 1.67 MN/m). (b) At 5 mm = 0.005 m: F_max = k × x_max = 1 666 667 × 0.005 ≈ 8333 N. The maximum safe load is about 8300 N. (c) A brittle material like ceramic is strong in compression but fails suddenly with little or no warning (no plastic deformation) once the stress exceeds its limit. A steel beam, by contrast, sags and yields before it breaks — it shows visible warning (the sag). For a load-bearing beam you want a material that deforms before it fails so people can see the warning and evacuate; a brittle beam would snap without warning, so it is a poor choice for a beam even though it is strong.

## Safety Notes
- This is a data-and-paper lesson; the main risk is paper cuts from the printed logs — remind learners to slide sheets apart, not tear.
- The force-extension graph refers to a past test on a spring or beam; do not re-run that test today or handle weights — this lesson uses only the recorded data.
- If calculators are shared, remind learners not to fight over them; the showdown-style timing does not apply here (this is paired work, not a race).
- Keep the year's data logs flat on the desk; they are the class record and should not be written on or folded.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this drill revises Unit 13–14 (Materials and Structures, Hooke's law), Unit 12 (Wind and Water Power, the windmill log), and Unit 15 (Sound and Mechanical Waves, v = fλ). Learners should already have kept a windmill daily log and a force-extension table during the year; if a log is missing, use the sample values given in the problem set and mark the assumption clearly.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the direct substitutions (k = F/x, water = flow rate × time, λ = v/f) and on naming one assumption for the windmill estimate. Ages 14–17 tackle the full annual estimate with an uncertainty range, the elastic-limit reading from the graph, and the brittle-vs-ductile material argument in the Advanced Questions.
- The biggest pitfall in the windmill estimate is multiplying a single day by 365 without accounting for wind variation. Push learners to name the assumption (steady wind) and the uncertainty (seasonal variation) — this is the "state your assumptions" skill that the capstone and the exam reward.
- For the wave block, watch for learners who confuse amplitude with wavelength. Amplitude is the height of the crest (loudness); wavelength is crest-to-crest distance (pitch). The pan-pipe build from Unit 15 is the anchor — a longer pipe gives a lower note because λ is longer, not because the wave is louder.
- If the class windmill log is thin (few days recorded), use those days honestly and state the gap as the main uncertainty — do not invent data. The skill being drilled is reading real logs and stating assumptions, not producing a perfect number.
- CBE assessment note: the Hooke's-law and material-structure tasks map to G10-PHY-1.3 (materials and their properties); the wave tasks map to G10-PHY-2.1 (waves). A learner who reads the elastic limit off the graph and states an assumption with the windmill estimate is at mastery level.
