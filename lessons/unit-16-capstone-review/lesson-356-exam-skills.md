# Lesson 356: Exam Skills: Reading CBE-Style Questions

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Identify the command word in a CBE-style question (state, explain, calculate, justify, describe) and match it to the expected answer type and length.
2. Structure a multi-mark answer using the "point — reason — evidence" format for explain and justify questions.
3. Budget time across a paper by allocating roughly one minute per mark and leaving the hardest question for last.
4. Name three common exam traps (missing units, answering a different question, ignoring the direction sign) and show how to avoid each.

## Materials
- A printed sample paper per learner — at least four CBE-style questions covering the year's units, with mark allocations shown (one "state", one "explain", one "calculate", one "justify")
- A printed official-style marking scheme per pair for two of the sample questions
- A printed "command words" reference card per learner — one line per word: state (one point, no working), explain (point + reason, sometimes with evidence), calculate (working + answer + unit), justify (a choice with reasons, weighing both sides), describe (say what happens, step by step)
- A printed "time budget" sheet per learner — a grid for marks per question and minutes per question
- A red pen per learner for marking
- A printed "common traps" card per learner — three traps with examples
- A clock or stopwatch visible to the class for the timed practice
- Pencils and the learners' exercise books

## Vocabulary
- **command word** (neno la amri) — the verb at the start of a question (state, explain, calculate, justify) that tells you what kind of answer to give
- **marking scheme** (mpango wa alama) — the official document showing how marks are awarded for each part of an answer
- **time budget** (bajeti ya muda) — a plan that allocates minutes to questions based on their marks so the paper is finished in time
- **justify** (kutoa sababu) — to give reasons for a choice, weighing the options, not just stating the choice
- **common trap** (mtego wa kawaida) — a frequent mistake in exams, such as omitting units or answering the wrong question

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What kind of answer does the command word "state" expect, and how is it different from "explain"?
2. A question carries 4 marks. If you spend roughly one minute per mark, how many minutes should you spend on it?
3. Name one common exam trap you have fallen into this year (for example, forgetting the unit, or answering a different question from the one asked).

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. "State" expects a single point or fact with no working or explanation — often one short sentence. "Explain" expects the point plus the reason (and sometimes evidence), so the answer is longer and shows why, not just what.
2. 4 marks × 1 minute per mark = 4 minutes.
3. Accept any real trap the learner names: forgetting the unit on a calculated answer, answering "calculate" with a one-word "state" answer, ignoring the direction sign in a momentum or velocity question, misreading the question (e.g. "time to fall" vs "speed on landing"), leaving the hardest question and running out of time. The trap must be concrete, not just "I made a mistake."

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect after 5 minutes. Hand out the command-words reference card and the sample paper. Tell learners this is the last drill before the capstone and the exam — today is not new physics, it is how to turn what you know into marks.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — write one question on the board: "A stone is dropped from a height. State the form of energy the stone has at the top." Ask one learner to answer. Then change the word: "Explain why the stone speeds up as it falls." Ask another learner to answer. The point: one word changed, and the expected answer doubled in length and depth. The hook: the command word is the first thing to read — it tells you what to write.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — paired work on the sample paper. Learners read all four questions, circle the command word in each, and write the answer type they expect (one point, point + reason, working + unit, or a justified choice). Then they answer the "state" and "calculate" questions fully and plan (not fully write) the "explain" and "justify" answers as bullet points. After about 8 minutes, pairs swap papers and mark the "state" and "calculate" answers against the official-style scheme with the red pen. **Checkpoint:** every pair has circled all four command words and marked at least two answers using the scheme.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Walk through the four command words and the expected answer shape: state (one point, no working — e.g. "potential energy"); explain (point + reason — e.g. "the stone speeds up because gravitational PE converts to kinetic energy, so velocity increases"); calculate (working shown + final answer + unit — e.g. "v² = u² + 2as = 0 + 2 × 10 × 5 = 100, v = 10 m/s"); justify (state the choice, then give reasons favouring it and a reason the other option is worse). Confront the misconception that *more writing always means more marks* — for a "state" question a long paragraph wastes time and earns no extra mark; for a "calculate" question the unit is worth a mark all by itself, so a correct number with no unit loses a mark. Then walk through time budgeting: total minutes ÷ total marks = minutes per mark; allocate one minute per mark and leave five minutes at the end for checking. Confront the trap of doing the hardest question first and running out of time on easy marks — do the easy questions first, bank those marks, then return to the hard one. Finally, name the three common traps: (1) missing units on a calculated answer, (2) answering a different question (misreading "time" as "speed", or "deceleration" as "acceleration"), and (3) ignoring the direction sign in a momentum or velocity question.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer under timed conditions (10 minutes, like a short exam section); collect or peer-mark using the printed scheme.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *read the command word first, budget one minute per mark, and never hand in a calculated answer without a unit.* Homework: take one question from any drill this week, circle the command word, and rewrite your answer in the shape the command word demands.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. What kind of answer does the command word "state" expect, and how is it different from "explain"? (mirrors pre-test)
2. A question carries 5 marks. If you spend roughly one minute per mark, how many minutes should you spend on it? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Read this question: "Calculate the work done when a force of 20 N moves a box 3 m. (3 marks)" Write the answer in the form a marking scheme would reward — show working, the answer, and the unit.
4. Read this question: "Justify why a windmill is a better choice than a petrol pump for a remote school with no mains power. (4 marks)" Write a structured answer that states the choice and gives two reasons favouring it.
5. Challenge: A learner answered the question "Explain why a heavier pendulum bob does not change the period" with the single word "No." Identify two things wrong with this answer and rewrite it in the form a marking scheme would reward.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. "State" expects one point or fact, often a single short sentence, with no working or reason. "Explain" expects the point plus the reason (and sometimes evidence), so the answer is longer and shows why, not just what.
2. 5 marks × 1 minute per mark = 5 minutes.
3. Work = force × distance = 20 × 3 = 60 J. Working shown (20 × 3), answer (60), unit (J). Full 3 marks: 1 for the correct formula or working, 1 for the correct numerical answer, 1 for the correct unit (joules, J). An answer of "60" with no unit loses the unit mark.
4. A windmill is the better choice. Reason 1: a remote school with no mains power cannot run a petrol pump without fuel deliveries, but a windmill uses wind, which is free and always available. Reason 2: a windmill has no fuel cost and lower running cost, so the school saves money it would spend on petrol. (A reason that the petrol pump is worse: it needs fuel, spare parts, and maintenance that the remote location makes hard to supply.) Full 4 marks: 1 for stating the choice, 1 for each of two reasons favouring it, 1 for a reason the other option is weaker or impractical.
5. Two things wrong: (a) The question says "explain", so a one-word answer "No" gives no reason and earns zero — an explain answer needs a point plus a reason. (b) "No" does not even answer the question asked; the question assumes the period does not change and asks why, so the answer should explain the reason, not deny the premise. A rewritten scheme-worthy answer: "The period of a pendulum is T = 2π√(L/g), which depends only on length L and g, not on the mass of the bob. So a heavier bob has the same period because mass does not appear in the formula — the larger weight and the larger inertia of the heavier bob cancel each other out." Full marks: the point (period depends on L and g, not mass) plus the reason (mass does not appear in the formula / weight and inertia cancel).

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)
1. **Q:** Mark two sample answers against an official-style scheme. The question: "Explain why a hydraulic jack can lift a heavy load with a small effort. (3 marks)" Answer A: "Because the pressure is the same in both plungers, so a small force on a small area gives a big force on a big area." Answer B: "Hydraulic jack uses liquid." Write the mark each deserves and the feedback you would give each learner.
   **Worked Answer:** Answer A: 3 out of 3. It states the key idea (pressure is equal throughout the connected fluid) and links it to the force-amplification (small area → small force, big area → big force), which is exactly what the scheme rewards (1 for equal pressure, 1 for the area-force link, 1 for correct reasoning). Feedback: "Full marks — clear, correct, and complete." Answer B: 0 out of 3. "Uses liquid" is true but is not an explanation; it does not say why a small effort lifts a big load. Feedback: "You identified the right device but did not explain the physics. An 'explain' answer needs the reason: the pressure is the same in both plungers, so the force scales with the area. Add the equal-pressure idea and the area-force link to earn the marks."
2. **Q:** A paper has 60 marks and the exam lasts 60 minutes. Question 1 is worth 4 marks and is easy for you; Question 5 is worth 15 marks and is hard. (a) How many minutes should you spend on each question if you follow the one-minute-per-mark rule? (b) In what order should you answer the questions, and why? (c) How much checking time should you leave, and what is the first thing you check?
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Q1: 4 minutes. Q5: 15 minutes. The rule gives one minute per mark, so a 4-mark question gets 4 minutes and a 15-mark question gets 15 minutes. (b) Answer the easy questions first (Q1), bank those marks, then attempt the hard 15-mark question (Q5), and if you get stuck, move on rather than spend 20 minutes on it. Reason: easy marks are guaranteed; hard marks are not, and spending all your time on Q5 risks losing easy marks on the rest of the paper. (c) Leave about 5 minutes for checking (60 minutes − 55 minutes of answering, or leave a buffer by answering slightly faster). The first thing to check is units on every calculated answer — a missing unit is the single most common lost mark and takes seconds to fix.
3. **Q:** Read this question: "A car of mass 1000 kg accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 10 s. Calculate the acceleration and the force. (4 marks)" A learner writes: "a = 2, F = 2000." (a) How many marks does this answer deserve, and why? (b) Rewrite the answer to earn all 4 marks. (c) State the command word and the two things a marking scheme rewards for a "calculate" answer.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) The learner has the right numbers but loses marks for missing working and missing units. A scheme typically awards 1 mark for the acceleration value, 1 for its unit, 1 for the force value, 1 for its unit. The answer "a = 2, F = 2000" gets 2 out of 4 (the two numerical values) and loses 2 for no units and (depending on the scheme) no working shown. (b) Rewritten: a = Δv/Δt = (20 − 0)/10 = 2 m/s². F = ma = 1000 × 2 = 2000 N. Working shown for both, units on both. (c) The command word is "calculate". A marking scheme for a calculate answer rewards: (1) the correct working or formula substitution, and (2) the final answer with the correct unit. Without the working the scheme may still award the answer mark but not the method mark; without the unit the answer mark is lost.

## Safety Notes
- This is a paper-and-pen lesson; the main risk is paper cuts from the sample papers — remind learners to slide sheets apart.
- Red pens for marking can stain clothes; remind learners to cap pens and keep them on the desk.
- The timed practice can cause anxiety; tell learners this is practice, not the real exam, and that the point is to learn the time-budget habit, not to finish perfectly.
- If learners become stressed by the timing, allow the post-test to be untimed and focus the timing practice on the explore phase only.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson draws on the whole year (Units 1–15) but teaches no new physics — it is about exam technique. Learners should have sat the year's drills and post-tests, so they have seen CBE-style questions before. The command words (state, explain, calculate, justify, describe) are the CBE vocabulary.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on identifying the command word and giving the right answer shape (one point for "state", working + unit for "calculate"), plus naming one common trap. Ages 14–17 tackle the full marking of sample answers against a scheme, the time-budget reasoning, and the "justify" answer that weighs both options, in the Advanced Questions.
- The biggest pitfall is learners writing a long paragraph for a "state" question (wasting time) or a one-word answer for a "calculate" question (losing the working and unit marks). The command-words card is the anchor — read the word first, then write.
- For the time budget, insist on one minute per mark as a starting rule, not a rigid law; some questions are faster (a one-mark "state"), some slower (a multi-step "calculate"). The rule prevents the failure mode of spending 20 minutes on a 4-mark question.
- The "common traps" card should grow from the class's real mistakes during the year — pull three traps the class actually fell into (from the drill post-tests) rather than generic ones, so the lesson feels like their own revision.
- Peer marking with the official-style scheme is itself exam-skill practice: learners see how marks are awarded, which is the best way to learn how to earn them. Collect the marked papers to see which traps are still common.
- CBE assessment note: this lesson maps directly to G10-PHY-1.1 (scientific skills, including communication and assessment literacy). A learner who identifies the command word, structures the answer to match it, and includes units on calculated answers is at mastery level for this skill strand.
