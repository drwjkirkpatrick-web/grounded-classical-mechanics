# Lesson 357: Practical Skills Review: Plan a Fair Test in 20 Minutes

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1; JS-FE  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Given a mystery question ("does blade angle matter?"), identify the independent, dependent, and control variables and write them in a plan.
2. Write a step-by-step method and a blank results table that match the variables chosen.
3. List the safety risks of the test and the controls that make the test fair.
4. Sketch the expected graph shape and state what the gradient would tell a reader.

## Materials
- A printed planning template per learner (three columns: variable type, name, how measured) — or a blank sheet ruled into three columns
- The mystery question written on the board: *"Does the angle of a windmill blade change how fast the rotor spins?"*
- A sample rotor or a photo of the Unit 14 windmill blade set at two different angles (for the Engage hook)
- Pencils and rulers for sketching the expected graph
- A stopwatch or phone timer (one per group, to remind learners how rotation rate is measured)
- The Unit 14 windmill builds, if still available, as a visual reference

## Vocabulary
- **independent variable** (tamko la kujitegemea) — the one thing the learner chooses to change on purpose, here the blade angle
- **dependent variable** (tamko linalotegemea) — what is measured to see the effect, here the rotations per second
- **control variable** (tamko la kudhibiti) — the things kept the same so only the independent variable causes any change
- **fair test** (jaribio la haki) — a test where only one thing changes and everything else is held constant
- **results table** (jedwali la matokeo) — a grid with a column for the independent variable and a column for the dependent variable
- **gradient** (mwinuko) — the slope of a graph line; tells how much the dependent variable changes per unit of the independent variable

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. In a fair test, how many variables should you change at a time?
2. You are testing how blade angle affects how fast a rotor spins. Which is the dependent variable?
3. Name one thing you would keep the same (control) in that test so it is fair.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. One. Only the independent variable is changed on purpose; all other variables are held constant so that any change in the result is due to that one variable alone.
2. The rotation rate (rotations per second, or how fast the rotor spins). The dependent variable is what you measure to see the effect of changing the blade angle.
3. Accept any correct control: the same fan or wind source at the same distance and speed; the same blade size, shape, and number; the same hub and bearing; the same axle; the same wind direction relative to the rotor. The control must be a variable that, if changed, could also affect the spin rate.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Tell learners this is the practical-skills review: the year's builds are done, and now the skill is *planning a fair test* under a time limit, the way a practical exam asks.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show the sample rotor (or photo) at two blade angles. Ask: *"If I bend the blades more, will it spin faster or slower?"* Take a quick show of hands, then reveal the mystery question on the board: *"Does the angle of a windmill blade change how fast the rotor spins?"* Tell learners they have the next block to plan this test on paper — not to build it, just to plan it as if an examiner gave them 20 minutes.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — individually, learners fill the planning template: (a) variables — independent, dependent, and at least three controls; (b) a numbered method of six steps or fewer; (c) a blank results table with correct headings and units; (d) two safety risks and their controls. The teacher moves between desks, prompting only with questions: *"What will you keep the same? How will you measure the spin? Where are the units in your table?"* **Checkpoint:** each learner has all four parts written before the Explain block.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board. Walk through one strong plan: independent = blade angle (degrees, measured with a protractor or set to fixed angles like 10°, 20°, 30°); dependent = rotations per second (count turns in 10 seconds, divide by 10); controls = same wind source, same distance, same blade size and number, same hub. Draw the blank table with headings "Blade angle (°)" and "Rotations per second (s⁻¹)". Confront the misconception that *"any graph of a fair test is a straight line"* — the relationship between blade angle and spin rate may rise then fall (too steep a blade stalls), so the expected graph could be a curve with a peak, not a line. The gradient of a straight section tells you how sensitive the spin rate is to the angle.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *a fair test changes one thing, measures one thing, and holds everything else still — and the plan must say so before you touch a tool.* Homework: take the mystery question home and add the analysis plan — which graph you would draw, what the gradient means, and one statement of uncertainty (e.g., reaction time on the stopwatch). Bring it next lesson; it becomes the entry point to the capstone brief.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. In a fair test, how many variables should you change at a time? (mirrors pre-test)
2. You are testing how blade angle affects how fast a rotor spins. Which is the dependent variable? (mirrors pre-test)
3. Write the two column headings (with units) for the results table of the blade-angle test.
4. A learner plans to test blade angles of 10°, 20°, and 30° but uses a different fan speed for each angle. Why is this plan not fair, and what should they do?
5. Challenge: sketch the expected graph of rotation rate against blade angle, and explain in one sentence why it might not be a straight line all the way.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. One. Only the independent variable is changed on purpose; all other variables are held constant.
2. The rotation rate (rotations per second). The dependent variable is what you measure to see the effect of the independent variable.
3. Column 1: "Blade angle (°)". Column 2: "Rotations per second (s⁻¹)" — accept "rotations per minute (min⁻¹)" or "turns in 10 s" with matching units. Both headings must have units.
4. The plan is not fair because the fan speed is also changing, so any difference in spin rate could be due to the wind speed, not the blade angle. The learner should keep the fan speed, distance, and direction the same for every angle, changing only the blade angle.
5. The graph rises at first (steeper blades catch more wind, so faster spin) but may level off and then fall at very steep angles (the blades stall and present a flat face to the wind, so the spin drops). So the curve can have a peak — it is not a straight line all the way. Award marks for a curve that rises then falls (or rises and levels off) and a correct one-sentence reason about stalling or the blade blocking the wind.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** For the blade-angle test, a learner proposes measuring the dependent variable as "the time for 10 rotations" instead of "rotations in 10 seconds." (a) Which arrangement gives a more precise result at high spin rates, and why? (b) If the graph of "time for 10 rotations" against blade angle is plotted, what does a *falling* line mean about the spin rate?
   **Worked Answer:** (a) "Time for 10 rotations" is more precise at high spin rates, because counting rotations in a fixed 10-second window becomes error-prone when the rotor spins very fast (you may miss a turn at the boundary). Timing a fixed count of 10 turns avoids the boundary-counting error. At low spin rates, "rotations in 10 seconds" is fine because the count is small and easy. (b) A falling line for "time for 10 rotations" means the time per 10 turns is getting shorter, so the rotor is spinning *faster*. The spin rate is the inverse of the time per turn, so a smaller time means a higher rate.
2. **Q:** The expected graph of rotation rate (y, in s⁻¹) against blade angle (x, in degrees) rises to a peak at about 25° and then falls. (a) State what the gradient dy/dx tells you at the steepest rising part. (b) Estimate, from the peak, the single best blade angle to choose for the capstone windmill, and justify it in terms of energy capture.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) The gradient dy/dx is the change in rotation rate per degree of blade angle — it tells how sensitive the spin is to the angle in that region. At the steepest rising part, the gradient is largest, meaning a small change in angle gives a large increase in spin rate. (b) The best single angle is the peak, about 25°, because that is where the rotation rate is highest for a given wind speed, so the rotor captures the most kinetic energy per second. Choosing an angle off the peak (either side) gives a lower spin rate and therefore less useful power.
3. **Q:** Write the analysis plan for the blade-angle test: (a) which graph to draw and why, (b) what the gradient of the straight (rising) section means in words, and (c) one source of uncertainty and how you would state it in the conclusion.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Plot rotation rate (y-axis, s⁻¹) against blade angle (x-axis, degrees), with error bars on the rate from repeat trials. A scatter-with-line is chosen because we are looking for a trend (rise to a peak and fall), not proving a straight-line law. (b) The gradient of the straight rising section is the change in rotation rate per degree of angle — it tells how much extra spin you gain per degree of extra pitch, i.e., the sensitivity of the rotor to blade angle in that region. (c) One uncertainty source is human reaction time on the stopwatch (about ±0.2 s). If timing 10 rotations, the rate has an uncertainty of roughly ±(0.2 / measured time) × (rate). In the conclusion, state: "The peak spin rate is at about 25°, with an uncertainty of ±X s⁻¹ due to reaction time; angles within ±2° of the peak give rates within this uncertainty, so they are indistinguishable."

## Safety Notes
- This is a planning lesson with no building — the main risk is classroom movement during the desk-by-desk Explore block. Keep bags and chairs tucked in so learners can move between rows without tripping.
- If a sample rotor is passed around for the Engage hook, blades and wire ends can scratch or poke. Inspect the rotor for sharp edges before passing it; do not let learners spin it near their faces.
- Stopwatches and phones used as timers should stay on the desk — do not wave them around; a dropped phone is a cracked screen and a cut risk.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson draws on Unit 14 (Wind & Water Power, Lessons 301–328), where learners built windmills and varied blade angle in practice. It also draws on the exam-skills lesson (356) just before it. No new physics is introduced; the skill is planning a fair test on paper under a time limit.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on naming the three variable types correctly, writing a fair method, and drawing a simple results table with units. Ages 14–17 must add the analysis plan (graph choice, gradient meaning, uncertainty statement) in the homework and handle the Advanced Questions on precision, peak interpretation, and error bars.
- The 15-minute Explore block is the heart of the lesson — protect it from interruptions. The teacher prompts with questions only, never gives the answer; the struggle to name controls is where the learning happens.
- If learners stall on the controls, point at the windmill and ask: *"If I changed the wind too, would you trust the result?"* — this usually unlocks the list of controls without giving them away.
- Collect the planning templates at the end; the best plans become worked examples for the capstone brief in Lesson 358. Photograph two strong plans for the portfolio as evidence of G10-PHY-1.1 practical-planning skill.
