# Lesson 358: Capstone I: Choose the Challenge

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** capstone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. As a team, choose one capstone challenge from the project menu (windmill upgrade, pump/siphon system, bridge-plus, instrument ensemble, or an approved new idea) and justify the choice.
2. Write a project brief that states the goal, the physics it uses, the materials, and the team roles.
3. List three measurable success criteria and a simple test plan that will tell the team whether the build meets the goal.
4. State the single riskiest part of their project and what they will prototype first to retire that risk.

## Materials
- A printed project menu per team (the four challenges plus a blank "approved new idea" line), with one line of physics for each
- A printed brief template per team: Goal / Physics / Materials / Roles / Success criteria / Test plan / Riskiest part
- The four source builds from earlier units, set out as a "menu table" if available: the Unit 14 windmill, the Unit 14 siphon/ram pump demo, a Unit 13 bridge or truss, and the Unit 15 instrument ensemble
- Pencils and blank paper for each team
- A timer or phone for the team-pitch clock
- A class wall board or flip chart to post the team briefs

## Vocabulary
- **capstone** (jiwe la pili) — the final project that pulls together the physics of the whole year into one team build
- **brief** (muhtasari wa mradi) — a short written plan stating the goal, the physics, the materials, the roles, and how success will be measured
- **success criteria** (viwango vya mafanikio) — the measurable targets that tell the team the build works, e.g., "lifts water 1 m in 30 seconds"
- **test plan** (mpango wa kupima) — the steps the team will follow to check the build against the success criteria
- **role** (jukumu) — each learner's job in the team, e.g., builder, measurer, recorder, presenter
- **riskiest part** (sehemu hatari zaidi) — the one component most likely to fail, which the team builds first to learn fast

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. What is a capstone project, in one sentence?
2. In a team project, why is it useful to write a brief before you start building?
3. Name one physics idea from this year that you would use to lift water using wind.

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A capstone is a final project that pulls together the physics and skills of the whole year into one team build that solves a real problem.
2. A brief makes the goal, the roles, the materials, and the measure of success clear before building, so the team does not waste time or argue later, and so the teacher can check the plan is fair and safe.
3. Accept any correct physics link: the wind's kinetic energy turns a rotor (Unit 14) which drives a pump or lifts a weight (work = force × distance, Unit 6); the rotor must be balanced (Unit 14); the blades must be angled to catch the wind (Unit 14). The link must connect wind to lifting water.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Have teams of 3–4 sit together; tell them this is the first of three capstone lessons and the goal today is to choose the challenge and write the brief.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — stand at the menu table and name each challenge in 20 seconds: *"Windmill upgrade — make last term's windmill lift water, not just spin. Pump/siphon system — move water uphill with no electricity. Bridge-plus — a truss that holds 5 kg and opens for a toy car. Instrument ensemble — three instruments that play a simple tune together. Or pitch your own idea."* Ask each team to lean in and point at the one they want. Board the rule: *choose in 5 minutes, justify in one sentence.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — each team fills the brief template: Goal (one sentence), Physics (two ideas from the year), Materials (local, real), Roles (one per member), three Success criteria (measurable), a Test plan (numbered steps), and the Riskiest part (one component). The teacher moves between teams checking that the goal is achievable in two build lessons and that the success criteria are measurable — e.g., *"lifts 500 mL of water to 1 m in 30 s"* not *"works well."* **Checkpoint:** every team has a brief with all seven sections filled before the Explain block.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — regroup and consolidate on the board. Walk through one team's brief as a worked example, fixing it live: make the goal specific, the physics explicit, the success criteria numeric, and the riskiest part concrete. Confront the misconception that *"the brief is just paperwork"* — a good brief is the team's contract; it tells you what "done" means and what to build first. Emphasise that the riskiest part is built first in Lesson 359 so the team learns fast and can pivot before the full build in Lesson 360.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *choose well, measure success, build the risky part first.* Homework: each learner writes one paragraph on their team role and what they will bring to the next lesson to start the prototype. Bring the brief — it is the entry ticket to Lesson 359.

## Hands-On Build: The Capstone Brief
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Print the brief template and the project menu the day before.*

### Parts
- A printed project menu (the four challenges plus the blank approved-new-idea line)
- A printed brief template with seven sections: Goal / Physics / Materials / Roles / Success criteria / Test plan / Riskiest part
- A pen or pencil per learner
- A sheet of flip-chart paper or a wall board to post the finished briefs
- Tape or pins to mount the briefs

### Workflow
1. Each team reads the project menu and points to one challenge. The teacher checks the choice is feasible in two build lessons; if a team picks the "approved new idea," the teacher approves or redirects it now. **Checkpoint:** every team has a chosen challenge the teacher has accepted.
2. The team writes the Goal in one sentence — what the build must do, e.g., "Our windmill lifts 500 mL of water to 1 m height in 30 seconds." **Checkpoint:** the goal has a number and a time, not a vague word like "well."
3. The team lists two physics ideas the build uses, named from the year, e.g., "rotor extracts wind kinetic energy" and "work = force × distance to lift the water." **Checkpoint:** each physics idea is named, not just described.
4. The team lists the materials — local, real objects, e.g., mabati offcuts, bicycle hub, plastic pipe, string, a jerrican. **Checkpoint:** every material is something the team can actually get in the next two days.
5. The team assigns one role per member: builder, measurer, recorder, presenter (or project lead, materials lead, data lead, safety lead). **Checkpoint:** every learner has one role and knows what it means.
6. The team writes three measurable success criteria and a numbered test plan with the steps to check each one. **Checkpoint:** each success criterion is a number or a yes/no test; the test plan steps are numbered.
7. The team names the single riskiest part — the component most likely to fail — and writes it in the box. **Checkpoint:** the riskiest part is a concrete component (e.g., "the blade-to-pump linkage"), not a vague worry.

### Common Mistakes
- **Vague goal** — "make a good windmill" has no measure of done. Fix: rewrite the goal with a number and a time, e.g., "lift 500 mL to 1 m in 30 s."
- **Too many success criteria** — five criteria make the test plan unmanageable in two lessons. Fix: keep three, each measurable in one test.
- **No riskiest part** — a team that says "nothing is risky" has not thought it through. Fix: ask, *"if one thing breaks the whole project, what is it?"* — that is the riskiest part, and it gets built first.
- **Roles without a job** — a learner named "helper" has no clear task. Fix: each role has one sentence describing the job, so every learner is busy in every lesson.
- **Materials the team cannot get** — listing "a motor" when no one has one wastes the build lessons. Fix: every material must be something a team member can bring from home or borrow from the school store today.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. What is a capstone project, in one sentence? (mirrors pre-test)
2. In a team project, why is it useful to write a brief before you start building? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A team's goal is "make a good bridge." Rewrite it as a measurable goal with a number.
4. A team lists four success criteria and runs out of time to test them all. What should they do, and why is three the recommended number?
5. Challenge: your team is building a windmill pump. Name the single riskiest part and say why you would build and test it first, before the rest of the windmill.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A capstone is a final project that pulls together the physics and skills of the whole year into one team build that solves a real problem.
2. A brief makes the goal, roles, materials, and measure of success clear before building, so the team does not waste time, and so the teacher can check the plan is fair, feasible, and safe.
3. Accept any measurable rewrite with a number and a condition, e.g., "Our bridge holds a 5 kg load for 10 seconds without breaking" or "Our bridge spans 40 cm and holds 3 kg." The goal must have a number, a span, and a load or time.
4. The team should drop to three criteria, keeping the most important ones. Three is recommended because each criterion needs a separate test, and with only two build lessons there is time to design, build, and test three things well — more than three means each test is rushed and unreliable.
5. Accept any sensible riskiest part with a reason, e.g., "the linkage between the rotor and the pump" because if the rotor spins but cannot drive the pump, the whole project fails; building and testing the linkage first tells the team whether the idea works before they spend time on the tower and blades. The reason must explain that building the risky part first lets the team fail fast and pivot.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A team's capstone goal is "lift 500 mL of water to 1 m height in 30 seconds using a windmill." (a) Calculate the minimum useful power the windmill must deliver to the water. (b) If the rotor captures 20% of the wind's kinetic energy passing through its swept area, estimate the wind speed needed (use a swept area of 0.2 m² and air density 1.2 kg/m³).
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Mass of water m = 0.5 kg (500 mL, density 1000 kg/m³). Energy to lift = mgh = 0.5 × 10 × 1 = 5 J. This is done in 30 s, so useful power = 5/30 ≈ 0.17 W. (b) Wind power through area A = ½ρAv³. At 20% efficiency, useful power = 0.2 × ½ρAv³ = 0.1 × 1.2 × 0.2 × v³ = 0.024v³. Set 0.024v³ = 0.17, so v³ = 7.08, v ≈ 1.9 m/s. So a light breeze of about 2 m/s is enough — well within the range a school fan or a windy day provides.
2. **Q:** The same team must write three success criteria and a test plan. (a) Write three measurable success criteria for the windmill pump, each with a number and a pass/fail test. (b) Explain why the gradient of a "water lifted (mL) against time (s)" graph is a better success measure than "does it lift water?"
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Three criteria: (1) The rotor spins freely when blown by the fan at 2 m distance — pass if it completes 5 turns in 10 s. (2) The pump lifts at least 400 mL to 1 m in 30 s — pass if ≥ 400 mL reaches the 1 m mark. (3) The system runs for 60 s without the linkage slipping or the tower tipping — pass if no slip and no tip. Each is a number with a pass/fail. (b) The gradient (mL/s) is the flow rate — a continuous measure of how well the system works. "Does it lift water?" is a yes/no that gives no information about how much or how fast; two builds could both "lift water" but one lifts ten times more. The gradient lets the team compare builds and choose the better one, and it is what a real engineer would report.
3. **Q:** The team identifies the riskiest part as the blade-to-pump linkage. (a) Write a one-lesson prototype plan that tests only the linkage, using a hand crank instead of the full windmill. (b) State the result that would "retire" the risk, and the result that would force a pivot.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Prototype plan: build just the linkage — a crank arm on the rotor axle connected to a piston or a string-and-pulley that lifts a small cup of water. Drive the crank by hand at a steady rate (count turns per 10 s). Measure the water lifted. This isolates the linkage from the wind and the tower, so the team learns whether the linkage itself works. (b) Retire the risk: the hand-cranked linkage lifts at least 200 mL to 1 m in 30 s with no slip — the linkage design works, and the team can build the full windmill around it. Force a pivot: the linkage slips or jams repeatedly, or lifts almost nothing — the design is wrong, and the team must redesign the linkage (e.g., switch from a string to a rigid rod, or from a crank to a cam) before building the full system.

## Safety Notes
- This lesson is planning, but the menu table has real builds from earlier units. Inspect each for sharp edges, loose blades, and broken glass before learners handle them; mabati edges cut.
- If a team's "approved new idea" involves a new hazard (e.g., heat, high voltage, a motor), the teacher must approve the safety plan before the build lessons. Do not allow mains electricity, open flame, or pressurised containers in any capstone.
- Teams will move around to look at the menu table — keep the floor clear of bags and chairs to prevent trips.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson pulls together the whole year. Learners should know the four source builds from Units 13–15 (bridge, windmill, pump/siphon, instruments) and the practical-planning skill from Lesson 357. No new physics is introduced; the skill is project design and team planning.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on choosing a challenge, writing a one-sentence goal, and assigning roles; their success criteria can be simpler (e.g., "spins and lifts some water"). Ages 14–17 must write measurable success criteria, a numbered test plan, and the power/energy estimate in the Advanced Questions.
- The 15-minute Explore block is where the briefs are written — protect it. The teacher's job is to make goals specific and criteria measurable, not to choose for the teams. The struggle to write a measurable goal is the learning.
- Post the finished briefs on the wall; they are the entry ticket to Lesson 359. A team that arrives next lesson with no brief builds nothing that day.
- If a team cannot agree on a challenge, let them pick the windmill upgrade — it is the most scaffolded because the build already exists from Unit 14, so the risk of total failure is lowest for a wavering team.
- CBE assessment note: this lesson addresses G10-PHY-1.6 (design and carry out a physics investigation). Photograph the posted briefs for the portfolio as evidence of project-design skill. The briefs also give the teacher a record of each learner's role for fair grading of the team build.
