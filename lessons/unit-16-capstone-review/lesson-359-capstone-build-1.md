# Lesson 359: Capstone II: Prototype

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-1.3  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** capstone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Gather the materials listed in their capstone brief and confirm each item is available before building.
2. Build a working prototype of the single riskiest part of their project first, isolating it from the rest of the build.
3. Test the prototype against one success criterion and record the result honestly, whether it works or fails.
4. Log the riskiest assumption they made and the prototype result that retired it (or the pivot it forced).

## Materials
- Per team: the materials listed in their own brief from Lesson 358 (local, real objects — mabati offcuts, bicycle hubs, plastic pipe, string, jerricans, boards, wire, stones, bottles, etc.)
- The team's posted brief from Lesson 358, brought to the bench (the entry ticket)
- A printed prototype log per team: Riskiest part / Assumption / What we built / What we measured / Result (retired or pivot)
- A phone or stopwatch for timing tests
- A ruler or measuring tape and a marker pen
- A bucket or basin of water for any pump/siphon team
- A 5 kg weight or bag of sand for any bridge-plus team
- The teacher's check-in sheet (one row per team: brief present? risky part identified? prototype built? result logged?)

## Vocabulary
- **prototype** (mfano wa kwanza) — the first rough version of one part of the build, made to learn whether the idea works
- **riskiest assumption** (dhana yenye hatari kubwa) — the belief the team holds that, if wrong, breaks the whole project
- **retired** (imeondolewa) — a risk is retired when the prototype proves the assumption was right, so the team can build on it
- **pivot** (kubadilisha mwelekeo) — a change of design forced by a prototype failure; the team learns the first idea was wrong and tries a new one
- **isolate** (kuitenga) — testing one part alone, with the other parts held fixed or replaced by a simple stand-in (e.g., a hand crank for the wind)

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. In a capstone build, which part should you build and test first — the easiest part or the riskiest part? Why?
2. What does it mean to "retire a risk" with a prototype?
3. A team's riskiest part is the blade-to-pump linkage. What is one way to test the linkage alone, without the full windmill?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The riskiest part first. Building the risky part first means the team learns fast whether the whole idea is sound; if it fails, the team can pivot before spending time on the rest of the build. Building the easy parts first wastes effort if the risky part later fails and forces a redesign.
2. To retire a risk means to prove, with a prototype test, that the assumption behind the risky part was correct, so the team no longer worries about it and can build the full system on that foundation.
3. Accept any correct isolation method, e.g., drive the linkage by hand with a crank instead of the wind, so the wind and tower are removed from the test; or use a fixed weight pulling a string to stand in for the rotor's pull. The key is that only the linkage is tested, with the wind replaced by a controlled stand-in.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Check that every team has their brief on the bench (no brief, no build — they spend the lesson rewriting it).
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up a single string-and-pulley linkage and say: *"This is the riskiest part of one team's windmill pump. Today, every team builds only their riskiest part, not the whole project. If it works, you retire the risk. If it fails, you pivot. Either way, you learn before the full build next lesson."* Board the rule: *build the risky part first, test it against one success criterion, log the result.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teams gather their materials and build a prototype of the riskiest part only, isolated from the rest of the build. A windmill team builds just the linkage and drives it by hand; a bridge team builds just the joint they trust least and loads it; an instrument team builds just the one pipe or string they are unsure about and tests its pitch. The teacher moves between teams with the check-in sheet, prompting: *"What is your assumption? What will you measure? Did it retire the risk or force a pivot?"* **Checkpoint:** every team has built something and run at least one test before the Explain block.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — regroup and consolidate on the board. Draw the prototype log: Riskiest part / Assumption / What we built / What we measured / Result. Walk through one team's log as a worked example, whether it retired the risk or forced a pivot. Confront the misconception that *"a failed prototype means the project is over"* — a failed prototype is the most useful result, because it tells the team what to change before the full build in Lesson 360. A retired risk is good news; a forced pivot is also good news, because the team learned the first idea was wrong cheaply, in one lesson, not in two.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *build the risky part first, test it, log the result — retired or pivot.* Homework: each learner writes one sentence on what their team will change or keep for the full build in Lesson 360, based on today's prototype result. Bring the prototype log and the brief — both are the entry ticket to the full build.

## Hands-On Build: Prototype the Riskiest Part
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Teams bring their own materials per the brief.*

### Parts
- The materials each team listed in their Lesson 358 brief (per team)
- A printed prototype log per team
- A phone or stopwatch for timing
- A ruler or measuring tape and a marker pen
- A bucket or basin of water for pump/siphon teams
- A 5 kg weight or bag of sand for bridge teams
- A tuner app on a phone (optional) for instrument teams
- Stand-in drivers to isolate the risky part: a hand crank, a fixed weight on a string, a fan at a fixed distance — whatever replaces the part not yet built

### Workflow
1. Each team lays out their materials on the bench and checks each item against the brief. The teacher signs the check-in sheet that the brief is present and the materials match. **Checkpoint:** every material on the brief is on the bench; missing items are found or a substitute is agreed before building starts.
2. The team writes the riskiest part and the assumption on the prototype log, e.g., "Riskiest part: blade-to-pump linkage. Assumption: a string-and-pulley linkage lifts water when the rotor turns." **Checkpoint:** the riskiest part and the assumption are written before any building.
3. The team builds only the risky part, isolated from the rest of the build, using a stand-in driver if needed (a hand crank for the wind, a fixed weight for the rotor pull). **Checkpoint:** the prototype is the risky part alone, not the full build; the stand-in is in place.
4. The team tests the prototype against one success criterion from the brief and measures the result (e.g., water lifted in mL, load held in kg, frequency in Hz). **Checkpoint:** a number is measured and recorded, not just "it worked."
5. The team writes the result on the log: "Retired" if the assumption held and the success criterion was met, or "Pivot" if it failed, with one sentence on what to change. **Checkpoint:** the result box is filled with a word (retired or pivot) and a sentence.
6. The teacher checks the log and signs the check-in sheet. **Checkpoint:** every team has a signed log before the end of the Explore block.

### Common Mistakes
- **Building the whole project** — a team that builds everything at once has not isolated the risk and cannot tell which part failed. Fix: build only the risky part; use a stand-in for the rest.
- **No assumption written** — testing without stating the assumption means the team cannot tell if the result retired the risk. Fix: write the assumption on the log before building; the test is a check on that assumption.
- **"It worked" with no number** — a result with no measurement cannot be compared to the success criterion. Fix: measure the relevant quantity (mL, kg, Hz, turns) and write the number on the log.
- **Hiding a failure** — a team that says "it sort of worked" to avoid a pivot has learned nothing and will fail in the full build. Fix: the teacher asks, "did it meet the number in your success criterion?" If not, it is a pivot, and that is good news.
- **Testing with the wind instead of a stand-in** — a windmill team that uses the real wind to test the linkage cannot tell if a failure is the linkage or the wind. Fix: use a hand crank or a fixed weight to isolate the linkage.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. In a capstone build, which part should you build and test first — the easiest part or the riskiest part? Why? (mirrors pre-test)
2. What does it mean to "retire a risk" with a prototype? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A team's prototype test gives a number that does not meet their success criterion. Is this a retired risk or a pivot, and what should the team do next?
4. Why is "it worked" not an acceptable result on a prototype log? Give the missing element.
5. Challenge: a bridge team's riskiest part is the centre joint. Describe how they would isolate and test just that joint, and name the one number they would measure.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The riskiest part first. Building the risky part first lets the team learn fast whether the whole idea is sound; if it fails, they pivot before spending time on the rest. Building the easy parts first wastes effort if the risky part later fails.
2. To retire a risk means to prove, with a prototype test, that the assumption behind the risky part was correct, so the team can build the full system on that foundation without worrying about it.
3. It is a pivot. The number did not meet the success criterion, so the assumption was wrong. The team should write what to change on the log (the pivot) and redesign that part before the full build in Lesson 360. A pivot is not a failure of the team — it is a success of the prototype method.
4. "It worked" has no measured number. The missing element is the quantity compared to the success criterion (e.g., mL lifted, kg held, Hz measured). A result must be a number so the team can compare it to the target and decide retired or pivot.
5. Accept any correct isolation and measurement, e.g., the team builds just the centre joint — two beams joined at the middle — and supports the two outer ends on blocks, so only the joint carries the load. They hang a 5 kg weight (or bag of sand) from the centre joint and measure the sag at the centre with a ruler. The one number is the sag in mm under the load (or the load in kg at which the joint fails). The isolation is that only the joint is tested, with the rest of the bridge replaced by simple supports.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A windmill-pump team's riskiest part is the blade-to-pump linkage. Their assumption is that a string-and-pulley linkage driven by the rotor lifts water. They build the linkage, drive it by hand at a steady 1 turn per second, and measure 120 mL lifted to 1 m in 30 s. (a) Calculate the useful power delivered to the water. (b) State whether this retires the risk or forces a pivot, given their success criterion is "lift 400 mL to 1 m in 30 s with the hand crank."
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Mass lifted m = 0.12 kg (120 mL). Energy = mgh = 0.12 × 10 × 1 = 1.2 J in 30 s. Useful power = 1.2/30 = 0.04 W. (b) The success criterion is 400 mL in 30 s, but the prototype lifted only 120 mL. This is a pivot — the linkage as built is too weak or too lossy. The team must redesign: a larger crank radius (more stroke per turn), a bigger piston, or a rigid rod instead of a string to reduce slip and friction, then re-test before the full build.
2. **Q:** The team redesigns the linkage to a rigid rod with a larger crank radius, doubling the stroke per turn. Assuming the losses stay the same fraction, predict the new water lifted per turn, and state the new useful power if they keep 1 turn per second.
   **Worked Answer:** Doubling the stroke doubles the volume per turn (the piston sweeps twice the length, so twice the water per cycle), so the water lifted per turn doubles from 120/30 = 4 mL/turn to 8 mL/turn. At 1 turn per second for 30 s, that is 8 × 30 = 240 mL in 30 s. New useful power = (0.24 kg × 10 × 1)/30 = 2.4/30 = 0.08 W. This is still below 400 mL, so the team may need a bigger piston or a faster crank — but the redesign has roughly doubled the output, which is a clear improvement and a justified pivot result to log.
3. **Q:** Log the riskiest assumption and the prototype result that retired it (or forced the pivot) for a bridge team whose riskiest part is the centre joint. Their assumption is that a nailed lap joint holds a 5 kg load at the centre without failing. They build the joint, support the outer ends, and load it: it holds 3 kg and then the nail pulls out. Write the full log entry and state the pivot.
   **Worked Answer:** Log entry — Riskiest part: centre joint (nailed lap joint). Assumption: a single nailed lap joint holds a 5 kg centre load. What we built: two beams joined with one nail at the centre, outer ends on blocks. What we measured: the joint held 3 kg, then the nail pulled out (failed at 3 kg). Result: PIVOT. The assumption was wrong — a single nail in a lap joint is not strong enough for 5 kg. The pivot: add a second nail or a bolt, or change to a gusset plate (a small triangle of plywood or mabati nailed over the joint on both sides) to spread the load, then re-test. The log records that the risk is not retired; the joint must be redesigned before the full bridge build in Lesson 360.

## Safety Notes
- Tools are in use: hammers, pliers, files, hand drills, heated nails. The teacher supervises all tool use; no learner uses a tool they have not been shown how to use safely. Wear eye protection if available when hammering or cutting wire.
- Wire under tension and blades can snap and whip. Inspect all wire and fishing line for nicks before tensioning; do not lean over a tensioned wire; keep faces back.
- Water for pump/siphon teams is a slip hazard on the floor. Keep basins on a bench or a tray; mop spills immediately; do not let learners carry open buckets across the room.
- A 5 kg weight or bag of sand dropped on a foot is an injury. Load bridge joints over a soft surface (a folded cloth or a tray of sand); do not stand under a loaded test.
- Mabati edges and cut plastic are sharp. File edges flat before handling; sweep up offcuts; no barefoot building.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson follows the capstone brief (Lesson 358). Teams arrive with a brief, a riskiest part, and a materials list. The skill is prototyping the risky part first and logging the result — the core of G10-PHY-1.6 (design and carry out) and G10-PHY-1.3 (evaluate and improve).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on building the risky part, running one test, and writing "retired" or "pivot" with a simple reason. Ages 14–17 must write the assumption explicitly, measure a number, and do the power or load calculations in the Advanced Questions.
- The 15-minute Explore block is the build — protect it. The teacher's job is the check-in sheet: brief present, risky part identified, prototype built, result logged. A team building the whole project is redirected to the risky part only.
- A team that retires its risk early should start the full build of the safe parts — but only after the log is signed. A team that pivots should redesign the risky part and re-test if time allows, or log the pivot and redesign next lesson.
- Store each team's prototype and log safely between lessons; label them with the team name. The log is the entry ticket to the full build in Lesson 360 — a team with no log builds blind.
- CBE assessment note: the prototype log is direct evidence of G10-PHY-1.3 (evaluate and improve) and the "fail fast" engineering disposition. Photograph the logs for the portfolio and note which teams pivoted and why — the pivots are often the richest learning and the best evidence of honest evaluation.
