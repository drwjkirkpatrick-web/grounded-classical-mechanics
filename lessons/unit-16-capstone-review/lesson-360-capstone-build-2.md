# Lesson 360: Capstone III: Build and Test

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** capstone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Assemble the full capstone build, integrating the prototyped risky part with the remaining components from the brief.
2. Execute the data collection plan from the brief — measure each success criterion and record the result with units and repeats.
3. Document the first failure honestly, stating what failed, what the measured data showed, and what the team changed.
4. Write a short mid-build report comparing what the data says to the brief, and naming the pivot they made and why.

## Materials
- Per team: the materials listed in their own brief (from Lesson 358), plus their prototype from Lesson 359
- The team's brief and their prototype log from Lesson 359 (the entry tickets)
- A printed data sheet per team, with a row for each success criterion: Criterion / Measurement 1 / Measurement 2 / Measurement 3 / Mean / Pass or fail
- A printed mid-build report per team: What the data says / What the brief said / The pivot we made / Why
- A phone or stopwatch for timing; a ruler or measuring tape and a marker pen
- A bucket or basin of water for pump/siphon teams; a 5 kg weight or bag of sand for bridge teams
- A fan or a windy window for windmill teams; a tuner app (optional) for instrument teams
- The teacher's check-in sheet (one row per team: brief + log present? full build assembled? data collected? report written?)

## Vocabulary
- **full build** (ujengo kamili) — the complete capstone, integrating the prototyped part with the rest of the system from the brief
- **data collection plan** (mpango wa kukusanya data) — the steps in the brief for measuring each success criterion, including repeats
- **mean** (wastani) — the average of repeated measurements, found by adding the values and dividing by the number of readings
- **first failure** (kushindwa kwa kwanza) — the first time the build breaks, slips, or misses a target during testing; it is recorded, not hidden
- **pivot** (kubadilisha mwelekeo) — a change made during the build or test in response to what the data showed
- **mid-build report** (ripoti ya katikati ya ujenzi) — a short written comparison of the data to the brief, with the pivot and the reason

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Why is it important to repeat a measurement at least three times during a build test?
2. Your build fails its first test — it lifts only 100 mL of water, but the brief says 400 mL. Should you hide this or report it, and why?
3. In one sentence, what is a mid-build report for?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Repeating a measurement at least three times lets the team spot outliers (one-off errors) and take a mean, which is more reliable than a single reading. A single reading could be a fluke; three readings show whether the result is repeatable.
2. Report it. A failure is data — it tells the team what to fix. Hiding it means the team builds on a false result and will fail later when it matters. An honest first failure is the most useful information in a build lesson, because it points straight to the part to change (the pivot).
3. A mid-build report compares what the data actually shows to what the brief expected, and records the change (pivot) the team made and the reason — so the team and the teacher can see whether the build is on track to meet the goal.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips. Check that every team has their brief and their Lesson 359 prototype log on the bench (no log, no build — they spend the lesson finishing the prototype).
2. **Engage (5 min)** — point at one team's prototype and say: *"Last lesson you proved the risky part works (or you pivoted it). Today you build the whole system and test it against your success criteria. You will measure three times, take the mean, and write a mid-build report. The first failure is not a disaster — it is data. Record it honestly."* Board the rule: *assemble, test, measure three times, mean, report — and pivot if the data says so.*
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teams assemble the full build, integrating the prototyped part with the remaining components. Then they run their data collection plan: for each success criterion, they measure three times, record the values, and compute the mean. When (not if) the first failure happens, they record what failed and what the data showed, then make a pivot and re-test if time allows. The teacher moves between teams with the check-in sheet, prompting: *"What does the data say? Does it meet the criterion? What will you change?"* **Checkpoint:** every team has a full build assembled and at least one success criterion measured three times before the Explain block.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — regroup and consolidate on the board. Draw the mid-build report: What the data says / What the brief said / The pivot we made / Why. Walk through one team's report as a worked example — include a team that failed and pivoted. Confront the misconception that *"a successful build is one that works the first time"* — almost no real engineering project works the first time. The mark of a good build is that the team measured honestly, saw the gap between data and brief, made a reasoned pivot, and can explain it. A build that "worked" with no measurement and no recorded failure is weaker evidence than a build that failed, was measured, and was improved.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *measure three times, take the mean, compare to the brief, pivot with a reason.* Homework: each learner writes one paragraph on the single biggest thing their team learned from the whole capstone (brief, prototype, build and test), to bring to the community science day. This is the capstone's closing reflection.

## Hands-On Build: Full Build and Test
*Build time fits inside the Explore block. Teams bring their own materials, prototype, and logs per the brief.*

### Parts
- The materials each team listed in their Lesson 358 brief, plus their Lesson 359 prototype (per team)
- A printed data sheet with a row per success criterion and columns for three measurements, the mean, and pass/fail
- A printed mid-build report form
- A phone or stopwatch for timing
- A ruler or measuring tape and a marker pen
- A bucket or basin of water for pump/siphon teams
- A 5 kg weight or bag of sand for bridge teams
- A fan or a windy window for windmill teams
- A tuner app on a phone (optional) for instrument teams
- A first-aid kit on the bench (tools and loads are in use)

### Workflow
1. Each team lays out their materials and their prototype on the bench, and the teacher checks the brief and the Lesson 359 log are present. **Checkpoint:** brief and log are on the bench; missing teams finish the prototype first.
2. The team assembles the full build, integrating the prototyped part with the remaining components (tower, blades, pipes, bridge deck, instrument bodies, etc.). **Checkpoint:** the full build stands or holds together without the team holding it; the prototyped part is connected, not rebuilt from scratch.
3. The team runs the data collection plan: for the first success criterion, they measure three times and record each value on the data sheet. **Checkpoint:** three values are written with units for the first criterion.
4. The team computes the mean of the three values and writes it in the mean column, then marks pass or fail against the criterion from the brief. **Checkpoint:** the mean is calculated and the pass/fail is marked, not guessed.
5. When the first failure happens (a slip, a break, a missed target), the team records what failed and what the data showed on the mid-build report, then makes a pivot (a change in response to the data) and re-tests if time allows. **Checkpoint:** the failure is recorded honestly, not hidden; the pivot is written with a reason.
6. The team writes the mid-build report: what the data says, what the brief said, the pivot they made, and why. **Checkpoint:** all four sections of the report are filled before the end of the Explore block.
7. The teacher checks the data sheet and the report and signs the check-in sheet. **Checkpoint:** every team has a signed data sheet and a signed mid-build report.

### Common Mistakes
- **Testing once and calling it done** — a single reading may be an outlier. Fix: measure three times, record each value, and take the mean; the data sheet has three columns for this reason.
- **Hiding the first failure** — a team that quietly fixes a break without recording it loses the best evidence of learning. Fix: the teacher asks, "what failed first, and what did the data show?" — the failure goes on the report.
- **No units on the data** — "400" without mL or kg or Hz is meaningless and cannot be compared to the brief. Fix: write units with every measurement; the data sheet headings include the unit.
- **Pivoting without a reason** — a change made on a hunch is not engineering. Fix: the pivot must be written with the reason, e.g., "the linkage slipped, so we switched from string to a rigid rod to reduce slip."
- **Rebuilding the prototype from scratch** — a team that ignores Lesson 359 and starts over wastes half the build time. Fix: integrate the prototyped part; connect it to the rest, do not rebuild it.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. Why is it important to repeat a measurement at least three times during a build test? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Your build fails its first test — it lifts only 100 mL of water, but the brief says 400 mL. Should you hide this or report it, and why? (mirrors pre-test)
3. A team measures their windmill's lift as 380 mL, 420 mL, and 400 mL. Calculate the mean and state whether it passes a 400 mL success criterion.
4. A bridge holds 4 kg and then breaks, but the brief said 5 kg. Is this a pass, a fail, or a pivot, and what should the team write on the mid-build report?
5. Challenge: write the four sections of a mid-build report for a team whose windmill lifted a mean of 250 mL (brief: 400 mL) because the linkage slipped, and who then switched to a rigid rod and re-tested.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Repeating a measurement at least three times lets the team spot outliers and take a mean, which is more reliable than a single reading. A single reading could be a fluke; three readings show whether the result is repeatable.
2. Report it. A failure is data — it tells the team what to fix. Hiding it means the team builds on a false result and will fail later. An honest first failure is the most useful information in a build lesson, because it points to the part to change.
3. Mean = (380 + 420 + 400)/3 = 1200/3 = 400 mL. It passes the 400 mL success criterion (the mean is exactly 400 mL; accept "passes, equal to the target").
4. It is a fail (it did not meet the 5 kg criterion), and it is also a pivot — the team must change the design to hold more. On the mid-build report, they write: what the data says (held 4 kg, then broke); what the brief said (hold 5 kg); the pivot they made (e.g., reinforce the centre joint with a gusset plate); why (the joint failed at 4 kg, below the 5 kg target, so it must be strengthened).
5. Accept a report with all four sections filled, e.g.,: What the data says: the windmill lifted a mean of 250 mL, below the 400 mL target. What the brief said: lift 400 mL in 30 s. The pivot we made: we replaced the string linkage with a rigid rod. Why: the string slipped under load, wasting the rotor's pull; a rigid rod transmits the pull without slip, so more of the rotor's work goes into lifting the water. (Award marks for all four sections present and the reason tied to the data, not a hunch.)

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** A windmill team measures the water lifted in three trials as 250 mL, 270 mL, and 230 mL in 30 s each. (a) Calculate the mean and the useful power delivered to the water. (b) Their brief target is 400 mL in 30 s. State whether the build meets the target, and give two quantitative reasons the team might give for the shortfall in their mid-build report.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Mean = (250 + 270 + 230)/3 = 750/3 = 250 mL = 0.25 kg. Energy = mgh = 0.25 × 10 × 1 = 2.5 J. Useful power = 2.5/30 = 0.083 W. (b) The build does not meet the 400 mL target (mean is 250 mL, 62.5% of target). Two quantitative reasons: (1) the linkage slipped, so only 250 mL of the intended stroke reached the water — compare to the 400 mL target, a shortfall of 150 mL or 37.5%; (2) the rotor turned at only 0.5 turns/s instead of the 1 turn/s assumed in the brief, halving the expected flow. Each reason is tied to a measured number, not a guess.
2. **Q:** The team pivots by switching the string linkage to a rigid rod and re-tests: 380 mL, 420 mL, 400 mL. (a) Calculate the new mean and useful power. (b) Calculate the percentage improvement in flow rate from the first build to the second. (c) State, with reference to the uncertainty, whether the new build "passes" the 400 mL target.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) New mean = (380 + 420 + 400)/3 = 1200/3 = 400 mL = 0.40 kg. Energy = 0.40 × 10 × 1 = 4.0 J. Useful power = 4.0/30 = 0.133 W. (b) Improvement in flow rate = (400 − 250)/250 × 100% = 150/250 × 100% = 60% increase. (c) The new mean is exactly 400 mL, which meets the target. However, the three readings range from 380 to 420 mL, a spread of ±20 mL about the mean (about ±5%). So the build passes, but the team should note the ±20 mL scatter in the report — the result is on the boundary, and reducing the scatter (e.g., steadier wind or a smoother crank) would make the pass more convincing.
3. **Q:** Write the full mid-build report for a bridge team whose brief target is "hold 5 kg at the centre for 10 s." Their first test held 3 kg and the nail pulled out. They pivoted by adding a plywood gusset on both sides of the joint and re-tested: it held 6 kg for 10 s. (a) Fill in all four sections. (b) Calculate the percentage by which the pivoted build exceeded the target, and state one further improvement the team could make to raise the safety margin.
   **Worked Answer:** (a) Mid-build report — What the data says: first test held 3 kg, then the nail pulled out (failed at 3 kg); after the pivot, the build held 6 kg for 10 s. What the brief said: hold 5 kg at the centre for 10 s. The pivot we made: added a plywood gusset plate nailed over the centre joint on both sides. Why: the single nail in a lap joint pulled out at 3 kg, well below the 5 kg target, because the nail carried the whole load in shear; the gusset spreads the load across a larger area and into the beams, raising the failure load. (b) The pivoted build held 6 kg vs. a 5 kg target, an excess of (6 − 5)/5 × 100% = 20% above target. One further improvement: add a second gusset or a bolt through the joint to raise the failure load further, giving a larger safety margin (a real bridge is designed to hold well above its rated load, so the team should aim for, say, 7–8 kg before failure, not just 5 kg).

## Safety Notes
- Tools, loads, and water are all in use in the same lesson — this is the highest-risk lesson of the capstone. The teacher must be on the floor the whole time; no team tests a loaded or tensioned build without the teacher watching.
- Wire under tension and blades can snap and whip. Inspect all wire and fishing line before the final tension; do not lean over a tensioned wire; keep faces back; wear eye protection if available when tensioning or cutting.
- A 5 kg weight or bag of sand dropped on a foot is an injury. Load bridge tests over a soft surface (a tray of sand or a folded cloth); do not stand under a loaded test; keep feet clear of the drop zone.
- Water for pump/siphon teams is a slip hazard. Keep basins on a bench or a tray; mop spills immediately; do not carry open buckets across the room; no barefoot building.
- Mabati edges and cut plastic are sharp. File edges flat before handling; sweep up offcuts; keep a first-aid kit on the bench and know who is the designated first-aider for the lesson.
- If a team's pivot involves a new hazard (e.g., heat, a new tool), the teacher checks the safety before the pivot is built, not after.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson follows the capstone prototype (Lesson 359). Teams arrive with a brief, a prototype log, and a prototyped risky part. The skill is full assembly, honest data collection with repeats and a mean, and a reasoned mid-build report — the core of G10-PHY-1.6 (design and carry out) and G10-PHY-1.1 (measurement and fair testing).
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on assembling the build, measuring three times, and writing pass/fail with a simple reason. Ages 14–17 must take the mean, do the power or load calculations, and write the four-section mid-build report with the quantitative reason for the pivot.
- The 15-minute Explore block is the build and test — protect it. The teacher's job is the check-in sheet and safety, not building for the teams. A team that hides a failure is redirected to record it; a team that pivots without a reason is asked, "what does the data say?"
- A team that finishes early should re-test a second success criterion or improve their safety margin — the brief has three criteria, and one is rarely enough in a real test.
- Store each team's build, data sheet, and mid-build report safely; these are the capstone artifacts for the community science day. The build is demonstrated, the data sheet is the evidence, and the mid-build report is the story of the engineering.
- CBE assessment note: this lesson is the culmination of G10-PHY-1.6 and G10-PHY-1.1. The data sheet (with three repeats and a mean) is evidence of fair measurement; the mid-build report (with an honest first failure and a reasoned pivot) is evidence of evaluation and improvement. Photograph the builds, the data sheets, and the reports for the portfolio, and note which teams pivoted and why — the pivots are the strongest evidence of honest, data-driven engineering.
