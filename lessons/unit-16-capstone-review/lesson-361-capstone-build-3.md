# Lesson 361: Capstone IV: Iterate and Document

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** capstone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Plan and execute one measured improvement cycle on their capstone build, changing only one variable at a time.
2. Complete project documentation with a labelled diagram, a data table, and a before-and-after comparison of performance.
3. Report final numbers for performance, efficiency, and cost, and justify whether the improvement was worthwhile.
4. Draft a realistic maintenance plan that names the parts most likely to wear and how often they need attention.

## Materials
- Per team: the capstone build from Lesson 360 (windmill, pump/siphon, bridge, or instrument ensemble)
- Per team: data log and brief from Lessons 358–360
- Spare materials for one improvement (blades, string, weights, sandpaper, wax, glue, tape)
- Tools: screwdriver, pliers, adjustable spanner, scissors
- Stopwatch, measuring jar or ruler, scale (if applicable to the build)
- Notebooks and pencils for documentation
- Camera or phone (one per team, if available) for build photos
- Graph paper or plain paper for diagrams
- Data table template (trial | change | measurement 1 | measurement 2 | notes)

## Vocabulary
- **iteration** (marudio) — making one change, testing it, and recording what happened before the next change
- **documentation** (nyaraka) — a written and drawn record of what was built, how it was tested, and what the results were
- **data table** (jedwali la data) — a grid of rows and columns that organises measurements so they can be compared
- **efficiency** (ufanisi) — useful output divided by total input; tells you how much effort is wasted
- **maintenance** (matengenezo) — regular checks and repairs that keep a build working over time
- **labelled diagram** (chorongo alama) — a drawing with parts named and measurements written so someone else could rebuild it

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. Your capstone team wants to improve the windmill. You bend the blades AND add a tail weight in the same session. The output goes up. Can you tell which change helped? Why or why not?
2. What three things must a complete data table include for someone else to understand your results?
3. Why does a real machine like a windmill or pump need a maintenance plan, not just a build plan?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. No — you changed two variables at once, so you cannot tell which one (or which combination) caused the improvement. Change only one thing per iteration, test, then decide.
2. A clear heading, units on every measurement, and a notes column explaining anything unusual about that trial.
3. Because parts wear out (blades crack, bearings rust, ropes fray). A maintenance plan names what to check and how often, so the machine keeps working instead of breaking down and being abandoned.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips; teams sit with their builds and logs from Lesson 360.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — show one team's build and ask: *what is the single weakest part of this build right now?* Take two answers. Introduce today's two goals: (a) make ONE measured improvement, (b) finish the documentation so a stranger could understand what you did. Remind teams that the Community Science Day (Lesson 363) is near — the documentation is what they will show guests.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — each team picks one improvement cycle based on their Lesson 360 data: change one variable, run a before-and-after test, and log the results. Simultaneously, one team member drafts the labelled diagram while another completes the data table. Photograph the build before and after the change.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — teams post their data table and diagram on the board. Confront the misconception that *if the output went up, the improvement worked* — ask whether wind, water level, or measurement error could explain the change. Show how a fair comparison needs the same conditions before and after. Then introduce the maintenance plan: which part will fail first, how would you know, and what would you do?
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *one change, fair test, document everything*. Homework: finish the labelled diagram and data table at home if not complete; write the maintenance plan as three bullet points.

## Hands-On Build: One Improvement Cycle and Documentation
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Capstone build from Lesson 360
- Spare materials for one improvement (team's choice)
- Tools and measuring instruments
- Notebook, graph paper, pencil
- Camera or phone (if available)
- Data table template

### Workflow
1. Review the Lesson 360 data as a team. Agree on the single weakest point and write the one change you will test today: "We change _______." Do not touch anything else. **Checkpoint:** only one variable is changed; all other parts stay the same.
2. Run a "before" trial with the current build. Record the measurement (water volume, load held, voltage, or note held) with units. Photograph the build. **Checkpoint:** the before measurement and photo are logged before any change is made.
3. Apply the one improvement. Run an "after" trial under the same conditions. Record the measurement with units. Photograph the modified build. **Checkpoint:** the after measurement uses the same instrument and method as the before.
4. Complete the data table: heading, units on every column, before and after rows, and a notes column. Draw the labelled diagram with all parts named and key measurements written. **Checkpoint:** a stranger looking at the table and diagram could tell what was built, what changed, and what happened.
5. Compute performance and efficiency if applicable (e.g., water per minute, load per gram of material, useful output ÷ input). Write the final numbers on the documentation. **Checkpoint:** efficiency has a unit and is compared before vs after.
6. Draft the maintenance plan: name the part most likely to fail first, how you would detect it, and what action to take. **Checkpoint:** the plan names a specific part and a check interval (e.g., "check bearings weekly, oil if squeaking").

### Common Mistakes
- **Changing two things at once** — if you add a tail weight and wax the bearing in the same session, you cannot attribute the result to either. Pick one, test, then decide on the next.
- **Skipping the "before" trial** — without a baseline measurement under today's conditions, you cannot prove the improvement did anything. Always log before first.
- **Diagram without measurements** — a drawing with no numbers is not documentation. Label lengths, angles, and masses so someone else could rebuild it.
- **Data table without units** — "water = 40" means nothing. Write "40 mL" or "40" with "mL" in the column heading.
- **Maintenance plan that says "check everything"** — that is not a plan. Name the specific part, the sign of failure, and the action.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. Your capstone team wants to improve the windmill. You bend the blades AND add a tail weight in the same session. The output goes up. Can you tell which change helped? Why or why not?
2. What three things must a complete data table include for someone else to understand your results?
3. Your "before" trial produced 120 mL of water and your "after" trial produced 150 mL. However, the wind was stronger during the "after" trial. Can you conclude the improvement worked? What should you do?
4. Name two parts of your capstone build that are most likely to wear out, and for each, state one maintenance action.
5. Challenge: your improvement made the build 20% more powerful but added 50% more material cost. Was it worth it? Explain how you would decide.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. No — two variables changed at once, so the improvement could be from either one, or from both together. To know, change only one variable per iteration and test again.
2. A clear heading, units on every measurement, and a notes column explaining anything unusual about that trial.
3. No — the stronger wind may have caused the higher output regardless of the improvement. You must compare at the same wind speed or normalise the output by dividing by wind speed. Run a fair re-test with matched conditions.
4. Answers depend on the build, but should name specific parts (e.g., bearings — oil weekly; blades — check for cracks monthly; rope — replace when frayed; joints — re-glue if loose).
5. It depends on the purpose. If the build is for a competition where maximum power matters, the 20% gain may be worth the 50% cost. If the build is for a community where cost matters (e.g., a low-cost water pump), the extra cost may not be justified. The decision requires knowing the goal stated in the brief and comparing the benefit (performance) against the cost trade-off.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your capstone windmill's "before" run delivered 120 mL in 60 seconds at 4 m/s wind. The "after" run delivered 150 mL in 60 seconds at 4.5 m/s wind. Calculate the normalised output (mL per m/s of wind) for each. Is the improvement real, or is it explained by the wind increase alone? Recall that wind power is proportional to v³.
   **Worked Answer:** Normalised "before" = 120 mL ÷ 4 m/s = 30 mL·s⁻¹·m⁻¹·s. Normalised "after" = 150 mL ÷ 4.5 m/s = 33.3 mL·s⁻¹·m⁻¹·s. The normalised output improved by about 11%. Now check the wind-power effect: (4.5/4)³ = (1.125)³ = 1.424, so available wind power rose by about 42%. If the windmill efficiency had stayed the same, the output should have risen by ~42% from wind alone, giving 120 × 1.42 = 170 mL. The actual output was only 150 mL, which is less than 170 mL. So after accounting for wind, the windmill actually performed worse — the apparent improvement was entirely due to the stronger wind, and the modification may have reduced efficiency.
2. **Q:** Write a complete documentation package for your capstone build. List every section it must contain, and for each section give one sentence describing what goes there.
   **Worked Answer:** (1) Problem statement — one sentence stating what the build is meant to solve. (2) Physics principles — the key equations or concepts the build relies on (e.g., wind power ∝ ½ρAv³, mechanical advantage). (3) Labelled diagram — a drawing with all parts named and key dimensions and angles marked. (4) Materials and cost — a table listing every part, its source, and its cost in KSh. (5) Data table — before-and-after measurements with units and a notes column. (6) Results — final numbers for performance (e.g., water per minute) and efficiency (useful output ÷ input). (7) Improvement log — what was changed, why, and the measured effect. (8) Maintenance plan — the part most likely to fail, the sign of failure, and the check interval and action.
3. **Q:** Your capstone pump costs 850 KSh to build. It lifts 12 L of water per hour to a height of 3 m. The useful power output is P = ρgQh. Calculate the useful power in watts, then estimate the cost per watt of useful pumping power. Is this competitive with a commercial pump that costs 6,000 KSh and delivers 60 W?
   **Worked Answer:** Flow rate Q = 12 L/h = 12 × 10⁻³ m³ / 3600 s = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m³/s. Useful power = ρgQh = (1000 kg/m³)(9.81 m/s²)(3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m³/s)(3 m) = 0.098 W ≈ 0.1 W. Cost per watt = 850 KSh ÷ 0.1 W = 8,500 KSh/W. Commercial pump: 6,000 KSh ÷ 60 W = 100 KSh/W. The capstone is 85 times more expensive per watt of useful power. However, the capstone uses free wind energy and has no fuel cost, while the commercial pump needs electricity or fuel. A fair comparison must include operating cost over time, not just the build cost. The maintenance plan should also factor in: if the capstone needs 2 hours of repair per month, the labour cost may further reduce its advantage.

## Safety Notes
- Maintain a 2 m safety perimeter around any build with moving parts (windmill rotor, pump linkage) during testing.
- Stop all moving parts before adjusting the build. Never reach into a rotor plane or pump mechanism while the build is running.
- Sharp edges on cut plastic or metal can cut hands. Sand edges smooth before handling; carry spare blades flat.
- Adhesives and wax can be hot if heated — let cool before touching. Use glue in a ventilated area.
- If testing near water (pump or siphon builds), keep electrical devices (phones used as cameras) away from water. Dry hands before handling electronics.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners need their capstone build and data log from Lessons 358–360, plus the A/B testing method from Lesson 314 (windmill iteration) and the documentation habits built throughout the course.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on making one visible change, recording before-and-after measurements with units, and drawing a labelled diagram with part names. Ages 14–17 complete the full documentation package including efficiency calculation, cost analysis, and a maintenance plan with failure-mode reasoning.
- Local resource tips: phones can substitute for cameras. Graph paper can be made by ruling plain paper. Spare materials come from previous units' leftovers — encourage reuse, not new purchase.
- The key habit is single-variable change paired with honest documentation. Resist letting teams "improve" by changing everything — if they do, the data is useless and the documentation becomes fiction.
- Save all documentation — it feeds directly into Lesson 362 (poster and rehearsal) and is the primary material teams will show guests at the Community Science Day (Lesson 363).
- If a team's improvement fails (output went down), that is a valid result — document it honestly and explain why. A failed improvement with good data is better than a fake success.
