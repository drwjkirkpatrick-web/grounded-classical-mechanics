# Lesson 362: Capstone V: Poster and Rehearsal

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** capstone

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Design a capstone poster that communicates the problem, the physics, the data, and the result in a clear visual layout.
2. Deliver a 3-minute demonstration of their capstone build, timed and structured so a guest can follow it from start to finish.
3. Give and receive specific, actionable peer feedback using a structured rubric (clarity, physics accuracy, timing, visuals).
4. Anticipate the two hardest audience questions for their project and prepare credible answers grounded in their data.

## Materials
- Poster paper (one large sheet per team, or two joined A3 sheets)
- Markers ( assorted colours, at least 3 per team)
- Ruler and pencil for layout lines
- Documentation from Lesson 361: labelled diagram, data table, final numbers
- Phone or stopwatch for timing rehearsals
- Peer feedback rubric (clarity | physics accuracy | timing | visuals — each scored 1–3)
- The capstone build itself, brought to the classroom for the rehearsal demo
- Sticky notes for written feedback (one pad per team)

## Vocabulary
- **poster** (bango) — a single large sheet that tells the story of a project visually, so a viewer can understand it in under one minute
- **demonstration** (maonyesho) — a live, timed showing of the build working, with the presenter explaining what is happening
- **rehearsal** (mazoezi) — practising the presentation out loud, with timing, before the real audience arrives
- **peer feedback** (maoni ya wanafunzi wenzako) — specific comments from classmates on what worked and what to improve, given using a rubric
- **rubric** (kiwango cha kupima) — a scoring guide that names what is being judged and what each score level means
- **audience question** (swali la hadhira) — a question a visitor might ask after the demo; preparing answers shows you understand your own project

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A visitor walks up to your poster and has 30 seconds to understand your project. What four pieces of information must be on the poster for them to get the idea?
2. Your demo runs for 5 minutes. The audience is getting restless. What is the problem, and what is one fix?
3. What makes peer feedback useful, as opposed to just saying "it was good"?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. The four pieces: the problem (what you set out to solve), the physics (the key principle or equation), the data (your best result with units), and the conclusion (did it work and what you would improve).
2. The demo is too long — 3 minutes is the target. Fix: cut the detail, keep the demo live, and save the deep explanation for the poster and audience questions.
3. Useful peer feedback is specific and uses the rubric: it names what worked (e.g., "your data table had units") and what to fix (e.g., "speak louder and cut the intro to 20 seconds"), rather than a vague "it was good."

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips; teams gather their Lesson 361 documentation and materials.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — hold up one team's data table and ask: *if this was all a visitor saw, would they understand your project?* Most will say no — they need the story, not just numbers. Introduce the poster as the story-maker and the demo as the proof. Show a quick sketch of a poster layout on the board: title at top, problem on the left, physics in the middle, data on the right, conclusion at the bottom.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — teams build their posters using the Lesson 361 documentation: problem statement, the key physics principle, the labelled diagram, the data table with final numbers, and a one-line conclusion. Then each team rehearses its 3-minute demo out loud once. The teacher times one team to model the 3-minute target. Teams photograph their finished posters.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — pairs of teams swap posters and watch each other's rehearsal demo. They fill the peer feedback rubric (clarity, physics accuracy, timing, visuals — each 1–3) and write one sticky-note suggestion. Confront the misconception that *a good demo is a long demo* — the best demos are short, clear, and let the build speak. Show how a 3-minute structure works: 30 seconds problem, 60 seconds physics + diagram, 60 seconds live demo, 30 seconds result and conclusion.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *poster tells the story, demo proves it, keep it to 3 minutes*. Homework: revise the poster using peer feedback and write the two hardest audience questions with answers on the back of the poster.

## Hands-On Build: Capstone Poster and Demo Rehearsal
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- Poster paper (one large sheet or two joined A3 sheets per team)
- Markers, ruler, pencil
- Lesson 361 documentation: diagram, data table, final numbers
- The capstone build
- Stopwatch or phone timer
- Peer feedback rubric and sticky notes

### Workflow
1. Sketch the poster layout in pencil first: title bar at the top, four quadrants below (problem, physics + diagram, data table, conclusion). **Checkpoint:** all four quadrants are outlined before any marker is used.
2. Fill each quadrant from the Lesson 361 documentation: write the problem in one sentence, the key physics principle or equation, copy the labelled diagram, copy the data table with units, and write a one-line conclusion. Use colour to separate sections. **Checkpoint:** a visitor standing 1 m away can read every word and see the diagram.
3. Stand the poster beside the build. Rehearse the 3-minute demo out loud: 30 s problem, 60 s physics and diagram, 60 s live build demo, 30 s result and conclusion. Time it with a phone. **Checkpoint:** the rehearsal finishes within 3 minutes ± 15 seconds.
4. Swap with a partner team. Watch their full demo. Score the rubric (clarity, physics accuracy, timing, visuals — each 1–3) and write one sticky-note suggestion. Receive their feedback on your team. **Checkpoint:** the rubric is fully scored and the sticky note names one specific change.
5. Revise the poster based on feedback: fix unreadable text, correct physics errors, and adjust the demo timing. **Checkpoint:** the revised poster and demo address every sticky-note suggestion.
6. On the back of the poster, write the two hardest questions a visitor could ask about your project and a one-sentence answer to each, grounded in your data. **Checkpoint:** each answer cites a number or observation from the data table.

### Common Mistakes
- **Too much text on the poster** — a poster is not a report. If a section has more than three sentences, cut it. The demo and audience questions carry the detail.
- **No live demo, only talk** — visitors want to see the build work. If the build cannot run in the classroom, show a video or photograph and say so, but always attempt the live demo first.
- **Running over 3 minutes** — the biggest risk. Time the rehearsal and cut sections that run long. The conclusion can always be shortened.
- **Ignoring peer feedback** — the sticky notes are the most valuable part. A team that does not revise after feedback will get the same complaints from guests on Science Day.
- **Audience answers without data** — "it works because we tested it" is not an answer. Cite the number: "our data table shows 150 mL per minute at 4 m/s wind, up from 120 mL before the improvement."

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. A visitor walks up to your poster and has 30 seconds to understand your project. What four pieces of information must be on the poster for them to get the idea?
2. Your demo runs for 5 minutes. The audience is getting restless. What is the problem, and what is one fix?
3. During peer feedback, a classmate says your physics explanation is wrong — your windmill does not use "air pressure" to spin, it uses lift. What should you do, and why does this matter before Science Day?
4. Your 3-minute demo structure is: 30 s problem, 60 s physics, 60 s live demo, 30 s result. Total: 180 s. You also need 20 s to switch the build on. Where do you find the 20 s without cutting content?
5. Challenge: a parent asks, "Why did you build this instead of buying a real pump?" Give a structured answer using the problem, the physics, and the result from your poster.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. The problem, the physics (key principle or equation), the data (best result with units), and the conclusion (did it work and what to improve).
2. The demo is too long. Fix: cut to the 3-minute structure (30 s problem, 60 s physics, 60 s demo, 30 s result) and save detail for the poster and questions.
3. Check your physics immediately — if the classmate is right, correct the poster and the demo script before Science Day so you do not repeat the error to parents and fundis. Accurate physics is part of the rubric and gives credibility with expert guests.
4. Borrow from the physics section: shorten the physics explanation to 40 s and use the saved 20 s to set up the build before starting the live demo. The total stays 180 s. Alternatively, start the build running as you begin speaking so no switch-on time is needed.
5. Structure: "We built this to learn how pumps work, not to replace a commercial one. Our problem was understanding how wind energy lifts water. The physics is wind power ∝ ½ρAv³ driving a crank pump. Our result shows 150 mL per minute at 4 m/s — enough to see the principle work, even if a real pump delivers more. Building it taught us more than buying it would."

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Design the optimal 3-minute demo script for a capstone windmill pump project. Write the script section by section with time allocations, and justify why each section is that length.
   **Worked Answer:** (0:00–0:30) Problem: "In rural Kenya, windmills pump water without fuel. We asked: can a student-built windmill lift water efficiently?" — 30 s because the problem must hook the audience but not bore them. (0:30–1:30) Physics and diagram: point to the poster diagram, state wind power ∝ ½ρAv³ and explain how the crank converts rotation to pumping. 60 s because this is where the audience learns the science and it needs the diagram for support. (1:30–2:30) Live demo: face the windmill into the wind, run for 60 s, measure water in the jar, announce the result. 60 s because the audience needs to see the build work, not just hear about it. (2:30–3:00) Result and conclusion: "150 mL in 60 s at 4 m/s — 11% better than our baseline after the blade improvement. Next: reduce bearing friction." 30 s because the result is the payoff and must be crisp. Total: 180 s.
2. **Q:** A fundi (craftsman) visits your station and says, "Your pump efficiency is only 3% — that is terrible." Using your data, write a 45-second response that defends your project honestly without exaggerating.
   **Worked Answer:** "You are right that 3% is low compared to a commercial pump. Our build cost 850 KSh, and we measured 0.1 W of useful pumping power from an estimated 3.3 W of wind input. The low efficiency is mostly bearing friction and blade design — we are students learning, not engineers optimising. The value is not in the efficiency number but in what we proved: the wind can lift water with locally available materials and no fuel. If we improved the bearings and blade pitch, we estimate efficiency could reach 8–10%. That is our next iteration." This answer is honest (admits the low number), grounded in data (cites measurements), and forward-looking (names the next step).
3. **Q:** List the two hardest questions a visitor could ask about your capstone project. For each, explain why it is hard and write a data-grounded answer.
   **Worked Answer:** Question 1: "What happens to your windmill in a storm?" — hard because it asks about a condition you did not test. Answer: "We did not test storm conditions for safety. Our data goes up to Beaufort 5. At higher winds, the rotor would overspeed and the pump rod could jam or break. A real windmill uses a tail furling mechanism to turn away from the wind — that is in our maintenance plan as a future improvement." Question 2: "How do you know your improvement was real and not just wind variation?" — hard because it challenges the validity of the data. Answer: "We interleaved A-B-A-B trials and normalised output by wind speed. Our B trials averaged 33.3 mL per m/s versus 30 for A, and the ranges did not overlap, so the improvement is consistent. We also checked the wind-power scaling: a 12.5% wind increase should give a 42% output rise by v³, but we only saw 25%, confirming the modification contributed, not just the wind." Both answers cite specific data from the log.

## Safety Notes
- If the capstone build has moving parts (windmill rotor, pump linkage), maintain the 2 m safety perimeter during the live rehearsal demo.
- Do not run the build indoors if it produces dust, spray, or loud noise that could disturb other classes — move outside or use the workshop area.
- Markers can stain clothing and hands. Keep caps on when not in use and avoid leaning on freshly drawn posters.
- If timing with a phone, keep it away from water-based builds. Use a stopwatch or clock as a backup.
- Poster paper on the floor is a slip hazard. Tape posters to walls or place on tables, not the floor.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners need their completed capstone documentation from Lesson 361 (diagram, data table, final numbers, maintenance plan). The poster and demo build directly on that material.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on a clear poster with all four sections and a 3-minute demo that runs the build live. Ages 14–17 add the data-grounded audience question answers, the efficiency defence, and the structured demo script with time allocations.
- Local resource tips: poster paper can be the back of a calendar, flattened carton, or joined exercise book pages. Markers can be shared between teams. If no large paper, two A4 sheets taped side by side work.
- The peer feedback round is the heart of this lesson — do not skip it. Teams that receive and act on specific feedback before Science Day will perform far better with real guests.
- Save the finished posters — they are the primary visual for the Community Science Day stations in Lesson 363.
- The two hardest audience questions (homework) should be checked by the teacher before Science Day so no team is caught off guard by a question they should have anticipated.
