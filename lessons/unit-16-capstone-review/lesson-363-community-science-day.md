# Lesson 363: Community Science Day

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-4.1  |  **Hands-On:** Yes  |  **Project:** science-day

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Set up and run a demonstration station that showcases a year's worth of physics builds for community guests.
2. Deliver a clear, timed capstone demonstration to parents, fundis, and local leaders, adapting the explanation to the audience.
3. Collect guest feedback using structured feedback cards with ratings and written comments.
4. Tally and summarise guest feedback quantitatively to produce a class report on the event.

## Materials
- All year's builds: windmill, pump/siphon, bridge models, instrument ensemble, capstone projects
- Posters from Lesson 362 (one per capstone team)
- Feedback cards (pre-printed, 4–5 per station: station name, 3 rating items scored 1–5, one comment line)
- Tables or benches for station display (one per station)
- Name tags for learners (paper and marker)
- Sign-in sheet for guests (name, role, signature)
- Bell or whistle for time signals (rotate guests every 5 minutes)
- Clipboards and pencils for collecting feedback cards
- Camera or phone for event photos (teacher-managed)

## Vocabulary
- **community science** (sayansi ya jamii) — sharing scientific work with the public so neighbours can see, question, and learn from it
- **station** (kituo) — a table or area where one team demonstrates one build or topic for visiting guests
- **feedback card** (kadi ya maoni) — a pre-printed card where a guest rates a station and writes a comment
- **tally** (hesabu ya alama) — counting responses in categories to turn written opinions into numbers
- **fundis** (mafundi) — local craftspeople and technicians whose practical knowledge helps learners connect physics to real work
- **quantitative summary** (muhtasari wa kibenki) — a report that uses numbers (counts, averages, ratings) to describe what guests thought

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. A parent arrives at your station and says, "I do not understand physics." What is the first thing you should do to help them engage with your build?
2. A guest rates your station 2 out of 5 and writes, "Could not hear the explanation." What do you do with this feedback, and why is it more useful than a rating of 5 with no comment?
3. After the event you have 20 feedback cards. What is the first step to summarising them quantitatively?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. Start with the live demo, not the explanation. Show the build working (e.g., the windmill pumping water) and say one simple sentence about what it does. Let them see the result before the physics — curiosity comes before equations.
2. You use it to improve for next time: the rating tells you there is a problem, and the comment tells you exactly what to fix (speak louder or face the audience). A rating of 5 with no comment feels nice but gives you nothing to act on — the critical feedback is more valuable.
3. Tally the ratings: count how many guests scored each item at each level (1, 2, 3, 4, 5), then compute the average rating per station and per item. This turns 20 cards into a few numbers you can compare.

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — collect slips; this lesson is run as a live event, so the pre-test is brief and the flow follows the event schedule.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — welcome the class and any early guests. Remind learners of the station rules: greet the guest, demo first, explain second, invite questions, hand out a feedback card before they leave. Post the event map on the board showing all station locations.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — stations run live. Teams demonstrate their builds to rotating guests. The bell signals every 5 minutes for guests to move to the next station. Capstone teams premiere their 3-minute demo (from Lesson 362) to gathered guests at the main area. Every guest receives a feedback card at each station they visit.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — gather all teams and any remaining guests. Collect feedback cards by station. On the board, demonstrate a quick tally: count the ratings for one station and compute the average. Confront the misconception that *only positive feedback matters* — show how a low rating with a specific comment is the most useful data for improving next year.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *science belongs to the community, not just the classroom*. Homework: tally all feedback cards for your station and write a one-paragraph summary of what guests thought and one improvement for next year.

## Hands-On Build: Science Day Stations
*Build time fits inside the Explore block.*

### Parts
- All year's builds brought to the event area
- Posters from Lesson 362
- Tables or benches (one per station)
- Feedback cards (4–5 per station)
- Clipboards and pencils
- Sign-in sheet for guests
- Bell or whistle for time signals
- Name tags

### Workflow
1. Set up stations before guests arrive: place each build on a table with its poster behind it. Arrange stations so guests can walk a clear path from one to the next. Post the event map. **Checkpoint:** every station has its build, poster, and a stack of feedback cards with a pencil.
2. When a guest arrives, greet them by name, show the build working first (live demo), then explain the physics using the poster, then invite questions. Hand them a feedback card before they move on. **Checkpoint:** every guest receives a feedback card at every station they visit.
3. Ring the bell every 5 minutes to rotate guests to the next station. Capstone teams run their full 3-minute demo (from Lesson 362) at the main area when a group gathers. **Checkpoint:** guests move on signal; no station is overcrowded.
4. Collect completed feedback cards into a clipboard folder by station name. Thank departing guests and ask them to sign the guest book. **Checkpoint:** all feedback cards are collected and sorted by station before the Explain phase.
5. After the event, tally the feedback cards for your station: count each rating level (1–5) for each item, compute the average, and read all comments. **Checkpoint:** the tally is complete with averages and at least three comments noted.
6. Write a one-paragraph summary: what the average ratings were, what guests liked, what they said to improve, and one change for next year. **Checkpoint:** the summary includes at least one number and one specific comment.

### Common Mistakes
- **Explaining before demonstrating** — guests lose interest in talk. Show the build working first; curiosity follows the demo, not the lecture.
- **Not handing out feedback cards** — if guests leave without a card, you have no data. Make it a habit: demo, explain, questions, then hand the card.
- **Speaking only to the teacher** — learners sometimes present to the teacher instead of the guest. Face the guest, make eye contact, and address them directly.
- **Ignoring negative feedback** — a rating of 2 with "could not see the demo" is gold. Do not dismiss it; it tells you exactly what to fix for next year.
- **No time signals** — without the bell, guests linger at one station and miss the rest. Ring the bell firmly every 5 minutes.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. A parent arrives at your station and says, "I do not understand physics." What is the first thing you should do to help them engage with your build?
2. A guest rates your station 2 out of 5 and writes, "Could not hear the explanation." What do you do with this feedback, and why is it more useful than a rating of 5 with no comment?
3. Your station received these ratings for "clarity of explanation": 4, 3, 5, 2, 4. Calculate the average (mean) rating. What does it tell you?
4. A fundi visits your windmill station and asks, "What size of pump rod would you use for a real 5 m well?" You did not test this. How should you respond honestly and usefully?
5. Challenge: your class collected 40 feedback cards across 5 stations. Describe step by step how you would turn these cards into a one-page quantitative summary for the school report.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. Start with the live demo, not the explanation. Show the build working and say one simple sentence about what it does. Let them see the result before the physics — curiosity comes before equations.
2. Use it to improve for next time: the rating signals a problem, and the comment tells you exactly what to fix (speak louder, face the audience). A rating of 5 with no comment feels nice but gives nothing to act on — the critical feedback is more valuable.
3. Mean = (4 + 3 + 5 + 2 + 4) ÷ 5 = 18 ÷ 5 = 3.6. This tells you the explanation was moderately clear but inconsistent — the rating of 2 shows at least one guest was confused, so there is room to improve.
4. Respond honestly: "We did not test a 5 m well — our tests were at 3 m. Based on our data, the pump rod would need to be longer and stiffer to avoid bending. A fundi like you would know the right rod thickness — what would you suggest?" This admits the limit of your data, offers a reasoned starting point, and invites the expert's knowledge, which is respectful and useful.
5. Step 1: Sort cards by station (5 piles). Step 2: For each station, tally each rating item (e.g., clarity, physics accuracy, demo quality) at each level 1–5. Step 3: Compute the mean rating per item per station. Step 4: Read all written comments and group them into themes (e.g., "hard to hear," "loved the live demo," "wanted more data"). Step 5: Count how many comments fall in each theme. Step 6: Write a one-page summary with a table of mean ratings per station, the top three comment themes with counts, and one recommended improvement per station.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Your station received the following ratings from 10 guests for three items (each scored 1–5): Clarity: 4, 3, 5, 2, 4, 5, 3, 4, 5, 3. Physics accuracy: 5, 4, 5, 3, 5, 4, 5, 4, 5, 4. Demo quality: 4, 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 4, 3, 5, 4. Calculate the mean and range for each item. Which item most needs improvement, and what evidence supports this?
   **Worked Answer:** Clarity: mean = (4+3+5+2+4+5+3+4+5+3)/10 = 38/10 = 3.8; range = 5−2 = 3. Physics accuracy: mean = (5+4+5+3+5+4+5+4+5+4)/10 = 44/10 = 4.4; range = 5−3 = 2. Demo quality: mean = (4+4+5+5+4+5+4+3+5+4)/10 = 43/10 = 4.3; range = 5−3 = 2. Clarity most needs improvement: it has the lowest mean (3.8) and the widest range (3), meaning some guests were confused while others were fine — the explanation is inconsistent. Physics accuracy and demo quality are both above 4.3 with narrow ranges, so they are consistently strong.
2. **Q:** A local leader says, "This is nice, but can any of these builds actually help our community?" Using the capstone data from Lessons 358–361, write a 60-second response that is honest about limitations but shows real value.
   **Worked Answer:** "Our windmill pump lifted 150 mL of water per minute at 4 m/s wind — that is not enough for a farm, but it proves the principle works with locally available materials costing 850 KSh. Our efficiency was about 3%, and we know commercial pumps do far better. The value is not in replacing a real pump today — it is in showing that students in this school can design, build, test, and improve a working machine. If we improve the bearings and blade design, we estimate 8–10% efficiency, and that knowledge stays in the community. A fundi who saw our work could take the design further. We are training problem-solvers, not selling a product." This answer is honest (admits the low output and efficiency), grounded in data (cites 150 mL, 850 KSh, 3%), and reframes the value as education and local capacity.
3. **Q:** Design a feedback card for Science Day that yields quantitative data you can summarise in a report. List every field on the card, explain why each is included, and describe how you would tally the results.
   **Worked Answer:** Fields: (1) Station name — identifies which build is being rated so data can be sorted by station. (2) Guest role (parent, fundis, leader, student, other) — lets you compare feedback by audience type. (3) Clarity of explanation, scored 1–5 — a single-number rating you can average. (4) Physics accuracy, scored 1–5 — checks whether the science was correct, a key learning outcome. (5) Demo quality, scored 1–5 — measures whether the live build worked and was visible. (6) One written comment — captures qualitative detail that numbers miss. Tally method: sort cards by station, then by guest role. For each station, count how many guests chose each level (1–5) for each item, and compute the mean. Group written comments by theme (positive, suggestion, question) and count each theme. Report the mean ratings in a table and the top three comment themes with counts, per station.

## Safety Notes
- Maintain a 2 m safety perimeter around any station with moving parts (windmill rotor, pump linkage) during live demos. Use a chalk line or rope on the ground to mark it.
- Keep water-based builds away from electrical outlets and phones used for photos. Dry hands before handling electronics.
- Crowded stations are a trip and crush hazard. Use the bell to rotate guests so no station has more than 6–8 people at once.
- Some builds have sharp edges (cut plastic, wire). Wrap exposed sharp ends with tape before the event and check after each rotation.
- If the event is outdoors, secure posters and light builds against wind — use stones, clips, or tape so nothing blows into a guest.
- Have a first aid kit visible at the teacher's station. Know who the designated first-aider is.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: learners need their finished posters and rehearsed demos from Lesson 362, plus their capstone builds and all year's builds brought to the event area. The feedback card concept and tallying method were introduced in the pre-test.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on running a clear station: greet, demo, explain, invite questions, hand out cards. Ages 14–17 add the quantitative feedback summary: tallying ratings, computing means, grouping comments by theme, and writing the class report.
- Local resource tips: feedback cards can be printed on half-sheets of exercise book paper. Tables can be borrowed desks or benches. The sign-in sheet can be a page from an exercise book. Invite guests early — at least one week before — through the school, local chief's office, and nearby fundis' workshops.
- This is the capstone community event of the year. The goal is not perfection — it is that learners stand in front of their community and present real work. Guests will remember a working demo and an honest answer far more than a polished speech.
- Collect all feedback cards and photos — they feed into Lesson 364 (reflection and portfolio) and provide evidence for the school's CBE outcome reporting.
- If guest attendance is lower than expected, run the event anyway with learners rotating as guests for each other. The feedback structure still works and the practice is still real.
