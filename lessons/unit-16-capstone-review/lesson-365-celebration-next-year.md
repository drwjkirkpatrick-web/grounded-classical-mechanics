# Lesson 365: Celebration and Next Year's Teaser

**Unit:** 16 — Capstone, Review & Community Science  |  **Duration:** 45 minutes  |  **Ages:** 10–17 (differentiated)
**CBE Alignment:** G10-PHY-1.6; G10-PHY-3.1  |  **Hands-On:** No  |  **Project:** —

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners can:
1. Receive a certificate of completion that records the physics skills they demonstrated through the year's portfolio.
2. Explain in one sentence how a dynamo turns mechanical energy into electrical energy, connecting it to the windmill and water-wheel builds of this year.
3. Describe one way solar energy could be used in their community, naming the energy transformation involved.
4. Recite the class physics pledge and state one physics idea they will carry into the holiday and next year.

## Materials
- Printed certificates, one per learner, with the learner's name and a signature line (head teacher or physics teacher signs)
- A small dynamo (bicycle dynamo or hand-crank generator) and a small bulb or LED connected to it
- A short wire with two crocodile clips to connect the dynamo to the bulb
- A solar panel demo (small panel, 1–5 W, with a motor or bulb) — if unavailable, a clear photograph of one
- The class's portfolios from Lesson 364, laid out on a side table for display
- A printed copy of the class physics pledge, large enough for all to read from their seats
- A camera or phone to photograph the certificate handout

## Vocabulary
- **dynamo** (jeneta) — a device that spins mechanical energy into electrical energy using magnets and coils
- **generator** (kizalishaji) — a larger dynamo; the same idea, scaled up to make mains electricity
- **electrical energy** (nguvu ya umeme) — the energy carried by moving charges in a wire
- **solar energy** (nguvu ya jua) — energy from sunlight, changed into heat or electricity by a panel
- **energy transformation** (ubadilishaji wa nishati) — changing energy from one form to another, e.g. motion to electrical
- **pledge** (ahadi) — a promise the class makes together to keep using physics
- **certificate** (cheti) — a signed paper that records the skills a learner has shown

## Pre-Test
*5 minutes, individual, closed book.*

1. In one sentence, what does a dynamo do?
2. Name one form of energy that turns into electrical energy in a generator.
3. What is one thing you are most proud of learning in physics this year?

**Answer Key (Pre-Test):**
1. A dynamo turns mechanical (motion) energy into electrical energy — it spins a magnet near a coil and the moving magnet pushes charges in the wire, making a current. Accept any answer naming the conversion from motion to electricity.
2. Mechanical energy (motion, kinetic energy of a spinning shaft) is the input. Accept "motion," "kinetic energy," or "spinning" as the form that becomes electrical energy.
3. Accept any honest, specific answer naming a skill or idea from the year — reading a graph, building a windmill, calculating efficiency, understanding waves, etc. The item must be specific, not "I am proud of physics."

## Lesson Flow (45 Minutes)

1. **Settle and Pre-Test (5 min)** — hand out slips; collect after 5 minutes. Tell learners this is the final lesson of the year — part celebration, part a look ahead to next year's Electricity and Magnetism.
2. **Engage (5 min)** — spin the dynamo by hand and light the bulb; ask the class: "Where did the bulb's energy come from, and where before that?" Take answers. The hook: every machine and every wave this year moved energy from one form to another — and next year the class will learn how to make that energy travel down a wire as electricity. The dynamo is the bridge.
3. **Explore (15 min)** — run two teasers. **Teaser A — Dynamo and the Windmill:** connect the dynamo to the bulb and spin it; then show the windmill build (or a photo) and ask how the windmill could drive the dynamo. Learners sketch the chain: wind → blades → shaft → dynamo → bulb, and name each energy form. **Teaser B — Solar:** show the solar panel running a small motor or bulb (or the photograph); ask what energy transformation is happening. Learners name it: light (radiant) energy → electrical energy → motion or light. Then the teacher reads the class physics pledge aloud once; the class reads it back together. **Checkpoint:** every learner has sketched one energy chain (wind to bulb, or sun to bulb) with at least three transformations labelled.
4. **Explain (8 min)** — consolidate on the board: the year's arc was forces → motion → energy → machines → fluids → waves, and the dynamo shows where it goes next — into electricity. Confront the misconception that "the dynamo makes energy." It does not — energy is never made or destroyed, only transformed. The dynamo transforms motion into electricity; the motion came from your hand (or the wind), and the bulb turns the electricity back into light and heat. The total energy is conserved at every step. Then hand out the certificates, calling each learner by name and naming one skill their portfolio proved. The class stands and recites the pledge together.
5. **Post-Test (10 min)** — administer; collect or peer-mark.
6. **Wrap and Homework (2 min)** — takeaway: *physics is the study of how energy moves and changes, and next year you learn to put it in a wire*. Homework: over the holiday, find one machine at home or in the market that transforms energy (a bicycle dynamo, a solar lamp, a water pump, a radio) and write down the input energy, the output energy, and one loss. Bring it on the first day of next year.

## Post-Test
*10 minutes, individual. Questions 1–2 mirror pre-test; 3–5 probe new ideas.*

1. In one sentence, what does a dynamo do? (mirrors pre-test)
2. Name one form of energy that turns into electrical energy in a generator. (mirrors pre-test)
3. Draw the energy chain from wind to a lit bulb, naming the energy form at each step.
4. A solar panel runs a small motor. Name the two energy transformations in order.
5. Challenge: a windmill drives a dynamo that lights a bulb, but the bulb is dim. Name two places energy is lost in the chain and say one fix for each.

**Answer Key (Post-Test):**
1. A dynamo turns mechanical (motion) energy into electrical energy by spinning a magnet near a coil, pushing charges in the wire to make a current. Accept any answer naming the conversion from motion to electricity.
2. Mechanical energy (motion, kinetic energy of a spinning shaft) is the input that becomes electrical energy. Accept "motion," "kinetic energy," or "spinning."
3. Wind (kinetic energy of moving air) → blades (rotational kinetic energy of the shaft) → dynamo (electrical energy) → bulb (light and heat energy). Accept any chain with at least four steps and the energy form named at each. The key transformations are wind-to-rotation and rotation-to-electricity.
4. Light (radiant) energy from the sun → electrical energy in the panel and wire → kinetic energy of the spinning motor (and some heat). The two transformations are light-to-electrical and electrical-to-motion. Accept "solar to electrical to kinetic" in words.
5. Two losses and fixes: (1) Wind spills past the blades — fix by improving the blade profile or angle so more wind is captured. (2) Friction in the shaft, gears, or dynamo bearings — fix by lubricating or aligning the shaft. Other valid losses: electrical resistance in the wire (use thicker or shorter wire); the bulb turning some energy to heat not light (use an LED). Award marks for two distinct losses with a realistic fix for each.

## Advanced Questions (Ages 14–17)

1. **Q:** Preview problem: the windmill from this year spins a dynamo that lights a bulb. Sketch the full energy chain from wind to lit bulb, label each transformation, and estimate the efficiency at each step if the overall efficiency is about 30%.
   **Worked Answer:** The chain: wind (kinetic energy of air) → blades capture ~40% of the wind's kinetic energy (the Betz limit is ~59%, real blades below that) → shaft and gears lose ~10% to friction, so ~30% reaches the dynamo as rotational kinetic energy → the dynamo converts ~80% of that to electrical energy (small dynamos are 70–90% efficient), so ~24% is electrical → the bulb converts ~20% of the electrical energy to visible light and ~80% to heat, so ~5% of the wind energy ends as light. Overall, light/bulb input ≈ 24%, but light as a fraction of wind ≈ 5%. If the stated overall efficiency to the bulb (electrical output) is about 30%, the steps are roughly: blades 40%, transmission 90% of that (net 36%), dynamo 85% of that (net ~31%), giving ~30% electrical delivered. The sketch should show each box with a percentage and an arrow to the next, with losses labelled (spillage, friction, resistance, heat). Accept any defensible set of step efficiencies that compound to about 30%.
2. **Q:** A 10 W solar panel in a Kenyan village runs a small DC motor for 4 hours of bright sun. Estimate the electrical energy delivered, and say what useful work that energy could do (e.g. pumping water). State your assumptions.
   **Worked Answer:** Assumptions: the panel delivers its rated 10 W in bright sun (a real panel averages 70–80% of rated in good conditions, but we take the rated figure for the estimate); it runs for 4 hours; the motor and wiring are about 70% efficient. Electrical energy delivered = power × time = 10 W × 4 h = 40 Wh = 40 × 3600 = 144 000 J. Useful work at the motor ≈ 70% of that ≈ 100 000 J. To pump water: lifting water against gravity takes E = mgh. If the pump lifts water 5 m, then m = E/(gh) = 100 000/(9.8 × 5) ≈ 2040 kg ≈ 2000 L of water (since 1 L ≈ 1 kg). So one bright day's sun could lift about 2000 L of water by 5 m — enough for a household or a small garden. If we account for the panel averaging 75% of rating, the figure drops to about 1500 L. The answer is an estimate; the method (power × time, then mgh) is what matters.
3. **Q:** Next year you will study electromagnetism. Using only what you learned this year (forces, motion, energy), predict two questions electromagnetism must answer, and say which this-year concept each one builds on.
   **Worked Answer:** Question 1 — "What force pushes the charges in the wire when the magnet spins?" This builds on forces (Unit 2): a force is a push or pull, and here an unseen force from the moving magnet acts on the charges, just as gravity acts on a mass. The new idea is that the force is magnetic and acts on moving charges, not on mass. Question 2 — "How much energy can the dynamo deliver, and where does it go?" This builds on energy and efficiency (Unit 5): energy is conserved and transformed, and efficiency = useful output / total input. The dynamo's input is mechanical, its useful output is electrical, and the losses (heat in the wire, friction) lower the efficiency — exactly the efficiency accounting done for the windmill. Full marks for two questions, each tied to a named this-year concept (forces; energy/efficiency), showing the learner can transfer this year's framework to the new topic.

## Safety Notes
- The dynamo spins by hand — keep fingers away from the rotating shaft and any gears; long hair and loose sleeves must be tied back before the demo.
- The bulb or LED can get warm after sustained cranking; do not touch the bulb glass immediately after running it.
- Crocodile clips have sharp ends — handle by the insulated sleeves, not the metal jaws; do not short the dynamo terminals (it can overheat).
- A solar panel in direct sun can heat up; do not leave it face-up unattended and do not look directly at a reflected glare from its surface.
- Certificates are paper — keep them dry; the handout is a short ceremony, not a rush, to avoid torn or crumpled certificates.

## Teacher Notes
- Prior knowledge: this lesson draws on the whole year (Units 1–15), especially energy transformations (Unit 5), machines (Unit 6), and wind/water power (Unit 14). The dynamo and solar teasers preview Electricity and Magnetism, next year's unit — no new content is taught to mastery here, only introduced.
- Differentiation: ages 10–13 focus on the dynamo demo (naming motion-to-electricity), the solar panel demo (naming light-to-electricity), the certificate handout, and reciting the pledge together. Ages 14–17 do the full efficiency chain with step estimates (advanced Q1), the solar energy-to-water calculation (advanced Q2), and the prediction of next year's questions from this year's concepts (advanced Q3).
- If no dynamo is available, a bicycle dynamo or a small hand-crank flashlight demonstrates the same idea; if no solar panel is available, use a clear photograph and the energy-chain sketch still works — the transformations are named, not measured.
- The certificate handout is the year's closing ritual. Call each learner by name, name one skill their portfolio proved, and have the class applaud. Keep it brief so all certificates are given within the Explain block.
- The class physics pledge should be short and memorable, e.g. "I will look for the physics in everyday things, ask how energy moves and changes, and use evidence — not guesses — to explain what I see." Read it once, then recite together. Post a copy in the classroom for next year.
- CBE assessment note: the dynamo and solar teasers map to G10-PHY-1.6 (energy transformations and applications) and G10-PHY-3.1 (introductory electricity and magnetism, previewed). The post-test measures whether learners can name the transformations and sketch a chain; the advanced questions probe quantitative efficiency and transfer to next year's concepts. The holiday homework (find one energy-transforming machine) keeps enquiry alive between years.
